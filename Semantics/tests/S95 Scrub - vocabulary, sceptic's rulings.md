## 0. What was checked

The owner's words, as in decision S23 and the relayed request (they match each other and the proposal's quotations word for word): "Get rid of all words that imply verificationism and see if the semantics still holds." "Anything that is accepted is always tentatively, and mean anywhere that "accept" or "accepted" is used." "Also, argument is short hand for: reasons why this and not that. Not reasons for this and not that. An argument is merely something that can be strung together into a coherent structure to decide why this and not another."

Each proposed replacement was put to three questions: does it carry a forbidden idea back in (verification, justification, belief, truth or authority in an absolute sense, reasons *for*); does the term keep its job in draft 5; and is one word used for one thing. The proposal's two book quotations were checked against the book text and are word for word (Deutsch, *The Beginning of Infinity*: "Proof is a physical process" in ch. 8; the definition of knowledge in ch. 6). Collisions were checked by search in draft 5: "counterpart", "trace", "import", "tolerance" and "parameter" do not occur there; "route" (l. 307, Part IX), "instance" (l. 429, 518, 528, 616), "appraisal" (l. 71), "aim" (l. 441) and "prediction" (l. 151, 177, 622–630) occur only in the senses the proposal gives them.

**KEEP** means the replacement carries none of the forbidden ideas and keeps the term's job. **CHANGE** gives the new words and why. **OWNER** is used once, where the choice is the owner's.

## 1. Two findings that cut across rows

**(a) "Tentatively accept" should not be a defined status.** The proposal defines it: an assessor tentatively accepts a result when the assessor holds a usable argument that rules out the result's denial. Three problems follow. First, acceptance becomes the output of holding an argument, which is the justificationist shape (accept when you hold a reason) in contrastive dress. The owner, in S21, puts acceptance with the person, who chooses "for whatever reason", and "Whatever happens is always the choice of the person"; what an argument does there is rule out: "sees no option but to choose the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument". Second, the proposal uses the one phrase for two things: the defined status (Part VI) and anyone's act (l. 67, "whether anyone tentatively accepts it"; and l. 71, where a thinker acts on an appraisal no argument has decided). Third, a positive status is exactly what the owner's sense of argument does without. So: the only status in Parts VI, VIII and IX is **ruled out for j** (j holds an argument usable by j that rules it out); "tentatively accept" stays only for a person's choice, which the semantics names and never defines by the arguments held. Part VI then reads: "A result's denial is ruled out for an assessor who holds a usable argument that rules it out (Part IX) … A candidate is not ruled out for an assessor when no argument usable by that assessor rules it out as failing a condition of (E)." Part VIII (l. 369): "Once the target's answering y at (a,b) is ruled out for an assessor, every such candidate alike is ruled out for that assessor …". Nothing in the structure is lost.

**(b) "Rule out" is used in three senses.** The proposal defines "rule out" as always relative to an argument someone holds and lasting only while it stays usable. But it also writes "(F1) rules out any component whose signature differs from its counterpart's" (a condition excluding cases: no assessor, and it does not lapse) and "Meeting (E) on the restricted contract does not rule out that the restriction drops changes …" and "a historical extension does not rule out its absence" (logical compatibility). Hard case 6 depends on keeping the assessor level apart from the world level; one verb for both merges them, and "(F1) rules out" would read as tentative, which (F1) is not. So: "rule out" only for an argument usable by someone; a condition's exclusion is said by the condition ("no component whose signature differs from its counterpart's meets (F1)"); compatibility is "leaves open".

So the word "knowledge" names nothing the definition needs, and in ordinary use it carries belief and justification, which S23 forbids. What the owner decides: (i) rename, and the theory loses only its link in wording to Deutsch's "creation of knowledge"; or (ii) keep "knowledge" in Deutsch's sense, which means adding a condition (the created explanation tends to keep itself in being in its environment) and saying in Part I that the word means that and no attitude. Keeping the word without the condition is the one choice that should not be made: the name would claim what the definition does not contain. The same question, smaller, covers "understanding" (Part X, defined by Deploy) and "surprise" (Part IV, defined as a violation of a selected transport outside its history): each is defined with no attitude in it, but each is a belief word in ordinary use.

## 4. The hard cases: does a replacement exist after all?

**1. The one predicate with no assessor.** No replacement removes the idea and keeps Part VI, and none is needed. [Claude's reading:] the owner has already drawn the line, in S21, between two levels: "But “matches the problem” is always tentative and may be wrong" (the assessor's level) and "The case for science is reality itself. It must match reality, but not by some fixed infallible metric" (the world's level). "Meets (E) on C" is the second: it is indexed (contract, grain, boundary, history, and the adopted physics Θ), and it is nobody's metric, since (E) names no assessor and no procedure that could certify it. What "truth in any absolute sense" would add, a meeting that someone could certify, or one with no index, the theory contains neither. So this is not left open for the owner unless the owner reads "match reality" otherwise. "In error" and "faithful" go the same way. ("Fidelity" and "faithful" come from the Latin for faith, but draft 5 uses them as in signal fidelity, a transport keeping its target's relations; no belief is in them.) The heading "Explanatory realism" (l. 67), missed by the register, can stay on the owner's own word "reality", or become "Faithfulness without assessors".

**2. Claims with two sides.** No replacement changes the cases, and none could: for a two-sided claim, ruling out ¬φ and deciding φ against ¬φ are the same act. With row 2 the stance shows in the grammar, since no positive status is left anywhere in Parts VI, VIII and IX: when the argument lapses, nothing is left over. **3. End points of the dependence order.** No replacement removes them. What S23 forbids is foundational truth or authority, and an end point of definitions passes on meaning and nothing else: every claim of the theory is made only given the imports, the indices and the declared inputs, which are declared and replaceable. So the proposal's words suffice. "Parameter" in place of "import" would make the word itself say that the semantics is relative to Θ and N; it is optional.

## 5. Does the semantics still hold? What these rulings add

On the rulings above, yes, and with less than the proposal: the only status an assessor has is that something is ruled out for them; acceptance is a person's choice that the semantics names and does not define; "rule out" has one sense; and the one predicate with no assessor is the owner's "match reality". The owner is asked one thing: whether "knowledge" (and, smaller, "understanding" and "surprise") may stay in Deutsch's sense, with the condition that sense needs.
