### 1.3 The owner's words and the theory's

The owner's words and the theory's overlap but do not coincide. Every reading below keeps them apart.

- **"problem"** (C1, C4, C5, C9): what the candidates attempt to solve. In the theory that is a **question** \(p\) (L135). File 92 calls the everyday sense a "challenge".
- **"the conflict"** (C4): the state in which both survive. In the theory that is a **problem for \(p\)**, "a conflict between ideas that what the assessor has established has not settled" (L317). The owner uses "problem" in this second sense in decision S20 ("If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem."), but not in this statement: C4 keeps "the conflict" and "the problem" apart.
- **"survive"** (C3) and **"isn't ruled out"** (C2): nearest to the theory's **fits**, "no result established for that assessor shows it failing a condition of (E)" (L315). The two match exactly only for failures that a test establishes. Whether a failure found by examining a candidate counts as established is file 93's open first choice; until it is settled, a candidate the chooser sees ruled out by argument can still fit. The theory's own "survives" is a word about selection (L195) and must not be used for the owner's.
- **"the person doing the choosing"**: the theory has no chooser, person or agent (none of the words occurs). The nearest figures are the **assessor**, for whom results are established and candidates fit (L315, L317), the **system** that holds, represents and decides to stop (L429), and the **thinker** who "may act on an appraisal without certifying it" (L71).
- **"match"** (C15–C17): an everyday word. File 92 uses "matching" for (F1) and (F2), but the owner has not signalled that sense, and C15's "match the problem" cannot mean matching the world. It is read here as fitting a question, or being an account of it, as a whole.
- **"metric"** (C17, C18): in C17, a measure of whether a theory matches reality. The theory's only "metric" is the distance \(d\) stated for approximate transport (L363).
- **"conflicts"** (C19): most likely file 93's other open points, which file 93 calls "choices"; possibly the conflicts inside those choices (the tilt against the myth, the two rescues), which is the theory's sense. The reply keeps the owner's word and explains both.

## 3. What the statement adds

**3.1 The theory's silence on choosing, stated.** The theory prescribes nothing about which rival anyone is to choose, keep or drop, or whether anyone is to pursue a problem, and choosing one of two rivals that both fit establishes nothing, so the problem stands (L315, L317). Neither is said outright. L317's last sentence, the only mention of choosing between rivals, presents choosing as a possible recognized difficulty, and read without its condition it could be taken to judge choices. The owner's "Notice I never once claimed what must happen", "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached" and "whatever happens to the candidates is up to the person doing the choosing" are the counterpart the text lacks. The theory is silent on, and does not imply, the owner's positive claim that what happens is the person's; so the proposed sentence (change 1) states only the silence and the consequence, and leaves the owner's claim about the person where the owner put it.

**3.2 A framing by kind of theory.** The owner opens with "In the case of non scientific theories" and gives science its own sentence ("The case for science is reality itself."). The theory has one definition of an account for every question, physical, mathematical (L49, L343), rule (L57, L347) and worth (L455), and never uses the word "scientific". The split is not a conflict and need not become a category. It could bear on the theory in two places: what can establish a failure (the first choice, section 6.1), and claims of worth, which take the normative relation as an input (L25, L455, L522). Whether it should bear is the owner's to say; the question in section 6.1 asks it.

**3.3 A bearing on the first choice** (section 6.1). Not a settlement.

**3.4 Beyond both the theory and the books.** "They're all valid choices" and "Choice is always important because …" go beyond both sources (reader D, as narrowed) and beyond the theory, which abstains on worth. They are not additions to be made to the theory (section 5).

**Not counted as additions.** Readers B's findings B2 (the wording "physically admitted" against non-physical targets) and B8 (whether the theory says its adopted physics may be wrong) are the reader's own notes on the text; the statement does not raise them (section 9).

## 4. Where it conflicts with the theory

**None, on the readings the owner's own words support.** Three readings would conflict. Each is set aside by the owner's own words; none is ascribed to the owner.

1. **"Resolution is up to the person and the person's choice." (C8) and "Whatever happens is always the choice of the person." (C11), read as outcomes.** If "resolution" meant that whether the problem is solved, or whether a candidate is an account, is the person's to choose, it would conflict with L317 (the problem "stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting"; being an account is "fixed by the candidate, the question and the world") and L67. Read as whether and how the person pursues or closes the inquiry, it agrees with L429 ("Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof."). The owner's own words favour the second: C6 speaks of "whatever happens to the candidates", what the person does with them; C15's "yes, if resolution is the goal" treats resolution as an aim that matching serves; and C16's "may be wrong" presupposes a fact of the matter. C17 is not used here: the owner confines it to "The case for science".
2. **"But “matches the problem” is always tentative and may be wrong." (C16), read as matching itself being unfixed.** That would conflict with L317 and L367. The natural readings, a judgement that can be wrong and a problem that may be ill posed, agree with the theory (section 2).
3. **L317's last sentence against "They're all valid choices" (C13).** Not a conflict. Choosing one of two rivals that both fit is a recognized difficulty only against obligations the system itself claims and protects: a recognized difficulty is "a conflict in which what the system holds meets a claimed obligation only by failing a protected one (Part XI), when the system represents it" (L429), and "The obligations are declared inputs" (L441). L317's "as when … keeping every rival that fits is among the protected obligations" is one example of such a rule, not the only case. The consequence is an attribution, no repair under those obligations, not a verdict on the choice: "(P) does not rank alternatives" (L441).

**One difference that is not a conflict.** C2 pictures choosing as seeing only one candidate not ruled out. L317's one example of choosing drops a rival "which fits as well". The theory does not define choosing, so the two pictures do not contradict each other; the owner's describes a chooser acting on an argument, the theory's a chooser acting while nothing established separates the two.

**A decision on the sceptics' split.** On reading 1, the accuracy sceptics (on B4 and B12) wanted the owner told that on the outcome reading C8 and C11 differ from the theory; the faithfulness sceptic (on D6) held that the outcome reading has no support in the owner's words and should not be put to the owner as a possible view. Both are met: the plain-words answer says what the theory holds (choosing does not solve a problem; only what is found out does) and that the owner's words fit the reading on which the two agree, without suggesting the owner holds the other.

## 6. File 93's three choices

The statement settles none of them. The owner answered file 93's choices together (decision S21) and said "I don't really understand the rest of the conflicts", which counsels against reading a ruling into the words. The readers who read settlements into it (B3 "SETTLES_CHOICE"; C1 and C3 "partly settled") were narrowed by all four sceptics who checked them; this file agrees with the sceptics.

### 6.1 First choice: does a failure found by examining a candidate count as established?

**What the statement says.** For non-scientific theories the chooser picks "the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument". Nothing asks that the argument come from looking at the world. "its best argument" has three readings: the chooser's own best argument (the owner uses "its" for a person in C10), the strongest argument bearing on the candidate, or the candidate's best case for itself. On all three, ruling out is by argument, as the chooser sees it; "best" stays the chooser's appraisal and is never a grade.

**What it tells against, and what it does not decide.**

- It tells against a rule under which a failure found by argument never counts, for the owner's non-scientific case. Both books hold that argument alone can rule an idea out (Deutsch pp.25, 101, 119–121; Marletto p.50), and none of the six S93 replies argued for "no" (tabulation §6.2).
- It gives no direction for a question about the world. The owner gives science its own sentence, "The case for science is reality itself.", and says nothing there about argument. File 93's example, the myth against the tilt on the Greeks' question, is such a question; on the Greeks' question nothing seen goes against the myth. So one reading of the science sentence would leave the myth to reality, which leans the other way for the example. Reader B's second reading (R2: "match" as file 92's technical "Matching", so that argument rules out an assumed answer in any theory) rests on a sense of "match" the owner has not signalled; C15 uses the same word for matching a problem, which cannot mean matching the world.
- It speaks of what a chooser "sees" in choosing, not of what the theory counts as established.

**How much turns on it.** Less than the choice's wording suggests. In the theory all establishing is already by argument: a receipt is "a derivation tree over leaves" (L397), and Standing is defined for an argument application (L387–L390). A test is "establishing it, the target's relations as well as its answer" (L317), and nothing confines it to experiment (L49, L397). What is open is whether a leaf may refer to an examination of the candidate itself. That matters for candidates whose only failure is in their own content, found by no test of the target: a failure of non-circular dependence (the myth's assumed answer), a component anchored to nothing, an internal clash that no test inside \(C\) exposes (ruling X10). For those, the answer decides whether they still fit, so whether they can pose a problem with a sound account and make it easy to vary.

**If the answer is yes.** Ruling X09's settling proposals apply; the owner chooses none of them by these words. Claude's inference, not the owner's view: the owner's fallibilism ("there is no such thing as an infallible creative agent") fits a settlement in which such a finding can lapse like any other result (X09's proposals 1, 2 and 6) better than one that exempts it from (K3) (proposal 3). It does not decide between a receipt and none (proposal 5). The (K3) gloss at L315 ("the test that yields it") and L369's "a usable receipt for the target's answer" would have to agree with the settlement.

**The question to the owner** (built from readers A, B and C, as the faithfulness sceptics corrected it: quoting the owner exactly, presupposing no settlement, and naming what the answer decides):

> You wrote that the person choosing "sees no option but to choose the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument". The myth about winter assumes its own answer. When someone sees that by an argument, and not by looking at the world, should the theory count the myth as no longer fitting, for that person, so that it can no longer make the tilt count as easy to vary? And is your answer the same for a question about the world, such as why winter comes, as for a non-scientific one?

### 6.2 Second choice: is "offered … in place of" what the owner means by "discovered"?

**Open; the statement is neutral.** Its picture, two candidates that "attempt to solve a problem", between which a person chooses, fits either reading of "offered … in place of" (L315). Choosing one of two is not the same as offering one in place of the other, so no lean is drawn from it (both sceptics on A16, and the faithfulness sceptic on C2).

**A point file 93 did not ask** (reader C, kept by both sceptics). "offered" is used at L69, L315, L317, L369 and L526 and defined nowhere. The owner's word in decision S20 was "discovered". A variation that the person choosing has thought of, and told no one, is on the present wording no one's rival. Whether that is what the owner means is worth asking.

**On the present wording.** The sentence after the rivals definition, "a candidate's rivals are among the candidates someone has offered", restricts rivals to offered candidates; it supports the broad reading but does not state it (accuracy sceptic on C2; ruling X09 read it as stating it). L526 repeats "the offer of one in place of the other". If the owner answers "broad", a small change at L315 with the matching words at L526 would state it outright (section 8.6).

### 6.3 Third choice: should "easy to vary" point at one side?

**Open; the statement does not speak to it.** The owner never uses "easy to vary" and says nothing about how the theory names a problem. ## 7. The Boundary leftover

**What it is.** L299 ends "For a declared family \(\mathcal V\) of organization edits," and L302 defines \(\operatorname{Boundary}_{E,p}\) (D): the pairs of versions in the family of which one is an account of \(p\) and the other is not. With \(P\) the versions that are still accounts of \(p\), a pair is in Boundary exactly when one version is in \(P\) and the other is not, and when the unchanged candidate is in the family and is an account, Boundary gives \(P\) back. So it is file 11's Pres for one question, written as pairs (reader C; checked by program over families of one to six members, and immediate from the definition).

**Why it is a remnant.** It states no hard to vary, counts nothing and grades nothing, as both S93 readers said. But its domain is a family of versions declared from outside the candidate, which is the object decision S20 set aside ("That assumes that the set even matters or that the creative agent can even list them."); its content is the "versions that still fit" that lesson S26 names; and its one use in the project's analyses was by the corrected lemma, as the place a correction was recorded. The supports of (S) are also versions of the candidate, but fixed by its own written \(\Gamma\) (L299); the difference is the domain, not the word "declared".

**What rests on it.** Nothing. \(\mathcal V\), \(E_v\) and "organization edits" occur only at L299 and L302; the tag occurs again only in the dependence order (L526); the change list's W34.1 says no case or other definition uses (D). Replacements someone actually puts forward are judged as candidates in their own right (L307, L369) and, when they conflict with the original, as rivals (L315).

**The owner's statement does not bear on it.** Decision S20 and lesson S26 do. The case for removal rests on three things: nothing uses it, it carries two loose ends, and it is the old Pres in pair form.
