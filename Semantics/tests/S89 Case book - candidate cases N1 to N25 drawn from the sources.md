## Cases

### N1 - The seasons and the sun god

**Status.** AGREED — round 1. Adapted from a book. Round 3 judged it without the book, and the verdict held.

**Situation.** Asked why there are seasons, Tomas explains that the Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space while the Earth travels round the sun, as a spinning body keeps its axis steady. For half the year the northern half of the Earth leans towards the sun: the sun's rays strike it more directly and the days are longer, so it warms. Half a year later it leans away, the rays strike it at a slant, and it cools. Each piece can be checked on its own: a lamp warms a card that faces it more than a card tilted away from it, and a spinning top keeps its axis pointing one way. The same tilt also tells how high the noon sun will stand at each time of year. Then Tomas adds one sentence: "And the sun god, who approves of this arrangement, keeps the tilt steady." He says nothing more about the sun god, and the rest of what he says is unchanged.

**Questions.** Does Tomas explain why there are seasons? Is the sentence about the sun god part of what explains them?

**Verdict.** Yes, Tomas explains the seasons. No, the sun-god sentence is not part of what explains them. Strike it out and the account works exactly as before. The steady tilt is already accounted for by the point about spinning bodies. Nothing about the seasons, and nothing anyone could check, depends on whether the god exists or approves. The sentence is an unsupported belief riding along with a good explanation, not a part of it.

**Confidence.** High (R1 A1 high; A2 high).

**Open notes.** A2, optional: the god is given the job the spinning-top point already does. For a purely idle add-on, use "And the sun god looks on and approves." Both readings give the same verdict.

### N2 - The myth amended

**Status.** AGREED — round 1. Adapted from a book. Round 3 judged it without the book, and the verdict held.

**Situation.** A storyteller tells the old Greek myth of the seasons. Hades, god of the underworld, carried off Persephone. Her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, won her release, but on terms that bind Persephone to go back to Hades for part of every year. While Persephone is away, Demeter grieves and makes the world cold and bare so that nothing grows: that is winter. When Persephone returns, Demeter rejoices and the world grows warm again. A sailor then tells the storyteller that in lands far to the south, summer comes at the very time the Greeks have winter, and winter when they have summer. The storyteller changes the myth: when Demeter grieves, she does not put out the warmth of the world but sends it south across the sea, and when Persephone comes back, Demeter calls the warmth home. The changed myth agrees with everything the sailor reported, north and south.

**Question.** Does the changed myth explain the seasons in both the north and the south?

**Verdict.** No. The changed myth agrees with the sailor's report only because it was altered to fit it. Nothing in the story of Demeter's grief says the warmth should go south rather than vanish or go anywhere else. Had the sailor reported something different, the storyteller could as easily have changed the story another way. What the myth now says about the south is the sailor's report retold in the myth's terms. It gives no reason why the seasons there are as they are.

**Confidence.** Medium-high (R1 A1 high; A2 medium-high). A2: some would call the changed myth "an explanation, just a false and patched one". That is a difference over the word "explain", and on neither reading does the myth say why the seasons are reversed in the south.

**Open notes.** Both round-1 authors would reword the question, but in opposite directions. A1 sets the myth's falsity aside, and A2's "real reason" brings it back in. A round should pick one and judge the case again.

### N3 - A myth about winter

**Status.** AGREED — round 1. Book-derived. Round 2 judged it without the book, and the verdict held.

**Situation.** In ancient Greece a farmer asks why winter comes every year. He is told the myth of Persephone. Hades, god of the underworld, carried her off; her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, won her release on terms that bind Persephone to go back to Hades for part of every year. While she is away, Demeter grieves and in her grief makes the world cold so that nothing grows. Consider the myth only as it stood for the Greeks. Every land they knew had its winter at the same time of year, and they knew of no other lands. The myth agrees with all of that: winter comes once a year, at the same season, in every land they knew. Nobody has ever seen Persephone leave or return, or seen Demeter grieve.

**Questions.** Does the myth explain why winter comes every year? Is it an explanation of winter?

**Verdict.** No, the myth does not explain why winter comes every year. Yes, it is an explanation of winter, but only in form: it is an attempt at one, and a false one.
- **Why it counts in form.** It says what brings winter about and why winter comes once a year: the bargain that sends Persephone back each year.
- **Why it does not explain.** The yearly return is simply written into the terms of the bargain. That restates the fact of a yearly winter; it gives no reason for it.
- **What supports it.** Even for the Greeks, nothing counted in its favour except winter itself, since nobody ever saw any of the events it describes.
- **Why fitting the facts is not enough.** Its details (which gods, what bargain, why grief brings cold) could be swapped for others that fit the Greeks' facts just as well. Fitting every fact the Greeks had does not help when the story was made to fit them.

**Confidence.** Medium (R1 A1 medium, A2 medium; R2 A1 medium, A2 medium). All four authors give the same alternatives:
- "For the Greeks it did explain winter; by what they knew, it was their explanation." This is a qualified yes to the first question.
- From a smaller group: "It is only a story, not an explanation at all." This is a no to the second question.

**Open notes.** All four authors say the two questions use "explain" in two senses without saying which: offering an account of the cause, or giving the real reason and making winter understood.

### N18 - Two footbridges on opening day

**Status.** AGREED — round 2. Rewritten; made for this set.

**Situation.** Two footbridges of similar length open on the same day in two towns. Rhea designed hers from a worked-out theory of how loads, stiffness and sway interact; by her calculations, the bridge would stay steady under any crowd she expected. She never tested it with a crowd before it opened. Dov designed his by copying, as closely as he could, a village footbridge that had stood for eighty years; he did not know why its design worked, only that it had never given trouble. The village bridge had never carried more than a few dozen people at once, but Dov did not know how many people it had ever carried. He expected his bridge to be as trouble-free as the original. On opening day a large crowd walks onto each bridge, and each bridge sways badly. The crowd on Rhea's bridge is no larger than the crowds she had calculated for.

**Questions.** On opening day, did each designer meet a problem, meaning something he or she must now understand and fix? Did the swaying go against what each designer expected? Did it contradict anything either of them had reason to expect?

**Verdict.** Yes, both met a problem. Yes, the swaying went against what both expected. It contradicted what Rhea had reason to expect, but nothing Dov had reason to expect.
- **A problem for both.** Each has a bridge that sways badly under a crowd and must be made safe, and neither can fix it reliably without finding out why it sways. Rhea must find what her theory, or her use of it, missed. Dov must find out, for the first time, how his borrowed design actually behaves.
- **Against both expectations.** Rhea expected her bridge to stay steady, and Dov expected his to be trouble-free.
- **Rhea's grounds were contradicted.** Her worked-out calculations covered a crowd of this size, so the swaying shows a flaw in her theory or her calculations.
- **Dov had no grounds.** The village bridge's record covered only a few dozen people at a time, so it gave him no reason to expect a copy to stay steady under a large crowd. He expected more than his evidence supported, without knowing that he did.

Round 1 was disputed on Dov's surprise because the sketch did not say what Dov expected or knew. The rewrite supplies both.

**Confidence.** The lowest rating differs by question:

| question | lowest | R2 A1 | R2 A2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| first: a problem? | medium-high | medium-high | high |
| second: against expectation? | high | high | high |
| third: reason to expect? | medium | medium | medium-high |

Alternatives:
- On the first question (A1): Dov faces only a practical fix, not something to understand, since he could stiffen the bridge by trial until it stops swaying.
- On the third (both authors): eighty trouble-free years were an ordinary, if weak, basis for Dov's expectation, so the swaying did contradict something he reasonably expected.

**Open notes.**
- Both authors: state plainly that the crowd on Dov's bridge was larger than any the village bridge had carried. At present only "a large crowd" implies it.
- A1: "had reason to expect" can mean grounds he knew of or what his evidence actually supported. The Dov half of the third question turns on that.
- A1: the definition of "problem" joins "understand" and "fix", so a respondent can say yes for Dov on the fix alone.
- A2: the definition is stipulative, which is fine if it is meant to be.

### N19 - The novelist's two demands

**Status.** AGREED — round 1. Adapted from a book. Round 3 judged it without the book, and the verdict held.

**Situation.** Odile is writing a novel and wants two things from every chapter: prose that is rich and lyrical, and a story that keeps readers turning the pages. Planning chapter five, she sees, before writing it and before anyone has read any of it, that the long description of a valley at dawn, which she wants for its language, would stall the story at its tensest point. She drafts the chapter two ways. In the first, she cuts the description to one plain sentence: the chapter moves fast, and the lyrical passage is gone. In the second, the heroine flees across the valley at dawn, and the valley is described through what she sees as she runs: the language stays rich, and the chapter moves fast.

**Questions.** Did Odile have a problem before anyone read the chapter? Does either draft solve it?

**Verdict.** Yes. She had a problem as soon as she saw that her two demands clashed in that chapter. A clash one can foresee is already a problem, and no reader needed to run into it. The first draft does not solve it: it keeps the pace by giving up the lyrical passage, trading one demand for the other. The second draft solves it: the valley keeps its rich language and the chapter moves fast, because the description has become part of the chase.

**Confidence.** High (R1 A1 high; A2 high). Both authors note a minor alternative: the first draft "solves" it by compromise, or solves the pacing problem. But for the problem as she set it, meeting both demands, dropping one abandons the problem rather than solving it.

**Open notes.** None. The verdict on the second draft rests on the stipulation that the draft really does both, and the situation says so plainly.

### N20 - Keeping count with string

**Status.** AGREED — round 1. Book-derived. Round 2 judged it without the book, and the verdict held.

**Situation.** Two groups of goatherds keep count of their flocks with string. Nobody can measure string exactly: every measurement is off by up to a tenth of an inch. In the first group, as each goat leaves the pen in the morning, a herder reels out a length of string equal to the length of that goat; in the evening, as each goat returns, its length is reeled back in, and when all the string is back, all the goats are home. In the second group, each goat stands for exactly one inch of string, and after every goat the herder trims or lengthens the string to the nearest whole inch. Both groups also add flocks together by joining strings, and split flocks by cutting a string in two.

**Questions.** Can each method keep count of a flock of any size, through any number of such joinings and splittings? What, if anything, limits it?

**Verdict.** Only the second method can.

**The first method fails as it grows.**
- Every measurement adds an error of up to a tenth of an inch: each goat out and back, each join, each cut. Nothing removes the error or lets the herders tell a slightly wrong length from a right one.
- With enough goats and operations, the accumulated error grows larger than a goat, and the string can no longer show whether a goat is missing.
- It also tracks the flock's total length rather than its number, and cutting a length string does not tell you which goats went where (R1 A1).
- What limits it is the accumulated error, measured against a goat's length.

**The second method holds at any size.**
- Each error is well under half an inch, and rounding to the nearest inch wipes it out. Errors never build up, and the string stays exactly as many inches long as there are goats.
- Only practical things limit it: enough string, space and time.
- It relies on three conditions: no single error ever reaching half an inch, however long the string; the herders rounding after every step; and the herders not blundering.

**Confidence.** High on the main contrast; medium on the second method's limits, which depend on details the case leaves open (R1 A1 high, A2 high; R2 A1 high on the main contrast, medium on the limits; R2 A2 high).

**Open notes.**
- Three of the four authors (R1 A2, R2 A1, R2 A2) ask the case to say that the tenth-of-an-inch bound holds however long the string. The verdict on the second method depends on it.
- R2 A1 asks the case to say that the second group rounds after joins and cuts too, not only after each goat. Several joins or cuts in a row could otherwise add up to half an inch.
- R2 A2: how a flock is split under the first method is not specified.
- R1 A1, optional: rather than "keep count", ask whether each method can reliably tell whether all the goats are home.

### N21 - The order of adjectives

**Status.** AGREED — round 2. Rewritten; book-derived; fixed without the book.

**Situation.** Ngozi is a native speaker of English. She says "a lovely little old green French knife" and would never say "a green French little old lovely knife". She puts adjectives in this order in sentences she has never heard before, and gets it right every time. Asked why, she cannot state any rule; the other order "just sounds wrong". Nobody ever taught her such a rule; she picked it up as a small child from hearing people talk.

**Questions.** Does Ngozi know the rule? Was her grasp of it handed to her, or did she form it herself?

**Verdict.** Yes, she knows it, tacitly. She formed her grasp of it herself.
- **She knows it.** She follows the rule without fail in phrases she has never heard, which she could not do from memory of phrases alone. She knows it the way a cyclist knows how to keep balance without being able to give the physics, not in the sense of being able to state it.
- **Her grasp of it was not handed to her.** She was given examples of other people's speech, never the rule, and the rule is in no single sentence she heard. Her mind worked out the pattern, without her noticing, well enough to apply it to new cases.
- **What came from elsewhere.** The examples came from other people, and the rule itself was already her community's convention, which she rebuilt rather than invented. An inborn readiness for language probably helped. But the grasp of the rule is her own construction.

Both round-1 authors leaned this way, and the reconciled proposal expected it.

**Confidence.** Medium (R2 A1 medium; A2 medium-high on the first question, medium on the second).
- On the first question (both authors): knowing a rule means being able to state it, so she has a skill that conforms to the rule without knowing it.
- On the second: she simply absorbed the rule from the speech around her, so in effect it was handed to her (both). Or it is largely inborn (A1).

**Open notes.**
- Both authors: "handed to her, or did she form it herself" is a two-way choice. It leaves out "largely inborn" (A1), and it blurs the examples, which were handed to her, with the rule, which she rebuilt (A2). A1 suggests "Where did her grasp of it come from?"
- Both authors: "know" can mean knowing how or being able to state. Say which, or ask both, as round-1 A2 also proposed.

### N22 - Denying the inner experience

**Status.** AGREED — round 2. Rewritten; book-derived; fixed without the book.

**Situation.** People commonly report that seeing blue involves an inner experience, over and above the light entering the eye and the word "blue". Two theorists deny that any such inner experiences exist. Wren says only: "There are no inner experiences. People are mistaken about this." Yuri says: "There are no inner experiences. When people look inwards, they are consulting memories of what they have just perceived, and the machinery that does this evolved to report inner experiences that never took place. People have false memories of experiences, not experiences."

**Question.** Does either theorist give any reason why people believe and say that they have inner experiences?

**Verdict.** Wren gives none; Yuri gives one.
- **Wren.** Wren only says that people are mistaken, with nothing about where the mistake comes from, and the same move could deny anything.
- **Yuri names a source for the belief.** When people look inwards they are reading memories of what they have just perceived, and the machinery that does this produces reports of experiences that never took place. So people sincerely believe and say they had them.
- **Whether his reason is good.** Whether it is good or true is another matter, and the question does not ask. It is incomplete: he never says why such machinery would have evolved, and a "false memory of an experience" arguably brings back what it denies. Still, he gives a reason.

This is the answer the reconciled proposal expected. Round 1 was disputed only on the old question, which asked whether either theorist had explained why people "seem" to have inner experiences.

**Confidence.** High on Wren, medium-high on Yuri (R2 A1 and A2 alike).
- Alternative on Yuri (both authors): his account is mostly a redescription ("people report experiences because their reporting machinery reports experiences") that moves the mystery elsewhere, so neither theorist really gives a reason.
- A1 adds that the account may undermine itself, since a false memory of an experience seems itself to be an experience of some kind.

**Open notes.** Both authors think the case may now be too easy.
- A2: the question asks only whether a reason is given, so Yuri's answer is close to a trivial yes. A one-line denial set against a detailed one gives the answer away.
- A1: give Wren a sentence of similar length that contains no reason.
- Whether Yuri's reason is a good one is the half that round 1 set aside. Asking it would bring the dispute back.

### N23 - The planets tonight and a thousand years ago

**Status.** AGREED — round 1. Book-derived. Round 2 judged it without the book, and the verdict held. See "Specialist knowledge".

**Situation.** Priya, an astronomer, knows exactly where each planet is tonight and how fast it is moving. The laws of motion work equally well run forwards or backwards in time, so she calculates where every planet was on this date a thousand years ago, and old records match her figures. She says: "The planets were where they were a thousand years ago because they are where they are tonight." Her colleague starts from the old records, calculates tonight's positions just as exactly, and says the reverse.

**Questions.** Does tonight's arrangement of the planets explain where they were a thousand years ago? Does their arrangement a thousand years ago explain where they are tonight?

**Verdict.** The arrangement a thousand years ago, together with the laws of motion, explains where the planets are tonight. Tonight's arrangement does not explain where they were a thousand years ago.
- **Why not tonight's.** Tonight's arrangement lets Priya work out where the planets were, and it is excellent evidence for it, but it did not bring it about: the present cannot have made the past what it was. The planets reached tonight's places by moving from their earlier ones; they were not where they were because of where they would later be.
- **Priya's mistake.** Her "because" is the "because" of "I know it rained, because the street is wet", not that of "the street is wet because it rained". She has confused a way of calculating, and a reason to believe, with a cause.
- **The forward explanation is thin, but runs the right way.** Most of the work is done by the laws, and it pushes the question back a thousand years, leaving the earlier arrangement itself unexplained (R2 A1, A2).

**Confidence.** Medium (R1 A1 medium, A2 medium; R2 A1 medium, A2 medium-high). All four authors give the same main alternative: "Since the laws run equally well both ways, neither arrangement explains the other any more than the reverse. Each only lets you calculate the other, and what explains both is the laws together with the history of the solar system." All note that this view is held by people who think hard about time-symmetric physics.

**Open notes.**
- R2 A1: say whether the laws of motion are included ("together with the laws of motion").
- R2 A2: "arrangement" can be read as positions only, but positions alone settle nothing. Say "positions and motions".
- R1 A2: "does X explain Y" may be read as "can Y be worked out from X". On that reading, both answers are trivially yes.

### N24 - Two sealed sorts of matter

**Status.** AGREED — round 1. Book-derived. Round 2 judged it without the book, and the verdict held.

**Situation.** Imagine a universe with two sorts of matter. Each sort can make atoms, machines and living things, but the two pass through each other without effect: nothing made of one sort can push, see, signal to or record anything made of the other. People made of the first sort have built a computer that, given enough time and memory, can carry out any calculation their sort of matter allows. People made of the second sort have built one that can carry out any calculation theirs allows. Some calculations can be carried out only in the first sort of matter, and some only in the second. Asha says: "Take the two computers together, and this universe has a machine that can carry out any calculation its laws allow." Bram says: "No machine in this universe can do that. A question written in one sort of matter can never be read by a computer made of the other."

**Questions.** Which of them is right about whether any machine in that universe can carry out every calculation its laws allow? Does Bram's reason explain why or why not?

**Verdict.** Bram is right.
- **Not one machine.** "The two computers together" are two machines that cannot exchange a single question or answer, and calling them one changes nothing.
- **A question reaches only one computer.** Whoever writes a question can write it only in their own sort of matter, so it can be given only to the computer made of that sort. That computer cannot do the calculations only the other sort allows.
- **They cannot be joined.** No machine can join the two, since nothing of one sort can touch the other.
- **Asha's weak sense.** Asha is right only in that every allowed calculation can be done by some machine somewhere in that universe. But nobody in it can hand an arbitrary question to the pair and get it done.
- **Bram's reason largely explains why.** Because nothing crosses between the two sorts of matter, the computers cannot be combined, and a question can never reach the computer able to answer it. But it explains the outcome only together with the stated fact that each sort allows calculations the other cannot do. If both allowed the same calculations, each computer alone would already do everything.

**Confidence.** Medium (R1 A1 medium, A2 medium-high; R2 A1 medium, A2 medium). All four authors give the same alternative: the dispute is partly about words. If "a machine" may be any collection of devices, and each question is posed to whichever computer suits it, Asha is right in that weak sense. R1 A1 adds that this needs a user who can write in both sorts of matter, which the story rules out.

**Open notes.**
- All four authors want "a machine that can carry out any calculation" defined, for example as one device to which any single user can set any allowed calculation.
- R1 A2 and R2 A1: "why or why not" is awkward, since Bram's reason is offered for "not". Their suggested "why no machine can" states the answer. A neutral form is "Does Bram's reason explain why his answer is right or wrong?"
- R2 A2: say explicitly that no calculation needs both sorts of matter at once.
- R1 A1: add one sentence on why some calculations are possible only in one sort.

### N25 - What holds the universe up

**Status.** AGREED — round 1. Book-derived. Round 2 judged it without the book, and the verdict held.

**Situation.** Three cosmologies each say what holds the universe up. The first: the universe rests, forever unmoving, on the head of a dog that stands outside it; the dog is eternal and will never change. The second is the same in every respect, except that it says a turtle. The third: a turtle holds the universe up, that turtle stands on another turtle, and so on down without end. In all three, nothing inside the universe can reach, see or affect whatever holds it up.

**Questions.** Does any of the three explain what holds the universe up? Do the dog story and the turtle story say different things?

**Verdict.** None of the three explains what holds the universe up.
- **The dog and the single turtle.** They are arbitrary picks that could be swapped for any other creature. Since nothing inside the universe can reach or see what holds it up, no observation could ever favour one over the other. Each also leaves open the same question about the holder: what holds it up?
- **The endless stack of turtles.** It answers that question for each turtle in turn but never for the whole stack, so the question is passed down forever and never answered.
- **All three.** They take for granted that the universe needs holding up at all, and none gives a reason to think so.

Do the dog and turtle stories say different things? In words, yes: a dog is not a turtle, and both stories cannot be true. But nothing anyone could see, do or predict depends on which is true. As explanations they make the same empty claim, that something outside holds the universe up, and the difference between them is idle.

**Confidence.** High on the first question; medium on the second (R1 A1 and A2 alike; R2 A1 and A2 alike). On the second, some would say flatly that the stories say different things, since one could be true and the other false. Others would say that two claims no possible observation could tell apart do not really say different things.

**Open notes.**
- R1 A1, R2 A1 and R2 A2: the first question assumes that the universe needs something to hold it up. R2 A1 suggests "what, if anything, holds the universe up".
- R1 A2 and R2 A2: "say different things" can mean literal content or any difference in consequences. R1 A2 would split the question into those two parts.
