### N1 — The seasons and the sun god
- Situation: Asked why there are seasons, Tomas explains that the Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space while the Earth travels round the sun, as a spinning body keeps its axis steady. For half the year the northern half of the Earth leans towards the sun: the sun's rays strike it more directly and the days are longer, so it warms. Half a year later it leans away, the rays strike it at a slant, and it cools. Each piece can be checked on its own: a lamp warms a card facing it more than a card tilted away, and a spinning top keeps its axis pointing one way. The same tilt also tells how high the noon sun will stand at each time of year. Then Tomas adds one sentence: "And the sun god, who approves of this arrangement, keeps the tilt steady." He says nothing more about the sun god, and the rest of what he says is unchanged. (Unchanged from the sketch.)
- Question: Does Tomas explain why there are seasons? Is the sentence about the sun god part of what explains them?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, Tomas explains the seasons. No, the sun-god sentence is not part of the explanation. Take the sentence out and the account works just as well. He has already said why the tilt stays steady (a spinning body keeps its axis), so the god does no work, and nothing anyone could check depends on whether the god exists or approves. The sentence is a belief of his attached to the explanation, not a part of it.
- CONFIDENCE: High.
- FIX NEEDED: None. "The rest of what he says is unchanged" gives away that the sentence is idle, but the case needs that fact stated.

### N2 — The myth amended
- Situation: A storyteller tells the old Greek myth of the seasons. Hades, god of the underworld, carried off Persephone. Her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, won her release, but on terms that bind Persephone to go back to Hades for part of every year. While Persephone is away, Demeter grieves and makes the world cold and bare so that nothing grows: that is winter. When Persephone returns, Demeter rejoices and the world grows warm again. A sailor then tells the storyteller that in lands far to the south, summer comes at the very time the Greeks have winter, and winter when they have summer. The storyteller changes the myth: when Demeter grieves, she does not put out the warmth of the world but sends it south across the sea, and when Persephone comes back, Demeter calls the warmth home. The changed myth agrees with everything the sailor reported, north and south. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does the changed myth explain the seasons in both the north and the south?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: No. It fits the sailor's report because it was changed to fit it. Nothing in the story of Demeter's grief says the warmth should go south, rather than vanish or go somewhere else. Had the sailor reported something different, the storyteller could just as easily have told a different version. The change describes the new fact in the myth's language. It gives no reason why the fact is so.
- CONFIDENCE: High.
- FIX NEEDED: Minor. Everyone knows there is no Demeter, so a reader can answer "no" just because the myth is false, and the case is not really asking that. Proposed question: "Leave aside that we know Demeter is not real. Judged as the storyteller's account, does the change make the myth explain the southern seasons, or does it only make the myth agree with them?"

### N3 — Persephone at home
- Situation: In ancient Greece a farmer asks why winter comes every year. He is told the myth of Persephone. Hades, god of the underworld, carried her off; her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, won her release on terms that bind Persephone to go back to Hades for part of every year. While she is away, Demeter grieves and in her grief makes the world cold so that nothing grows. Consider the myth only as it stood for the Greeks. Every land they knew had its winter at the same time of year, and they knew of no other lands. The myth agrees with all of that: winter comes once a year, at the same season, in every land they knew. Nobody has ever seen Persephone leave or return, or seen Demeter grieve. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does the myth explain why winter comes every year? Is it an explanation of winter?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: It is an explanation of winter in the sense of an attempt: it says what brings winter about. It does not actually explain why winter comes. It is false, and even as the Greeks had it, nothing supported it except winter itself. The yearly timing comes from a contract the story simply stipulates, and every detail (which gods, why grief brings cold) could be swapped for another and fit just as well.
- CONFIDENCE: Medium. The two questions lean on two senses of "explain": offering an account of the cause, and succeeding in giving the real reason. Main alternative verdict: "For the Greeks it did explain winter. It was their explanation, and it only stopped explaining when better knowledge came." Some would also say a myth is a story, not an explanation at all.
- FIX NEEDED: The two questions carry the split, but the split is not stated. Proposed wording: "(a) Does the myth say what brings winter about, so that it is an attempt at an explanation? (b) Judged by what the Greeks knew, did a careful Greek have good reason to think it gives the real reason winter comes?" The expected answers are yes to (a) and no to (b).

### N4 — The tilt and the midnight sun
- Situation: Over two thousand years ago, an astronomer on a Mediterranean island proposes that the Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space as the Earth goes round the sun. She proposes it to explain one thing: why the noon sun stands higher in the sky in summer than in winter. Put together with what was known about heat and about spinning bodies, it also explains the seasons she knows. She knows the Earth is a sphere. She and everyone she knows have seen only the lands around the Mediterranean, and nobody has heard of the far north or the far south. It follows from her idea, though nobody at the time works it out, that far enough north the summer sun never sets, that near the middle of the Earth the seasons are slight, and that far to the south the seasons are reversed. Centuries later, travellers find all three. (Changed: added "She knows the Earth is a sphere", because the midnight sun follows from the tilt only for a round Earth; the Greeks knew this, and the source says so. Changed "there are no seasons" to "the seasons are slight", because near the equator there is still some seasonal change, often wet and dry seasons.)
- Question: When she proposed it, did her explanation already explain the midnight sun of the far north, before anyone had seen it or thought of it? Or did it come to explain it only later, when someone worked out the connection or when travellers found it?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Her idea already accounted for the midnight sun when she proposed it. Nothing had to be added to it, and the midnight sun was a consequence waiting to be worked out. Working out the connection revealed that the idea covered it, and the travellers confirmed it. Neither changed what the idea says. What is true is that she did not explain the midnight sun, since she had never thought of it. The idea covered it; she did not.
- CONFIDENCE: Medium. Main alternative verdict: "Something is explained only once someone connects the explanation to it. Before that, the idea explained what it was used for, and the midnight sun was an unknown consequence of it, not something it explained."
- FIX NEEDED: The sentence "It follows from her idea, though nobody at the time works it out" states the key fact outright. That is fair, since the case needs it, but it pushes towards "already". The question also mixes "her explanation" with what she herself did. Proposed question: "When she proposed it, did her idea already account for the midnight sun, or did it come to account for it only once someone worked out the connection? And did she herself explain the midnight sun?" Keep the sphere addition.

### N5 — A farmer's rule carried south
- Situation: In a village on the northern shore of the Mediterranean, farmers follow a rule handed down for generations: "Sow the wheat after the first autumn rains, in October, and it ripens in early summer, in June." Nobody in the village knows why it works. It has held year after year, and fields sown at other times have done worse. A family from the village settles in a land far south of the equator. There the seasons fall in the opposite months: the cool rains begin in April and May, and the hottest months are December and January. The father means to farm by the village rule. (Unchanged.)
- Question: What, if anything, does the village rule tell him about when to sow and when his wheat will ripen in the new land?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: On its own, it tells him nothing he can rely on. At home "after the first autumn rains" and "in October" were one instruction. In the new land they point to different times: the rains come in April and May, while October there is spring. The rule does not say which part matters, and "ripens in June" would now mean midwinter. Only someone who understands why the rule worked (the wheat needs the cool wet months to grow and the turn to heat to ripen) could tell him to sow in April or May and expect a harvest around December. That understanding comes from outside the rule.
- CONFIDENCE: High.
- FIX NEEDED: None essential. Optionally say whether the new land's climate is otherwise like home (wet winters, dry summers). As written, a reader must assume it is, or conclude the rule says even less.

### N6 — Two astronomers and the unknown world
- Situation: Two astronomers on a Mediterranean island, over two thousand years ago, hold that the tilt of the Earth's axis causes the seasons: the axis leans and keeps pointing the same way in space, so each half of the Earth leans towards the sun for part of the year and away from it for another part. Neither has any evidence about places beyond Europe, North Africa and the nearer parts of Asia. The tilt idea, taken as it stands, implies strange things about places far beyond: days and nights six months long in the far north, and seasons reversed in the far south. Each astronomer states the idea with a limit. Iras says: "The tilt causes the seasons in the Mediterranean and the lands around it. I make no claim about anywhere else." Leon says: "The tilt causes the seasons in the Mediterranean and the lands around it; everywhere else on Earth, the seasons also come at the same times of year as they do here." Each statement agrees with everything either of them has seen or heard. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does Iras explain the seasons in the Mediterranean? Does Leon?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Iras does. She gives the real cause at home and only declines to say what she hasn't checked. That is caution, not a change to the explanation. Leon states the same cause at home, but his addition contradicts it. If the tilt works by making each half of the Earth lean towards or away from the sun, then the far south must have its seasons at the opposite time. By denying that, Leon either hasn't grasped his own explanation or thinks something unexplained overrides the tilt elsewhere. So his account as a whole is not a coherent explanation, even though its home half, taken alone, is right.
- CONFIDENCE: High for Iras. Medium for Leon. Main alternative verdict for Leon: "He explains the home seasons exactly as well as Iras. His claim about elsewhere is just an extra mistake, and it does not touch his account of home."
- FIX NEEDED: Yes, a difference from the source that matters. The sketch says "Leon's statement is the book's", but in the book (p.27) the modified theory does not say the tilt causes the home seasons. It says the seasons in the known world "happen at the times of year predicted by the axis-tilt theory", and elsewhere at those same times: a rule about timing that has dropped the cause. As written here, Leon asserts the cause and then contradicts what it implies, which is a different test (an explanation with a contradicting add-on). If the book's case is wanted, proposed wording for Leon: "In the Mediterranean and the lands around it, the seasons come at the times of year the tilt idea says; everywhere else on Earth, they come at those same times of year." On that wording the verdict for Leon is clearer: he no longer claims any cause, so he has a timing rule, not an explanation, even at home. Confidence would then be high.

### N7 — Two bakers
- Situation: Two bakers in the same town each explain why their bread dough rises. Yeast in the dough feeds on sugars in the flour and gives off gas; the gas is trapped in the stretchy dough, which swells. Maya adds: "This holds when the dough is kept between 18 and 35 degrees." She found those limits by experience and says nothing about why they are where they are. Bea gives the same explanation with the same limits, and adds why: below about 18 degrees the yeast works so slowly that the dough hardly rises in the usual time, and above about 35 degrees the yeast begins to be harmed, and nearer 50 it dies. Between those temperatures both doughs rise just as described. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does each baker explain why her dough rises?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, both do. The rising is explained by the yeast making gas that the dough traps, and both give that. Maya's limits are an honest statement of where she has seen it work. They don't weaken her explanation of the rising, though she leaves the limits themselves unexplained. Bea's account is fuller because she also explains the limits.
- CONFIDENCE: High.
- FIX NEEDED: None.

### N8 — The machine that runs forever
- Situation: At a fair, an inventor shows drawings of a wheel that would keep turning and driving a water pump forever, with no fuel, no wind, no flowing water and nobody pushing it. Visitors ask why no such machine exists. Pia answers: "Nobody has found the right design yet. Hundreds of designs have been tried, and every one has come to a stop. Perhaps someone will find one that works." Quentin answers: "A machine that keeps doing work with nothing going into it would be making energy out of nothing. The energy anything gives out must be matched by energy put in or used up somewhere. That holds for any design, including ones nobody has tried." (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does each answer explain why there is no machine that runs forever without fuel?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Only Quentin's does. Pia reports that every attempt so far has failed, which is evidence against such a machine but no reason for it. Her answer even leaves room for one to work, and "nobody has found it yet" could be said of anything not yet invented. Quentin gives a reason that applies to every possible design, including untried ones: the pump's work would have to come from somewhere, and nothing is supplying it.
- CONFIDENCE: High.
- FIX NEEDED: None. The pump matters: a wheel doing no work could in principle spin a very long time, so keep "driving a water pump" in any rewrite.

### N9 — Dark moths
- Situation: A species of moth rests by day on tree bark. In a district where smoke has blackened the bark, the moths change over many generations until most of them have dark wings. Pale moths stand out on the dark bark and birds eat more of them, and moths get their wing colour from their parents. In a clean district nearby, where the bark is pale, most moths of the same species are pale. No moth has any idea of the colour of the bark or of its own wings, and nobody planned the change. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Is there knowledge of the colour of the bark anywhere in this story? If so, where is it, and what holds it?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: In the everyday sense, no. Nothing and no one in the story knows the bark's colour: not the moths, and nobody planned anything. In the looser sense biologists often use, yes. The moths' inherited make-up (the wing-colour genes, as they are now spread through the dark-district population) has come to match the dark bark, and it matches because the bark is dark, not by accident. That match is carried down the generations in the moths' heredity, not in any moth's mind. Whether to call it "knowledge" is a choice about how widely to use the word.
- CONFIDENCE: Low. Thoughtful people split mainly over the word. One side says there is no knowledge here, only a match produced by birds eating the conspicuous moths. The other says there is knowledge of the bark, held in the population's genes. A third point: the birds see the bark when they hunt, which a reader may count as the birds knowing its colour. This case may be set aside.
- FIX NEEDED: Yes. The answer turns on which sense of "knowledge" the reader takes, and "If so, where is it" nudges towards "somewhere". The birds' eyesight is also an unintended candidate. Proposed rewrite of the question: "Leaving aside the birds' eyesight and any person who studies the moths: has anything in the moths come to reflect the colour of their district's bark? If so, what carries it from one generation to the next, and would you call it knowledge?" Or state which sense of "knowledge" is meant.

### N10 — The burned notebook
- Situation: Halima, an engineer, spends a month working out why a proposed design for a long bridge would fail in strong wind: gusts of a certain speed would make the deck twist a little further with each swing until it broke. Her reasoning is sound. She writes the whole explanation, with her calculations, in a notebook, and tells no one. Before anyone reads it, a fire destroys the notebook, and she dies soon afterwards. Nobody else has worked it out. (Changed: added "Her reasoning is sound." The sketch implies it but does not say it, and the verdict depends on it.)
- Question: Did Halima create knowledge? Does that knowledge exist now?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, she created knowledge: she worked out, correctly and for the first time, why that design would fail. No, it no longer exists. Its only copies, the notebook and her own mind, are gone, and nobody else holds it. The fact it described is still true, and someone could work it out again, but that would be new work, not her knowledge surviving.
- CONFIDENCE: High. A minority would say "the knowledge still exists in the sense that the truth it captured is still there to be found". Most would call that the fact, not the knowledge.
- FIX NEEDED: Only the added sentence that her reasoning is sound.

### N11 — Two students, ten drafts each
- Situation: Two students each write ten drafts of an essay on the same topic. Jana hands each draft to her teacher, who returns it with a mark and no comment. She writes each new draft without knowing what the teacher liked or disliked in the earlier ones, and in the end hands in whichever draft received the highest mark. Kasia shows her drafts to nobody. After each one, she writes down what she thinks is wrong with it (a claim with no support, a paragraph that repeats another, an ending that does not follow) and writes the next draft to fix those faults. She hands in the draft that best survives her own criticism. The two final essays are good, and about equally good. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Did each student create what is good in her final essay?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, both did, but differently. Every word of each final essay was written by the student, and the teacher added no content, so what is good in Jana's essay is her own writing. Kasia also improved her essay by her own judgement. For Jana, the only thing that made the final essay better than a typical draft was the teacher's choice among drafts, not anything she learned or fixed. So Jana made the good parts, while the teacher's marks decided which of her attempts was handed in.
- CONFIDENCE: Medium. Main alternative verdict: "Kasia did; Jana only partly. The teacher's judgement, not Jana's, did the improving, so part of the essay's quality is the teacher's doing."
- FIX NEEDED: Yes, a matter of plausibility. Jana sees a mark on each draft before writing the next, and a real student would compare high- and low-marked drafts and guess what the teacher liked. "Without knowing what the teacher liked" then becomes hard to believe, and readers may assume she learned from the marks. Proposed rewrite: "Jana writes all ten drafts without any feedback. Only then does the teacher mark all ten, with no comments, and Jana hands in the one with the highest mark."

### N12 — Consent by rephrasing
- Situation: Before a knee operation, a surgeon reads two patients the same list of risks: infection, a blood clot in the leg, stiffness, and numbness around the scar. Asked to repeat the list, each does so word for word. The surgeon then asks each of them the same thing in other words: "Suppose your calf is swollen and sore a week after the operation. What might that be?" Then a question about a slightly different operation: "If we were operating on your elbow instead of your knee, which of these would you still need to worry about?" Mr Okafor answers each question by reciting the list again, in the same words. Ms Varga says the swollen calf could be the clot and that she would call at once; and that with the elbow, infection, stiffness and numbness would still apply, and a clot in the leg less so. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does each patient understand the risks of the knee operation?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Ms Varga does, and Mr Okafor, on this evidence, does not. Varga can apply the risks to a symptom she was never told about and to a changed operation, which is what understanding them looks like. Okafor can only say the words back. When asked to use them, he recites again, which shows he has memorised the list, not that he knows what it means for him. (He might understand more than he shows, for example if he is anxious or struggling with the language. The surgeon should find out before taking his consent.)
- CONFIDENCE: High.
- FIX NEEDED: None.

### N13 — Popper's parrot
- Situation: A parrot lives in the hall where the philosopher Karl Popper gives a course of lectures. It learns several of his favourite sentences by heart and repeats them, word for word, to visitors. A student who missed the lectures later learns some of Popper's ideas by listening to the parrot and working out what the sentences mean. When a visitor asks the parrot a question about what it has said, it answers with another of its sentences, or with a squawk. Had the lectures been recipes, it would have repeated those just as readily. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Has the parrot acquired Popper's ideas? What part did the parrot play in the student's learning them?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: No. The parrot has acquired Popper's sentences, not his ideas. It cannot use them, answer questions about them or tell them from recipes. Its part was that of a carrier, like a recording or a copied page: it brought the words to the student. Working out what they meant was all the student's own doing.
- CONFIDENCE: High.
- FIX NEEDED: None.

### N14 — The robot that learned to walk
- Situation: Dr Ferreira spends months designing a walking robot: its legs, joints and motors, its sensors, and a programming language for its control program. The language has commands such as "veer left by so many degrees", "lengthen stride" and "step over". She wrote each of them by hand from what she knows of balance, levers and the geometry of legs, keeping in mind throughout the sorts of walking the robot would need to do. She then writes only one simple program in the language: "walk forward; stop at an obstacle". From there a computer takes over. It makes thousands of slightly random variants of the program, tries each on a simulated robot over courses she chose (flat ground, slopes, small obstacles), keeps the one that walks best, makes variants of that, and repeats. After many rounds the program walks well on those courses. Put on rough, rocky ground unlike any of the test courses, the robot also walks well. (Unchanged.)
- Question: The robot walks well on ground it was never tested on. Whose knowledge makes it do so?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Mostly Dr Ferreira's. The ability to handle ground it was never tried on most plausibly comes from what she built in: the body, and commands like "step over" and "lengthen stride" written from general knowledge of balance and legs, with walking of all sorts in mind. The computer's trial-and-error search contributed something: it found a combination of her commands that works well on the courses she chose. But nothing in the story lets us say how much of the rough-ground walking that search added, and the likeliest answer is not much.
- CONFIDENCE: Medium. Main alternative verdict: "Both. The evolved program contains a combination nobody wrote, and that combination is knowledge the search produced, even if it is built from her commands."
- FIX NEEDED: Yes, two problems. (1) "Whose knowledge" asks for a person and so leans towards "hers". The search is not a "who". (2) The story does not say how well her starting program already walked, on the courses or on rough ground, so there is no way to measure what the search added. Proposed rewrite: add "Her starting program fell over on the slopes and at most obstacles" (or "already walked tolerably on all of them"), and ask: "Where does the knowledge that lets it walk on the rough ground come from: from Dr Ferreira's design, from the computer's search, or both, and in what share?"

### N15 — The miscopied notes
- Situation: Salma misses a class and borrows a friend's copy of the teacher's explanation of why a hot-air balloon rises: heating the air inside the balloon makes it take up more room, so the air in the balloon is lighter than the same volume of the cooler air outside, and the cooler air pushes the balloon up. The copy contains one slip: it says heated air takes up less room. Salma notices that with "less" the rest does not hang together, since the balloon's air would then be heavier than the air outside and the balloon would sink, and she changes it to "more". She then explains the idea to the teacher in her own words, and he agrees it is exactly what he taught. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does Salma now have the teacher's explanation? In coming to have it, did she create anything herself?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, she has it, and the teacher confirms it. Yes, she made something herself. The correction was her own reasoning: the copy she was given was wrong, and she worked out the right version from how the parts must fit together. Restating it in her own words also shows she rebuilt it rather than memorising it. What she arrived at was not new to the world, since it was what the teacher already knew, but her copy of it was made by her, not handed to her.
- CONFIDENCE: High that she has the explanation. Medium on "create". Main alternative: "She created nothing. She restored what the teacher had already said. Spotting a slip is checking, not creating."
- FIX NEEDED: None essential. If the second question is meant to separate "new to the world" from "produced by her rather than received", say which. For example: "Did any part of what she now holds come from her own reasoning rather than from the notes?"

### N16 — The domino that never falls
- Situation: A huge network of dominoes is built as a calculating machine. Each domino is spring-loaded: when knocked over, it knocks over its neighbour and then springs back up, so waves of falling dominoes travel along the network's branching and looping lines. This network is set up to test whether a number is prime. The number is fed in as a row of exactly that many dominoes, and one particular domino somewhere in the network falls only if the number turns out to have a divisor. The network is fed 641 and started, and it runs until it stops. An observer who does not know what the network is for watches the whole run and asks: "Why did that domino there never fall?" Answer A traces the run domino by domino. That domino never fell because no domino next to it ever fell at a moment when it would have passed the wave on. Each of those did or didn't fall because of what reached it from its own neighbours, and so on, back through billions of steps to the first domino that was knocked over. None of the waves set off from there ever reached it. Answer B: "Because 641 is prime. The network tests whether its input is prime, and that domino falls only when a divisor is found." Both answers are true. (Changed: the sketch's Answer A says the neighbours "never fall because none of theirs does; and so on, back ... to the first domino". Taken literally that is false, since the first domino and many others do fall, and it clashes with "both answers are true". A is reworded as a full step-by-step trace. Also added "runs until it stops" and "watches the whole run", since "never falls" needs the run to be over.)
- Question: Does each answer explain why that domino never falls?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: B explains it. A is true but tells the observer almost nothing. B says why no wave ever reached that domino: the machine checks for divisors, and 641 has none. It also tells him what would change the result (feed in 640 and it falls). A amounts to "no wave reached it, and here is every step of that", and the observer had already seen that nothing reached it. A is a correct record of how it happened, and it could not reveal why unless you already knew what the machine does.
- CONFIDENCE: Medium. Main alternative verdict: "Both explain, at different levels. A is the complete physical cause and B the reason behind it. A is merely too long to be useful."
- FIX NEEDED: Yes. Answer A as sketched is literally false, as noted above, and the case also stipulates "both are true". Use the reworded A, or the source's short form: "Because none of its neighbours ever fell at a moment that would have knocked it over, and so on back; in sum, none of the patterns of motion started by knocking over the first domino ever included it." Also make clear the run has finished.

### N17 — The transistor at the end of the run
- Situation: A computer runs a program that finds the factors of 15. At the end of the run, one particular transistor is on. Someone asks: "Why is this transistor on at the end of the run?" Answer A: the electrons in the computer started out in such-and-such a state, and the laws that govern their motion carried them to a state in which this transistor is on. Answer B: the computer was finding the factors of 15, and this transistor is part of how it holds the answer, 3 and 5. Both answers are true. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Are A and B two answers to one question, or answers to two different questions? Does each explain why the transistor is on?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: The words are one question, but A and B answer two different things someone might want to know. A says how the physics brought it about in this machine. B says why this is the outcome at all: the machine was factoring 15, and this transistor is part of how it shows the answer. Both explain, at different levels. B is what most askers want: it tells you the transistor would be on in any machine running this program, and that asking about 14 would change it. A, as stated, is only the outline of an explanation. It names no actual state, and the full version would be too vast for anyone to follow.
- CONFIDENCE: Medium. Alternative verdicts: "Only B really explains. A only says 'the physics did it'." Or the reverse: "Only A gives the cause. B is an interpretation of the state, not what produced it."
- FIX NEEDED: Minor. "Such-and-such a state" makes A an empty placeholder, which pushes readers to dismiss it. If A is meant as a real rival, say so: "the full calculation of the electrons' motion from their starting state, which exists but would fill libraries."

### N18 — Two footbridges on opening day
- Situation: Two footbridges of similar length open on the same day in two towns. Rhea designed hers from a worked-out theory of how loads, stiffness and sway interact; by her calculations, the bridge would stay steady under any crowd she expected. She never tested it with a crowd before it opened. Dov designed his by copying, as closely as he could, a village footbridge that had stood for eighty years; he did not know why its design worked, only that it had never given trouble. The village bridge had never carried more than a few dozen people at once. On opening day a large crowd walks onto each bridge, and each bridge sways badly. (Unchanged.)
- Question: On opening day, did each designer meet a problem? Was each of them surprised?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Both met a problem, since each has a bridge that sways badly under a crowd. Rhea should certainly be surprised: her own calculations said this could not happen, so the swaying shows her theory is wrong somewhere, and it gives her a definite puzzle to work on. Whether Dov was surprised the story doesn't say. He presumably expected no trouble, but that expectation had no basis for a large crowd, since the bridge he copied never carried one, and he has no theory for the swaying to contradict. If he was surprised, he had less reason to be. He is also left with less to go on in finding out why.
- CONFIDENCE: Medium on "each met a problem" (yes). Low on "surprised", because the story does not say what Dov expected, or whether he knew the village bridge never carried crowds. Main alternative: "Both were equally surprised: each expected a steady bridge." As written, the second question may have to be set aside.
- FIX NEEDED: Yes. "Surprised" is a state of mind the story doesn't describe, and the answer for Dov depends on facts not given. Proposed rewrite: say what each expected and what Dov knew. For example: "Dov did not know how many people the village bridge had ever carried; he expected his to be as trouble-free as the original." Then ask: "Did the swaying go against what each designer expected? Did it go against anything either had a reason to expect?" Also make clear that "a problem" means something each must now understand and fix, not just the swaying itself.

### N19 — The novelist's two demands
- Situation: Odile is writing a novel and wants two things from every chapter: prose that is rich and lyrical, and a story that keeps readers turning the pages. Planning chapter five, she sees, before writing it and before anyone has read any of it, that the long description of a valley at dawn, which she wants for its language, would stall the story at its tensest point. She drafts the chapter two ways. In the first, she cuts the description to one plain sentence: the chapter moves fast, and the lyrical passage is gone. In the second, the heroine flees across the valley at dawn, and the valley is described through what she sees as she runs: the language stays rich, and the chapter moves fast. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Did Odile have a problem before anyone read the chapter? Does either draft solve it?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, she had a problem as soon as she saw that her two aims clashed at that point. No reader needed to find it. The first draft does not solve it: it gives up one of the two things she wants, which is a trade-off. The second draft solves it, since it keeps the rich language and the pace together.
- CONFIDENCE: High. A few might say the first draft "solves the pacing problem". But she set herself both demands, so dropping one abandons the problem rather than solving it.
- FIX NEEDED: None. The second draft's success is stipulated ("stays rich ... moves fast"), which is fine for the case.

### N20 — Keeping count with string
- Situation: Two groups of goatherds keep count of their flocks with string. Nobody can measure string exactly: every measurement is off by up to a tenth of an inch. In the first group, as each goat leaves the pen in the morning, a herder reels out a length of string equal to the length of that goat; in the evening, as each goat returns, its length is reeled back in, and when all the string is back, all the goats are home. In the second group, each goat stands for exactly one inch of string, and after every goat the herder trims or lengthens the string to the nearest whole inch. Both groups also add flocks together by joining strings, and split flocks by cutting a string in two. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Can each method keep count of a flock of any size, through any number of such joinings and splittings? What, if anything, limits it?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Only the second can. In the first, every measurement adds a small error and nothing ever removes it, so errors pile up with each goat, join and cut. With enough goats or operations, the leftover or missing string is bigger than a small goat, and the string can no longer show whether a goat is missing. Cutting a length string also doesn't tell you which goats went where. In the second, each error is under a tenth of an inch and is wiped out by rounding to the nearest inch after every step, so errors never build up. It is limited only by practical things: enough string, time, and handling very long strings.
- CONFIDENCE: High.
- FIX NEEDED: None essential. Strictly, the first method records total length rather than a count. That is the point of the case, but "keep count" in the question lets readers dismiss the first method on that ground alone. Optionally ask: "Can each method reliably tell whether all the goats are home, for a flock of any size, through any number of joinings and splittings?"

### N21 — A rule she cannot state
- Situation: Ngozi is a native speaker of English. She says "a lovely little old green French knife" and would never say "a green French little old lovely knife". She puts adjectives in this order in sentences she has never heard before, and gets it right every time. Asked why, she cannot state any rule; the other order "just sounds wrong". Nobody ever taught her such a rule; she picked it up as a small child from hearing people talk. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does Ngozi know the rule? Did learning it involve creating anything?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, she knows it, without being able to say it. She applies it reliably to phrases she has never heard, which is more than remembering examples. On the second question: since nobody stated the rule to her, she must have worked it out for herself, without noticing, from the examples she heard. So yes, in the sense that her mind built something that wasn't handed to her ready-made. It was not new to the world, since the rule was already in the language around her.
- CONFIDENCE: High on the first question. Low on the second. Main alternative verdict: "Nothing was created. Children pick up their language's patterns by an inborn capacity, and 'creating' is the wrong word for absorbing a pattern that is already there." Some would also deny that following a pattern without being able to state it counts as knowing a rule. The second half may be set aside.
- FIX NEEDED: The second question turns on the word "creating": making something new to the world, or building something oneself rather than receiving it. Proposed rewrite: "Since nobody ever stated the rule to her, where did her grasp of it come from: was it handed to her, or did she have to work it out herself from what she heard?"

### N22 — Denying the inner experience
- Situation: People commonly report that there is something it is like to see blue: an inner experience over and above the light entering the eye and the word "blue". Two theorists deny that any such inner experiences exist. Wren says only: "There are no inner experiences. People are mistaken about this." Yuri says: "There are no inner experiences. When people look inwards, they are consulting memories of what they have just perceived, and the machinery that does this evolved to report inner experiences that never took place. People have false memories of experiences, not experiences." (Unchanged.)
- Question: Has either theorist explained why people seem to have inner experiences?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Wren has not. Saying people are mistaken gives no reason why they make this mistake, and the same move could deny anything. Yuri has at least offered an explanation of why people believe and report that they have inner experiences: false memories produced by machinery that evolved to report them. It is thin, though. He does not say why such machinery would have evolved, or how a false memory of seeing blue differs from seeing blue. Many would say that "seeming to have an experience" is itself an experience, so his account leaves the very thing in question unexplained.
- CONFIDENCE: High for Wren. Low for Yuri. Thoughtful people disagree sharply over whether his account explains the seeming or only the reports. Main alternative for Yuri: "Yes, he explains it. The seeming just is the false memory, and he says where that comes from." The Yuri half may be set aside.
- FIX NEEDED: "Seem" is doing hidden work: if seeming is an experience, Yuri's view contradicts itself; if it is a belief, he has an answer. Proposed split: "(a) Does either theorist give any reason why people would believe and say they have inner experiences? (b) Is that reason a satisfying account of why seeing blue seems to be like something?" The expected answer to (a) is Yuri yes and Wren no; (b) is contested.

### N23 — The planets run backwards
- Situation: Priya, an astronomer, knows exactly where each planet is tonight and how fast it is moving. The laws of motion work equally well run forwards or backwards in time, so she calculates where every planet was on this date a thousand years ago, and old records match her figures. She says: "The planets were where they were a thousand years ago because they are where they are tonight." Her colleague starts from the old records, calculates tonight's positions just as exactly, and says the reverse. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does tonight's arrangement of the planets explain where they were a thousand years ago? Does their arrangement a thousand years ago explain where they are tonight?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: The earlier arrangement, together with the laws of motion, explains where the planets are tonight. Tonight's arrangement does not explain where they were a thousand years ago; it only lets Priya work that out. The planets reached tonight's places by moving from their earlier ones, but they were not where they were because of where they would later be. Priya has confused a way of calculating, and a reason to believe, with a cause.
- CONFIDENCE: Medium. Many physicists would push back. Since the laws run equally well both ways, nothing in them makes one moment the explainer and the other the explained, so preferring the past is a choice the laws themselves don't make. Main alternative verdict: "Neither explains the other. Each is just the same motion seen from a different moment, and what explains both is the laws together with how the solar system formed."
- FIX NEEDED: None essential. If the case wants to test whether calculating counts as explaining, add: "Is there any difference between what Priya claims and what her colleague claims?"

### N24 — Two sealed sorts of matter
- Situation: Imagine a universe with two sorts of matter. Each sort can make atoms, machines and living things, but the two pass through each other without effect: nothing made of one sort can push, see, signal to or record anything made of the other. People made of the first sort have built a computer that, given enough time and memory, can carry out any calculation their sort of matter allows. People made of the second sort have built one that can carry out any calculation theirs allows. Some calculations can be carried out only in the first sort of matter, and some only in the second. Asha says: "Take the two computers together, and this universe has a machine that can carry out any calculation its laws allow." Bram says: "No machine in this universe can do that. A question written in one sort of matter can never be read by a computer made of the other." (Unchanged.)
- Question: Which of them is right about whether any machine in that universe can carry out every calculation its laws allow? Does Bram's reason explain why or why not?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Bram is right, and his reason explains why. "The two computers together" are not one machine. A question has to be written in one sort of matter, so it can be given only to the computer made of that sort, and the calculations only the other sort can do can never be applied to it. Asha is right only in a weak sense: between them, the two computers cover every calculation. But nobody in that universe can hand an arbitrary question to the pair and get it done.
- CONFIDENCE: Medium. Main alternative verdict: "Asha is right if each question is simply posed to whichever computer suits it. The two together do cover everything." That holds only if one allows a user who can write in both sorts of matter, which the story rules out.
- FIX NEEDED: Minor. The disagreement turns on what counts as "a machine that can carry out any calculation". Proposed clarification: "a single machine to which any question, however it is written, can be given, and which then carries out any calculation the laws allow on it." Readers may also stumble over the idea that some calculations are possible only in one sort of matter. One sentence would help, such as "the two sorts obey different laws, so each can do things the other cannot".

### N25 — What holds the universe up
- Situation: Three cosmologies each say what holds the universe up. The first: the universe rests, forever unmoving, on the head of a dog that stands outside it; the dog is eternal and will never change. The second is the same in every respect, except that it says a turtle. The third: a turtle holds the universe up, that turtle stands on another turtle, and so on down without end. In all three, nothing inside the universe can reach, see or affect whatever holds it up. (Unchanged.)
- Question: Does any of the three explain what holds the universe up? Do the dog story and the turtle story say different things?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: None of them explains it. The dog and the lone turtle are arbitrary picks that could be swapped for any other creature, nothing could ever tell us which is right, and each leaves the same question about the holder: what does it stand on? The endless stack of turtles just passes that question down forever, so it is never answered. All three also take for granted that a universe needs holding up at all, and none gives a reason to think so. The dog and turtle stories say different things in words, since a dog is not a turtle. But nothing we could see, do or predict depends on which is true, so the difference is idle.
- CONFIDENCE: High that none explains. Medium on the second question. Some would say flatly that the two say different things (one could be true and the other false), and others that, since no possible observation could tell them apart, they say the same thing.
- FIX NEEDED: Minor. The question presupposes that the universe needs holding up, and a careful reader will want to reject that. Optionally add: "Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the universe does need something to hold it up."
