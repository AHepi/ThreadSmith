### N1 — The seasons and the sun god
- Situation: Asked why there are seasons, Tomas explains that the Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space while the Earth travels round the sun, as a spinning body keeps its axis steady. For half the year the northern half of the Earth leans towards the sun: the sun's rays strike it more directly and the days are longer, so it warms. Half a year later it leans away, the rays strike it at a slant, and it cools. Each piece can be checked on its own: a lamp warms a card that faces it more than a card tilted away from it, and a spinning top keeps its axis pointing one way. The same tilt also tells how high the noon sun will stand at each time of year. Then Tomas adds one sentence: "And the sun god, who approves of this arrangement, keeps the tilt steady." He says nothing more about the sun god, and the rest of what he says is unchanged.
- Question: Does Tomas explain why there are seasons? Is the sentence about the sun god part of what explains them?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, Tomas explains the seasons; no, the sun-god sentence is not part of what explains them. Strike it out and the account works exactly as before: the steady tilt was already accounted for by the spinning-top point, and nothing about the seasons depends on anyone approving of anything. The sentence is an unsupported extra riding along with a good explanation; at most it muddies the account by giving a second, idle reason for the steady tilt.
- CONFIDENCE: high.
- FIX NEEDED: None essential. One note: the sun-god sentence claims the same job the spinning-top point already does (keeping the tilt steady), so a reader may take it either as idle decoration or as a rival cause that makes Tomas's account slightly inconsistent. Both readings give the same verdict. If the set wants a pure idle add-on, give the god no job the account already covers, e.g. "And the sun god looks on and approves."

### N2 — The myth amended
- Situation: As sketched. A storyteller tells the old Greek myth of the seasons. Hades, god of the underworld, carried off Persephone. Her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, won her release, but on terms that bind Persephone to go back to Hades for part of every year. While Persephone is away, Demeter grieves and makes the world cold and bare so that nothing grows: that is winter. When Persephone returns, Demeter rejoices and the world grows warm again. A sailor then tells the storyteller that in lands far to the south, summer comes at the very time the Greeks have winter, and winter when they have summer. The storyteller changes the myth: when Demeter grieves, she does not put out the warmth of the world but sends it south across the sea, and when Persephone comes back, Demeter calls the warmth home. The changed myth agrees with everything the sailor reported, north and south.
- Question: Does the changed myth explain the seasons in both the north and the south?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: No. The changed myth fits the sailor's report only because it was altered to fit it: nothing in the story of Demeter's grief said her warmth went south, rather than nowhere or anywhere else, until the report came in. It still gives no reason to think Demeter exists or that anyone's grief moves the weather, so what it says about the south is the sailor's report retold in the myth's terms, not a reason for it.
- CONFIDENCE: medium-high. Some careful readers would say "it is an explanation, just a false and patched one", meaning an attempted explanation that fails. That is a difference over the word "explain", not over the case: on neither reading does the myth tell anyone why the seasons are reversed in the south.
- FIX NEEDED: The question can be read as "Does it offer an explanation?" (it offers an account of causes, so arguably yes) or "Does it succeed in explaining?" (no). Pick one. Suggested: "Does the changed myth give the real reason the seasons are reversed in the south? Is it a better explanation of the seasons than the original myth was?"

### N3 — Persephone at home
- Situation: As sketched. In ancient Greece a farmer asks why winter comes every year. He is told the myth of Persephone. Hades, god of the underworld, carried her off; her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, won her release on terms that bind Persephone to go back to Hades for part of every year. While she is away, Demeter grieves and in her grief makes the world cold so that nothing grows. Consider the myth only as it stood for the Greeks. Every land they knew had its winter at the same time of year, and they knew of no other lands. The myth agrees with all of that: winter comes once a year, at the same season, in every land they knew. Nobody has ever seen Persephone leave or return, or seen Demeter grieve.
- Question: Does the myth explain why winter comes every year? Is it an explanation of winter?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: It is an explanation of winter in form, since it says what causes winter and why it comes once a year (the bargain that sends Persephone back each year). But it does not explain why winter comes: it is false, and even for the Greeks nothing counted in its favour except winter itself, since nobody ever saw any of the events it describes. Fitting every fact the Greeks had is not enough, because many other god-stories would have fitted those facts just as well.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Thoughtful people split on whether a false account ever "explains". The main alternative verdict is: "For the Greeks, yes: it was their explanation, and by what they knew it explained winter." The instruction "consider the myth only as it stood for the Greeks" invites that reading. A smaller group would say it is not even an explanation, only a story.
- FIX NEEDED: The two questions use "explain" in two senses (offering an account of causes, and giving the true reason), and the case doesn't say which is meant, so answers will differ on wording alone. Suggested rewrite: (a) "Does the myth offer an account of what causes winter and why it recurs every year?" (b) "Does it actually explain why winter comes?" (c) if wanted, "Given only what the Greeks knew, did they have good reason to accept it?" Also, the last sentence ("Nobody has ever seen...") leans towards "no". That is fair, but the case should be read knowing it is there.

### N4 — The tilt and the midnight sun
- Situation: As sketched. Over two thousand years ago, an astronomer on a Mediterranean island proposes that the Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space as the Earth goes round the sun. She proposes it to explain one thing: why the noon sun stands higher in the sky in summer than in winter. Put together with what was known about heat and about spinning bodies, it also explains the seasons she knows. She and everyone she knows have seen only the lands around the Mediterranean, and nobody has heard of the far north or the far south. It follows from her idea, though nobody at the time works it out, that far enough north the summer sun never sets, that near the middle of the Earth there are no seasons, and that far to the south the seasons are reversed. Centuries later, travellers find all three.
- Question: When she proposed it, did her explanation already explain the midnight sun of the far north, before anyone had seen it or thought of it? Or did it come to explain it only later, when someone worked out the connection or when travellers found it?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Her idea already contained the explanation of the midnight sun when she proposed it: the midnight sun follows from the idea as she stated it, and nothing had to be added later. But nobody had explained the midnight sun until someone worked out the connection. The travellers added nothing to the explanation; they only confirmed what the idea already implied. So the idea had it from the start, and the explaining, as something people do, came when someone worked it out.
- CONFIDENCE: medium, and low on the wording. Thoughtful people split on whether "explain" belongs to ideas or to people. The main alternative: "It came to explain the midnight sun only when someone worked out the connection; before that it was only true that it would explain it." Few would say it waited on the travellers.
- FIX NEEDED: The question runs two things together: whether the midnight sun already followed from the idea, and whether anyone had yet explained it. Suggested split: "Did the midnight sun already follow from her idea as she stated it?" and "Had anyone explained the midnight sun before someone worked out the connection?" Minor: the "nobody has heard of the far north" condition is a made-up stipulation (Greeks of that era did have travellers' reports from the far north). It does no harm as fiction, but don't present it as history.

### N5 — A farmer's rule carried south
- Situation: As sketched. In a village on the northern shore of the Mediterranean, farmers follow a rule handed down for generations: "Sow the wheat after the first autumn rains, in October, and it ripens in early summer, in June." Nobody in the village knows why it works. It has held year after year, and fields sown at other times have done worse. A family from the village settles in a land far south of the equator. There the seasons fall in the opposite months: the cool rains begin in April and May, and the hottest months are December and January. The father means to farm by the village rule.
- Question: What, if anything, does the village rule tell him about when to sow and when his wheat will ripen in the new land?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Little he can rely on. Read by its months, it would mislead him: October there is spring, and wheat sown then would grow up into the hottest months. Read by its seasons ("after the first autumn rains"), it points to sowing in April or May and expecting ripe wheat around December, which is a sensible first guess. But the rule can't tell him which reading to trust, because nobody knows why it works. He would need to know why it works (wheat grows through the cool, wet months and ripens as it turns warm and dry), or else to try it.
- CONFIDENCE: medium-high. Alternative: "It tells him to sow after the first autumn rains, which there means April or May, so the rule carries over well enough." Some people read the rule as being about seasons rather than months.
- FIX NEEDED: The rule mixes two cues (the rains and the month), and the new land is described so that the rains cue lines up neatly (cool rains in April and May). That hints at the intended answer. It's acceptable if the case is testing which part of an unexplained rule carries over. But "What, if anything" is open-ended, which makes verdicts hard to compare. Suggested questions: "If he sows in October, as the rule's months say, should he expect ripe wheat in June?" and "If he sows after the first cool rains, can the rule itself tell him that this will work?"

### N6 — Two astronomers and the unknown world
- Situation: As sketched. Two astronomers on a Mediterranean island, over two thousand years ago, hold that the tilt of the Earth's axis causes the seasons: the axis leans and keeps pointing the same way in space, so each half of the Earth leans towards the sun for part of the year and away from it for another part. Neither has any evidence about places beyond Europe, North Africa and the nearer parts of Asia. The tilt idea, taken as it stands, implies strange things about places far beyond: days and nights six months long in the far north, and seasons reversed in the far south. Each astronomer states the idea with a limit. Iras says: "The tilt causes the seasons in the Mediterranean and the lands around it. I make no claim about anywhere else." Leon says: "The tilt causes the seasons in the Mediterranean and the lands around it; everywhere else on Earth, the seasons also come at the same times of year as they do here." Each statement agrees with everything either of them has seen or heard.
- Question: Does Iras explain the seasons in the Mediterranean? Does Leon?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Iras explains them; Leon, as stated, does not do so coherently. Iras gives the tilt account and just declines to draw its consequences elsewhere. That is caution, not a flaw in her account of home, though her silence can't stop the tilt having consequences beyond her world. Leon also names the tilt as the cause at home, but then insists the seasons everywhere else fall at the same times, which the tilt itself rules out for the far south unless something he never mentions overrides it there. He keeps the cause while denying what it must do, so his account as a whole contradicts itself. He explains the home seasons only in the part he shares with Iras.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Thoughtful people could reasonably give either of two other verdicts. (a) Both explain the home seasons equally well, and Leon just adds a false prediction about far places that doesn't touch his account of home. (b) Leon explains nothing even at home, because his second clause shows he doesn't really take the tilt to be the cause.
- FIX NEEDED: There is a material mismatch with the source. The sketch says Leon's statement is the book's, but the book's version (Deutsch, Ch.1, p.27) is: "In the known world, the seasons happen at the times of year predicted by the axis-tilt theory; everywhere else on Earth, they also happen at …" That claims only *when* the seasons happen, not what causes them. The sketch's Leon says "The tilt causes the seasons" at home. The two make different cases. The book's Leon gives a timetable and plainly explains nothing; the sketch's Leon gives the cause and then contradicts what it implies. Choose one. If the book's case is wanted, rewrite Leon's line as: "In the Mediterranean and the lands around it, the seasons come at the times the tilt idea predicts; everywhere else on Earth, they also come at those same times of year." The verdict would then be firmer (high): Iras explains the home seasons; Leon does not, because he gives a timetable, not a cause.

### N7 — Two bakers
- Situation: As sketched. Two bakers in the same town each explain why their bread dough rises. Yeast in the dough feeds on sugars in the flour and gives off gas; the gas is trapped in the stretchy dough, which swells. Maya adds: "This holds when the dough is kept between 18 and 35 degrees." She found those limits by experience and says nothing about why they are where they are. Bea gives the same explanation with the same limits, and adds why: below about 18 degrees the yeast works so slowly that the dough hardly rises in the usual time, and above about 35 degrees the yeast begins to be harmed, and nearer 50 it dies. Between those temperatures both doughs rise just as described.
- Question: Does each baker explain why her dough rises?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, both do. What makes the dough rise is yeast giving off gas that the stretchy dough traps, and both bakers say that. Bea explains something extra, namely why the rise only works within a temperature range. Maya not knowing why her limits sit where they do leaves her explanation of the rise itself intact: within the range, she has the reason right.
- CONFIDENCE: high. A minor alternative ("Maya's explanation is less complete") is true but doesn't change the answer.
- FIX NEEDED: None.

### N8 — The machine that runs forever
- Situation: As sketched. At a fair, an inventor shows drawings of a wheel that would keep turning and driving a water pump forever, with no fuel, no wind, no flowing water and nobody pushing it. Visitors ask why no such machine exists. Pia answers: "Nobody has found the right design yet. Hundreds of designs have been tried, and every one has come to a stop. Perhaps someone will find one that works." Quentin answers: "A machine that keeps doing work with nothing going into it would be making energy out of nothing. The energy anything gives out must be matched by energy put in or used up somewhere. That holds for any design, including ones nobody has tried."
- Question: Does each answer explain why there is no machine that runs forever without fuel?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Only Quentin's explains it. Pia reports that every attempt so far has failed and hopes one may yet work. That is evidence plus a hope, but it gives no reason, and it leaves open that the next design might succeed. Quentin gives a reason that applies to every design, tried or not: such a machine would have to make energy out of nothing.
- CONFIDENCE: high. A nitpicking alternative is that Pia explains why no machine has been built *so far*, on the false assumption that one could be; even so, she doesn't explain why none exists.
- FIX NEEDED: None. (Pia's answer fits the narrower reading "why hasn't one been built yet?" The case works as posed, and narrowing the question to "why can't one be built?" would tip it towards Quentin, so leave it.)

### N9 — Dark moths
- Situation: As sketched. A species of moth rests by day on tree bark. In a district where smoke has blackened the bark, the moths change over many generations until most of them have dark wings. Pale moths stand out on the dark bark and birds eat more of them, and moths get their wing colour from their parents. In a clean district nearby, where the bark is pale, most moths of the same species are pale. No moth has any idea of the colour of the bark or of its own wings, and nobody planned the change.
- Question: Is there knowledge of the colour of the bark anywhere in this story? If so, where is it, and what holds it?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: In the ordinary sense, no: nobody in the story knows the colour of the bark, whether moth, bird or person. In a looser sense, yes: the moths' inherited colouring now matches the bark, and that match is carried in their heredity, the genes passed from parents to offspring across the population. Birds put it there by eating the moths that didn't match, so you could read the colour of the bark off the moths. Whether to call that "knowledge" or just a match produced by selection is a choice of words, not a fact about the moths.
- CONFIDENCE: low. Thoughtful people will split on the word. One camp says "yes, knowledge, held in the moths' genes"; the other says "no, a match or an adaptation, but knowledge needs a knower".
- FIX NEEDED: Unusable as posed. The answer turns wholly on how widely one uses the word "knowledge", which the case leaves open, so it cannot test anything against ordinary judgment. Suggested rewrite as a question ordinary judgment can settle: "Is there anything in the moths that carries information about the bark, something from which an observer could tell the bark's colour? If so, where is it, and how did it get there?" (Verdict: yes. It is in the inherited wing colour and the genes behind it, and it got there because birds ate the moths that stood out.) Or ask directly: "Would it be right to say the moths know the bark is dark?" (Verdict: no.)

### N10 — The burned notebook
- Situation: As sketched. Halima, an engineer, spends a month working out why a proposed design for a long bridge would fail in strong wind: gusts of a certain speed would make the deck twist a little further with each swing until it broke. She writes the whole explanation, with her calculations, in a notebook, and tells no one. Before anyone reads it, a fire destroys the notebook, and she dies soon afterwards. Nobody else has worked it out.
- Question: Did Halima create knowledge? Does that knowledge exist now?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, she created knowledge: she worked out, correctly, why the design would fail, which nobody had done before. No, that knowledge does not exist now: it was held only in her notebook and in her head, and both are gone. The fact it was about (that the design would fail in that way) still holds, and someone may work it out again, but that would be a fresh discovery, not her knowledge surviving.
- CONFIDENCE: medium-high. Two alternatives: some would say knowledge must be shared or checked by others before it counts, so she had only a private conclusion; others would say the knowledge still "exists" in the sense that the truth is there to be found again.
- FIX NEEDED: The sketch never says her explanation was right, and "Did she create knowledge?" depends on that. Add something like: "Her explanation was correct: years later a bridge built to that design failed exactly as she had worked out." The sketch already says she told no one; it could also say that no draft or other copy survives anywhere.

### N11 — Two students, ten drafts each
- Situation: As sketched. Two students each write ten drafts of an essay on the same topic. Jana hands each draft to her teacher, who returns it with a mark and no comment. She writes each new draft without knowing what the teacher liked or disliked in the earlier ones, and in the end hands in whichever draft received the highest mark. Kasia shows her drafts to nobody. After each one, she writes down what she thinks is wrong with it (a claim with no support, a paragraph that repeats another, an ending that does not follow) and writes the next draft to fix those faults. She hands in the draft that best survives her own criticism. The two final essays are good, and about equally good.
- Question: Did each student create what is good in her final essay?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, both did. Every sentence in each final essay was written by the student. Jana's teacher only ranked her drafts, and because the marks told her nothing about what worked, they didn't shape what she wrote. The difference lies elsewhere. Kasia also judged and improved her own work, so she learned something across her ten drafts. Jana relied on the teacher's judgment to pick the best one, and her tenth draft was no better informed than her first.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Alternative: "Jana wrote the drafts, but whether her final essay is good was settled by the teacher's choice among them, and she may just have been lucky once, so what is good in it is partly the teacher's doing."
- FIX NEEDED: Minor. "What is good in her final essay" can mean the good qualities of the text (both students wrote those) or the fact that this draft, and not a weaker one, was handed in (for Jana, the teacher's choice). If the second is what's being tested, ask it separately: "Who picked Jana's final essay, and does that make any of its quality the teacher's?"

### N12 — Consent by rephrasing
- Situation: As sketched. Before a knee operation, a surgeon reads two patients the same list of risks: infection, a blood clot in the leg, stiffness, and numbness around the scar. Asked to repeat the list, each does so word for word. The surgeon then asks each of them the same thing in other words: "Suppose your calf is swollen and sore a week after the operation. What might that be?" Then a question about a slightly different operation: "If we were operating on your elbow instead of your knee, which of these would you still need to worry about?" Mr Okafor answers each question by reciting the list again, in the same words. Ms Varga says the swollen calf could be the clot and that she would call at once; and that with the elbow, infection, stiffness and numbness would still apply, and a clot in the leg less so.
- Question: Does each patient understand the risks of the knee operation?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Ms Varga does; as far as anyone can tell, Mr Okafor does not. He can repeat the list but can't use it: asked what a swollen calf might mean, or which risks carry over to an elbow, he just recites the words again. Ms Varga applies the list to situations she wasn't given, and for someone about to consent, that is what understanding the risks amounts to.
- CONFIDENCE: high. Caveat: Mr Okafor might understand but be too anxious, or too thrown by the questions, to show it. On this evidence, though, the surgeon should not take it that he understands.
- FIX NEEDED: None. Optional: "Does each patient show that he or she understands the risks?" would match what the surgeon can actually know.

### N13 — Popper's parrot
- Situation: As sketched. A parrot lives in the hall where the philosopher Karl Popper gives a course of lectures. It learns several of his favourite sentences by heart and repeats them, word for word, to visitors. A student who missed the lectures later learns some of Popper's ideas by listening to the parrot and working out what the sentences mean. When a visitor asks the parrot a question about what it has said, it answers with another of its sentences, or with a squawk. Had the lectures been recipes, it would have repeated those just as readily.
- Question: Has the parrot acquired Popper's ideas? What part did the parrot play in the student's learning them?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: No, the parrot has not acquired Popper's ideas, only the sounds of some of his sentences. It can't answer a question about them, and it would have repeated recipes just as happily, so nothing it does depends on what the words mean. In the student's learning it was a carrier, like a recording or a letter: it delivered the words, and the student did the work of finding the ideas in them.
- CONFIDENCE: high.
- FIX NEEDED: None.

### N14 — The robot that learned to walk
- Situation: As sketched. Dr Ferreira spends months designing a walking robot: its legs, joints and motors, its sensors, and a programming language for its control program. The language has commands such as "veer left by so many degrees", "lengthen stride" and "step over". She wrote each of them by hand from what she knows of balance, levers and the geometry of legs, keeping in mind throughout the sorts of walking the robot would need to do. She then writes only one simple program in the language: "walk forward; stop at an obstacle". From there a computer takes over. It makes thousands of slightly random variants of the program, tries each on a simulated robot over courses she chose (flat ground, slopes, small obstacles), keeps the one that walks best, makes variants of that, and repeats. After many rounds the program walks well on those courses. Put on rough, rocky ground unlike any of the test courses, the robot also walks well.
- Question: The robot walks well on ground it was never tested on. Whose knowledge makes it do so?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Mostly Dr Ferreira's, as far as the story tells us. Walking well on rocky ground it was never tested on most plausibly comes from what carries over to any ground: the legs, joints and sensors, and her hand-written commands for balancing, veering and stepping over things, all built from her general knowledge of balance and leverage. The computer's search only kept programs that did well on her chosen courses, so on its own it had no reason to produce skill on ground unlike those; most likely it tuned how her commands are combined. Without looking inside the evolved program, though, nobody can rule out that the search found something she didn't put there.
- CONFIDENCE: medium-low. The main alternative is "Both: her design, plus whatever the search found, since the evolved program does things her one simple program couldn't." Some thoughtful people would also reject the word "whose", because a trial-and-error search is not a someone.
- FIX NEEDED: The sketch is both leading and underspecified. (1) It stresses that she wrote every command from her knowledge, "keeping in mind throughout the sorts of walking the robot would need to do", which points to her. (2) It gives no fact that could settle how much the search added, such as how her simple starting program does on rocks, or what the evolved program does that hers didn't. (3) "Whose knowledge" assumes the answer is a person. Suggested rewrite: drop the "keeping in mind" clause; add one telling fact, for example "Her one simple program, run on the rocky ground, falls within a few steps" and/or "Given a plain, general-purpose command language instead of hers, the same search on the same courses produces a program that walks on the courses but falls on rocks"; and ask: "What accounts for the robot walking well on the rocky ground: her design, the search, or both? How could you tell?"

### N15 — The miscopied notes
- Situation: As sketched. Salma misses a class and borrows a friend's copy of the teacher's explanation of why a hot-air balloon rises: heating the air inside the balloon makes it take up more room, so the air in the balloon is lighter than the same volume of the cooler air outside, and the cooler air pushes the balloon up. The copy contains one slip: it says heated air takes up less room. Salma notices that with "less" the rest does not hang together, since the balloon's air would then be heavier than the air outside and the balloon would sink, and she changes it to "more". She then explains the idea to the teacher in her own words, and he agrees it is exactly what he taught.
- Question: Does Salma now have the teacher's explanation? In coming to have it, did she create anything herself?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, Salma has the teacher's explanation: she can state it in her own words, and he confirms it is what he taught. And yes, she created something herself, though it was small. Nobody gave her the correction: she saw that "less" made the rest fall apart and worked out that it had to be "more". What she ended up with was not new, since it was the teacher's idea, but her copy of it was repaired by her own reasoning rather than handed to her.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Alternative: "She created nothing; she restored something already known, and fixing an obvious slip isn't creating."
- FIX NEEDED: "Create" is ambiguous between "make something new to the world" and "make something oneself that one wasn't given", and the verdict turns on which is meant. Say which, or split the question: "Did she work out anything that was not in what she was given?" (yes, the correction) and "Did she produce any idea the teacher didn't already have?" (no).

### N16 — The domino that never falls
- Situation: As sketched. A huge network of dominoes is built as a calculating machine. Each domino is spring-loaded: when knocked over, it knocks over its neighbour and then springs back up, so waves of falling dominoes travel along the network's branching and looping lines. This network is set up to test whether a number is prime. The number is fed in as a row of exactly that many dominoes, and one particular domino somewhere in the network falls only if the number turns out to have a divisor. The network is fed 641 and started. An observer who does not know what the network is for watches for hours and asks: "Why does that domino there never fall?" Answer A: that domino never falls because none of its neighbours ever falls; they never fall because none of theirs does; and so on, back through billions of steps to the first domino, so that none of the waves set off by knocking the first domino over ever reaches it. Answer B: "Because 641 is prime. The network tests whether its input is prime, and that domino falls only when a divisor is found." Both answers are true.
- Question: Does each answer explain why that domino never falls?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: B explains it; A, as given, doesn't really. A is true, but it only passes the question from each domino to its neighbours and ends by saying that no wave ever reaches this domino, which is what the observer had already seen, and the same answer would do for any domino that stays up. B tells the observer what is special about this one: the machine hunts for divisors, this domino falls only when one is found, and 641 has none.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Alternative: "Both explain it, at different levels: A is the full mechanical account (followed step by step, it would show exactly why), and B is the account in terms of what the machine is doing." People who hold that a complete causal history always explains will give that verdict.
- FIX NEEDED: The sketch doesn't settle whether A is the full step-by-step trace (billions of steps, which would in principle contain the whole calculation) or only its summary ("none of the waves ever reaches it"), and the verdict may differ between the two. Say which the observer actually receives. Suggested: "Answer A, as the observer hears it: 'None of the waves set off from the first domino ever reaches it: each of its neighbours stays up because theirs do, and so on back to the start.'"

### N17 — The transistor at the end of the run
- Situation: As sketched. A computer runs a program that finds the factors of 15. At the end of the run, one particular transistor is on. Someone asks: "Why is this transistor on at the end of the run?" Answer A: the electrons in the computer started out in such-and-such a state, and the laws that govern their motion carried them to a state in which this transistor is on. Answer B: the computer was finding the factors of 15, and this transistor is part of how it holds the answer, 3 and 5. Both answers are true.
- Question: Are A and B two answers to one question, or answers to two different questions? Does each explain why the transistor is on?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: They are two answers to one question, given at two levels, and only B, as given, explains in a way that tells the asker anything. A is true but, as stated, it is a placeholder: "the electrons started in some state and the laws carried them here" would be just as true of any transistor in any state, so it says nothing about why this one is on. Filled out in full, A would be a real physical account of how the transistor came to be on, and so an explanation of a sort, though no one could follow it. B would still be needed to say why it is this transistor: it holds part of the answer, 3 and 5.
- CONFIDENCE: medium on "B explains; A as stated explains little"; low on "one question or two", which is partly a matter of words. Many would say A answers "how did it come about?" and B answers "what is it doing?", so they are answers to two questions.
- FIX NEEDED: A is stated only in outline ("such-and-such a state"), so some readers will judge it as an empty placeholder and others as an imagined full physical account. Say which. The first question is partly about wording; consider replacing it with "Does A, as stated, tell the asker anything B does not? Does B tell anything A does not?"

### N18 — Two footbridges on opening day
- Situation: As sketched. Two footbridges of similar length open on the same day in two towns. Rhea designed hers from a worked-out theory of how loads, stiffness and sway interact; by her calculations, the bridge would stay steady under any crowd she expected. She never tested it with a crowd before it opened. Dov designed his by copying, as closely as he could, a village footbridge that had stood for eighty years; he did not know why its design worked, only that it had never given trouble. The village bridge had never carried more than a few dozen people at once. On opening day a large crowd walks onto each bridge, and each bridge sways badly.
- Question: On opening day, did each designer meet a problem? Was each of them surprised?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Both met a problem, since each bridge swayed badly under a crowd. Both were very probably surprised, since each opened a bridge they expected to be steady. The difference is in what the surprise tells them. Rhea's calculations said the bridge would stay steady under such a crowd, so something in her theory is wrong and she has somewhere to look for it. Dov's expectation rested only on the old bridge's record, which never covered a crowd like this; he learns that his model was never tested for crowds, not where the fault lies.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. The sketch says nothing about either designer's state of mind, nor whether the opening-day crowd was within the range Rhea planned for. A thoughtful person might say Dov had no particular expectation and so wasn't surprised, or ought not to have been.
- FIX NEEDED: Ambiguous in three places. (1) "Any crowd she expected": say whether the opening-day crowd was within that range, because if it was larger, her calculations weren't contradicted. (2) Say what Dov expected, e.g. "He was confident it would be as trouble-free as the original." (3) "Meet a problem" can mean a practical problem (a swaying bridge, which both clearly had) or a clash between what one expected and what happened. If the second is what's being tested, ask it directly: "Did what happened contradict something each designer believed?"

### N19 — The novelist's two demands
- Situation: As sketched. Odile is writing a novel and wants two things from every chapter: prose that is rich and lyrical, and a story that keeps readers turning the pages. Planning chapter five, she sees, before writing it and before anyone has read any of it, that the long description of a valley at dawn, which she wants for its language, would stall the story at its tensest point. She drafts the chapter two ways. In the first, she cuts the description to one plain sentence: the chapter moves fast, and the lyrical passage is gone. In the second, the heroine flees across the valley at dawn, and the valley is described through what she sees as she runs: the language stays rich, and the chapter moves fast.
- Question: Did Odile have a problem before anyone read the chapter? Does either draft solve it?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, she had a problem before anyone read the chapter. She saw that her two demands clashed in this chapter, and a clash you can foresee is already a problem, whether or not a reader ever runs into it. The first draft doesn't solve it: it keeps the pace by giving up the lyrical passage, trading one demand for the other. The second draft, as described, does solve it: the valley keeps its rich language and the chapter moves fast, because the description has become part of the chase.
- CONFIDENCE: high. A minor alternative is that the first draft "solves" it by compromise; but for the problem as she set it (meeting both demands), it doesn't.
- FIX NEEDED: None. (The verdict on the second draft rests on the stipulation that it really does both; the sketch says so plainly.)

### N20 — Keeping count with string
- Situation: As sketched. Two groups of goatherds keep count of their flocks with string. Nobody can measure string exactly: every measurement is off by up to a tenth of an inch. In the first group, as each goat leaves the pen in the morning, a herder reels out a length of string equal to the length of that goat; in the evening, as each goat returns, its length is reeled back in, and when all the string is back, all the goats are home. In the second group, each goat stands for exactly one inch of string, and after every goat the herder trims or lengthens the string to the nearest whole inch. Both groups also add flocks together by joining strings, and split flocks by cutting a string in two.
- Question: Can each method keep count of a flock of any size, through any number of such joinings and splittings? What, if anything, limits it?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: The first method cannot; the second can. With goat lengths, every measurement adds a small error and nothing lets the herders tell a slightly wrong length from a right one, so errors pile up with every goat, join and cut. Past some number of goats or operations, a leftover stretch of string might be a missing goat or might just be error. (The first method also tracks the flock's total length rather than its number.) With whole inches, trimming to the nearest inch after every step wipes out each error before it can grow, since a tenth of an inch is well under half an inch, so the count stays exact through any number of steps. Only the supply of string and time limit the second method, plus the assumption that no single measurement is ever off by as much as half an inch.
- CONFIDENCE: high.
- FIX NEEDED: None needed. Two small clarifications would help: say how the second group tallies goats in and out (an inch reeled out for each goat leaving, an inch reeled in for each goat returning), and whether the tenth-of-an-inch limit holds however long the string gets.

### N21 — A rule she cannot state
- Situation: As sketched. Ngozi is a native speaker of English. She says "a lovely little old green French knife" and would never say "a green French little old lovely knife". She puts adjectives in this order in sentences she has never heard before, and gets it right every time. Asked why, she cannot state any rule; the other order "just sounds wrong". Nobody ever taught her such a rule; she picked it up as a small child from hearing people talk.
- Question: Does Ngozi know the rule? Did learning it involve creating anything?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Yes, she knows the rule in the sense that matters for speaking: she follows it reliably, even in sentences she has never heard, although she can't put it into words. Learning it did involve something like creating. Nobody gave her the rule, only examples, and to get new cases right she must have formed the general pattern herself from what she heard. Whether to call that "creating" or "picking up" is partly a matter of words; what is clear is that the rule was never handed to her.
- CONFIDENCE: medium on the first question (some insist that knowing a rule means being able to state it); low on the second.
- FIX NEEDED: "Create" is undefined here. Suggested second question: "Was the rule given to her, or did she form it herself from what she heard?" (Verdict: she formed it herself, with whatever inborn aptitude for language children bring.) The first question could also be split: does she know how to follow the rule, and does she know what the rule says?

### N22 — Denying the inner experience
- Situation: As sketched. People commonly report that there is something it is like to see blue: an inner experience over and above the light entering the eye and the word "blue". Two theorists deny that any such inner experiences exist. Wren says only: "There are no inner experiences. People are mistaken about this." Yuri says: "There are no inner experiences. When people look inwards, they are consulting memories of what they have just perceived, and the machinery that does this evolved to report inner experiences that never took place. People have false memories of experiences, not experiences."
- Question: Has either theorist explained why people seem to have inner experiences?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Wren has not: saying people are mistaken gives no reason why they seem to have such experiences, and it could be said of anything. Yuri has at least offered an explanation (people consult memories, and the machinery that does so evolved to report experiences that never happened), but it is thin and disputed. He gives no account of how a false memory of seeing blue could seem exactly like having seen it, which was the puzzle in the first place, nor of why such machinery would evolve. So Wren has not explained it, and Yuri has made an attempt that is not, as it stands, convincing.
- CONFIDENCE: high for Wren; low for Yuri. This is a live dispute: those who hold Yuri's view say it does explain the seeming, and critics say it only restates the puzzle. Thoughtful people disagree, so the Yuri half may need to be set aside.
- FIX NEEDED: As posed, the Yuri half cannot be settled by ordinary judgment. If kept, split the question: "Does Yuri at least offer a reason why people would seem to have inner experiences, where Wren offers none?" (yes, high confidence) and "Is Yuri's reason a good one?" (disputed, so set aside).

### N23 — The planets run backwards
- Situation: As sketched. Priya, an astronomer, knows exactly where each planet is tonight and how fast it is moving. The laws of motion work equally well run forwards or backwards in time, so she calculates where every planet was on this date a thousand years ago, and old records match her figures. She says: "The planets were where they were a thousand years ago because they are where they are tonight." Her colleague starts from the old records, calculates tonight's positions just as exactly, and says the reverse.
- Question: Does tonight's arrangement of the planets explain where they were a thousand years ago? Does their arrangement a thousand years ago explain where they are tonight?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: The arrangement a thousand years ago, together with the laws of motion, helps explain where the planets are tonight. Tonight's arrangement does not explain where they were a thousand years ago. It lets Priya work that out and is excellent evidence for it, but it did not bring it about. Her "because" is the "because" of "I know it rained, because the street is wet", not that of "the street is wet because it rained".
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Main alternative: "Since the laws run equally well both ways, neither arrangement explains the other any more than the reverse; each only lets you calculate the other, and the explanation lies in the laws and the history of the solar system." Some people who think hard about time-symmetric physics hold this view, but most people will say the past explains the present and not the other way round.
- FIX NEEDED: The sketch has Priya stress that the laws run both ways, which invites the symmetric answer. That is fine as a test, but "does X explain Y" may be taken to mean "can Y be worked out from X", and on that reading both answers are trivially yes. Suggested wording: "Is tonight's arrangement part of the reason the planets were where they were a thousand years ago? Is the arrangement a thousand years ago part of the reason they are where they are tonight?"

### N24 — Two sealed sorts of matter
- Situation: As sketched. Imagine a universe with two sorts of matter. Each sort can make atoms, machines and living things, but the two pass through each other without effect: nothing made of one sort can push, see, signal to or record anything made of the other. People made of the first sort have built a computer that, given enough time and memory, can carry out any calculation their sort of matter allows. People made of the second sort have built one that can carry out any calculation theirs allows. Some calculations can be carried out only in the first sort of matter, and some only in the second. Asha says: "Take the two computers together, and this universe has a machine that can carry out any calculation its laws allow." Bram says: "No machine in this universe can do that. A question written in one sort of matter can never be read by a computer made of the other."
- Question: Which of them is right about whether any machine in that universe can carry out every calculation its laws allow? Does Bram's reason explain why or why not?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: Bram is right, and his reason, together with the stated fact that each sort of matter allows some calculations the other doesn't, explains why. A machine that can carry out any calculation must be able to take any question and work on it. A question written in one sort of matter can never reach the computer that can do the calculations only the other sort allows, and no machine can join the two, since nothing of one sort can touch the other. Asha's "two computers together" are two machines that can pass nothing between them, not one machine.
- CONFIDENCE: medium-high. Alternative: the dispute is partly about words. If "a machine" may be any collection of devices, and "can carry out" means "for each calculation, some part of it can do it when the question happens to be posed in that part's matter", then Asha is right in that weak sense.
- FIX NEEDED: (1) Say what carrying out a calculation requires, e.g. "take a question from whoever poses it and give them back the answer"; this settles the verbal dispute. (2) The second question ("Does Bram's reason explain why or why not?") is garbled; suggested: "Does Bram's reason explain why no such machine can exist?" (3) Bram's reason works only together with the stated fact that some calculations are possible in only one sort of matter. The case states that fact, so this needs no change.

### N25 — What holds the universe up
- Situation: As sketched. Three cosmologies each say what holds the universe up. The first: the universe rests, forever unmoving, on the head of a dog that stands outside it; the dog is eternal and will never change. The second is the same in every respect, except that it says a turtle. The third: a turtle holds the universe up, that turtle stands on another turtle, and so on down without end. In all three, nothing inside the universe can reach, see or affect whatever holds it up.
- Question: Does any of the three explain what holds the universe up? Do the dog story and the turtle story say different things?
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT: None of the three explains it. The dog and the single turtle are chosen for no reason: nothing about the universe points to one rather than the other, and since nothing inside the universe can reach or see what holds it up, no observation could ever favour either. The endless turtles don't explain it either, because each turtle raises the same question the universe did, so the question is repeated forever and never answered. (One might also doubt the question itself, since nothing suggests the universe needs holding up.) Do the dog and turtle stories say different things? Literally, yes: one says a dog and the other a turtle. But the difference makes no difference to anything anyone could ever observe, or to what either story explains; as explanations they make the same empty claim, that something outside holds the universe up.
- CONFIDENCE: high on the first question; medium on the second. Some would say that two claims no possible observation could tell apart don't really say different things.
- FIX NEEDED: Split the second question: "Do the two stories make different claims?" (yes) and "Does the difference matter to anything either story explains or predicts?" (no).
