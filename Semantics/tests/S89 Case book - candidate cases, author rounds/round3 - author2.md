### N1 — The seasons and the sun god
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q1 (Does Tomas explain why there are seasons?) **Yes.** He gives the tilt, the fixed direction of the axis, and what follows from them: more direct sun and longer days for half the year, then slanting sun and shorter days. Each part can be checked with a lamp or a top, and the account also predicts how high the noon sun stands. One odd extra sentence doesn't undo that.
  - Q2 (Is the sun-god sentence part of what explains them?) **No.** It does no work. Tomas has already said why the tilt stays steady (the spinning-top effect). If you delete the sentence, or swap in a different god or none, nothing about the seasons comes out differently. Nothing can check the god's approval, and nothing depends on it.
- CONFIDENCE: high for both. A stricter reader might say the idle claim slightly spoils Q1 ("an explanation with an unsupported add-on is a flawed explanation"), but would still agree the tilt part explains. Almost nobody would count the god as doing any explaining.
- FIX NEEDED: minor. "Keeps the tilt steady" gives the god the same job the spinning top already does. That is probably intended, but a reader may wonder whether the god is meant as a rival cause or just a flourish. Also make clear whether Q1 is about his whole answer including the sentence, or about the tilt part (I read it as the whole answer).

### N2 — The myth amended
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT (Does the changed myth explain the seasons in both the north and the south?) **No.** The change was made only to fit the sailor's news. Nothing in the story of Demeter's grief required the warmth to go south rather than simply go out. The storyteller could just as easily have sent it east, or split it, if the news had been different. It fits the reports it was built around, but it gives no reason why things must be that way. It tells you nothing new you could check, for example what happens between north and south, or why the winter sun is low.
- CONFIDENCE: high that it doesn't explain in the sense of giving the real reason. Main alternative: some people would say it "explains in the way a myth explains", as a story that accounts for the facts, while agreeing it is not a good or true explanation. So the split is over the word, not the substance.
- FIX NEEDED: "explain" can mean "give an account that fits" or "give the actual reason". Say which is meant, or ask it as "Does the change give any real reason for the southern seasons?" Also make clear whether the question is about the whole changed myth or only whether the amendment adds anything. The original myth already fails as an explanation, so the answer is "no" either way, but the two readings invite different reasoning.

### N6 — Two astronomers and the unknown world
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q1 (Does Iras explain the seasons in the Mediterranean?) **Yes.** She gives the right cause and how it works (the axis leans and holds its direction, so her half of the Earth leans towards the sun and then away). Declining to make claims about places she knows nothing of is caution, not a gap in her explanation of home.
  - Q2 (Does Leon?) **Not properly. His words about the Mediterranean are the same, but his position as a whole undercuts them.** The tilt belongs to the whole Earth, so if it makes the north lean towards the sun in June, it must make the south lean away. Leon states the tilt as the cause here and then says the seasons elsewhere match ours, which is the very thing the tilt rules out. Either he hasn't grasped how his own cause works, or he believes in some unexplained exception. In both cases his hold on the explanation is weaker than Iras's, though he has named the right cause.
- CONFIDENCE: Iras medium-high. A few might fault her for not following her idea where it leads, but most would say she explains home. Leon **low to medium; thoughtful people could reasonably split.** The main alternative is that Leon does explain the Mediterranean seasons exactly as well as Iras, and has merely added a wrong guess about faraway places that doesn't touch the local explanation.
- FIX NEEDED: yes. (1) Say whether the astronomers know that the tilt idea implies reversed southern seasons. "Taken as it stands, implies" leaves this open, and the Leon verdict turns on it: knowingly denying a consequence is different from not having worked it out. (2) Leon's sentence is ambiguous. Is he saying the tilt also produces same-time seasons elsewhere (self-contradictory), or that something else does there (an unexplained extra)? (3) Say whether "explain" is judged by what each person says about the Mediterranean alone, or by their whole stated view.

### N8 — The machine that runs forever
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT (Does each answer explain why there is no machine that runs forever without fuel?)
  - Pia: **No.** She reports that attempts have failed and says one might still succeed. That is a record of failures, not a reason. It also leaves open that such a machine could exist, which is the opposite of explaining why it can't. "Nobody has found the design" just restates that nobody has built one.
  - Quentin: **Yes.** He gives a reason that covers every design, tried or not. Pumping water takes energy, energy has to come from somewhere, and this machine takes nothing in. That is why the failures happen, and why the next design will fail too.
- CONFIDENCE: high for both. A small minority might say Pia offers an explanation of a sort, just a poor or wrong one, or that Quentin only cites a rule without saying why the rule holds. Neither would change the ranking.
- FIX NEEDED: minor. "Driving a water pump" is doing important work: it makes clear the machine does useful work. Keep it. Someone who knows physics might note that a machine drawing on the surrounding warmth would need a second, different argument. "Nothing going into it" covers this, but the Situation lists fuel, wind and water, not heat. Adding "no heat" would close the gap.

### N12 — Consent by rephrasing
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT (Does each patient understand the risks of the knee operation?)
  - Mr Okafor: **No, or at least he gives no sign of it.** He can repeat the words, but when the question is put another way he can't link a swollen calf to a clot. He also can't tell which risks come from the leg and which from any operation. Being able to recite a list is not the same as knowing what it means.
  - Ms Varga: **Yes.** She recognises a symptom she was never told about as a sign of one of the risks, and knows to act on it. She also sorts which risks go with any operation and which go with the leg. That is what understanding looks like.
- CONFIDENCE: high. The one reasonable hedge concerns Okafor: nerves, language or shyness might hide an understanding he has. So a careful person might say "he has not shown he understands" rather than "he does not understand". On the evidence given, the answers stand.
- FIX NEEDED: slightly. Asking "Does each patient understand" invites the reply "we can't know what's in his head". Either accept "on this evidence", or add a line ruling out language or anxiety as the reason Okafor only recites. Also note that Okafor's recited list does include "a blood clot in the leg". The point is that he doesn't pick it out, and the case could say so to avoid a pedantic "he did name it".

### N15 — The miscopied notes
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q1 (Does Salma now have the teacher's explanation?) **Yes.** She can explain it in her own words, it matches what he taught, and he confirms it. She got there despite a wrong copy.
  - Q2 (In coming to have it, did she create anything herself?) **Yes, in a modest way.** Nobody gave her the correction. She worked out for herself that "less" made the rest fall apart and that "more" was needed. She also put the explanation in her own words. She did not invent a new explanation, since the one she ended with was the teacher's, but spotting and repairing the fault, and seeing why the pieces fit, was her own work.
- CONFIDENCE: Q1 high. Q2 **medium; thoughtful people could split.** The main alternative is "No: she only recovered what the teacher already had. Fixing a copying slip is reconstruction, not creation, and nothing new came into the world."
- FIX NEEDED: yes for Q2. "Create anything herself" is vague between "new to her, produced by her own thinking" and "new to anyone". Say which is meant. It might also help to say whether the friend's copy gave any other hint, such as a diagram, that pointed to "more".

### N19 — The novelist's two demands
- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q1 (Did Odile have a problem before anyone read the chapter?) **Yes.** She wanted two things from the chapter and could see they pulled against each other at that point. A problem doesn't need a reader to complain about it. It is there as soon as what you want can't all be had the obvious way, and she saw it herself.
  - Q2 (Does either draft solve it?) **The second does. The first does not really.** The first gets rid of the stall by giving up the lyrical passage, one of the two things she wanted, so it avoids the problem by sacrifice. The second keeps both: the valley is still described richly and the chase keeps the pace up.
- CONFIDENCE: Q1 high. Q2 medium-high. The main alternative is that the first draft is also a legitimate solution, a trade-off writers make all the time. On that view both drafts solve it, and the second just solves it better.
- FIX NEEDED: minor. "Does either draft solve it?" can be read as "does at least one?" (answer yes), and that hides the contrast. Ask "Does each draft solve it?". The phrase "before anyone has read any of it" slightly signals what Q1 is testing, but it is needed for the facts. The claim that the second draft "moves fast" with rich language is simply given by the narrator. That's fine, but readers are taking it on trust.
