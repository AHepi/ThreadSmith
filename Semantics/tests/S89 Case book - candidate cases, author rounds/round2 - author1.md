### N3 — A myth about winter

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does the myth explain why winter comes every year?* No, not really. It tells a story that fits a yearly winter, but only because it was made to fit: the bargain was given exactly the terms that would produce one winter a year. Nothing in the story was checked against anything, and any other tale of gods fitted to the same facts would have done just as well, even for the Greeks. It gives a cause, but it does not make winter any better understood.
  - *Is it an explanation of winter?* In the loose sense, yes. It is offered as an account of why winter happens, it has the shape of one (a cause, and a reason for the yearly timing), and the Greeks took it as one. It is a poor explanation, and a false one, rather than no explanation at all.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. People could reasonably split on both questions, because "explain" is used in two senses. The main alternative on Q1 is "Yes, it explains it, just wrongly": it gave the Greeks a reason for the yearly return that fitted everything they knew. On Q2 a stricter reader would say "No; a story that is false and untested is not an explanation, only a story."
- FIX NEEDED: "Explain" could mean "offers an account of why" or "succeeds in making it understood". The two questions look designed to separate those senses, but neither says which sense it uses, so a respondent can answer "yes" to both or "no" to both and still be consistent. "Consider the myth only as it stood for the Greeks" asks the reader to set aside what we now know (southern winters come at the opposite season, which the myth gets wrong), but it does not say whether "does it explain" is to be judged by the Greeks' standards or by ours.

### N13 — The parrot in the lecture hall

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Has the parrot acquired Professor Marsh's ideas?* No. It has acquired his sentences, not his ideas. It cannot say anything new about them or answer a question about them, and it would have repeated recipes just as readily, so nothing in it depends on what the sentences mean.
  - *What part did the parrot play in the student's learning them?* It was a carrier, like a tape recording or a notebook. It kept the words and passed them on. The student did all the work of understanding: she worked out what the sentences meant and so rebuilt the ideas for herself.
- CONFIDENCE: high. A few people might say the parrot "holds" the ideas in the way a book does, since the words that carry them are stored in it. Even they would agree that it does not have them in the way a person who understands them does.
- FIX NEEDED: "Acquired" is slightly ambiguous: it could mean "holds a copy of the words that express them" (as a book does) or "has grasped them". Asking "Does the parrot have or understand Professor Marsh's ideas?" would remove that ambiguity. Otherwise the case is clean.

### N16 — The domino that never falls

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does each answer explain why that domino never falls?* Answer B does: the domino is the one that falls only when a divisor is found, and 641 has no divisors, so it never falls. That answer also says what the observer did not know, namely what the network is for. Answer A is true, but it explains almost nothing. It says that nothing ever knocked the domino over, and that nothing ever knocked over its neighbours either. That is little more than the observed fact put in terms of dominoes. It does not say why no wave of motion ever reaches that domino, which is what the observer asked.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. People are unlikely to disagree about B. On A, some would say it is a genuine explanation, a low-level and unilluminating one that names the immediate cause (no neighbour fell at the right moment) and that could be spelled out in full.
- FIX NEEDED: It is unclear what Answer A actually contains. As written it is a summary ("and so on back; in sum...") that nearly restates the question. If it is meant to be the full trace of every domino's motion, many readers would give it more credit, and the case becomes a copy of N17. The case should say which it is.

### N17 — The transistor at the end of the run

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does Answer A tell the asker anything that Answer B does not?* Yes, in content: an immense amount of detail about how every electron moved, which parts switched and when. Almost none of it bears on the question in a way the asker could use, and since it would fill libraries, in practice it conveys nothing the asker could take in.
  - *Does Answer B tell the asker anything that Answer A does not?* Yes. It says that the run was a calculation of the factors of 15 and that this transistor is part of how the answer 3 and 5 is held. A never says any of that. These facts are settled by the physics that A describes, but nobody could read them out of A without already knowing what the computer was doing.
  - *Does each explain why the transistor is on?* B does. A shows, step by step, that from that exact starting state the transistor had to end up on, so it proves that the transistor is on. It does not show why, in the sense the asker wants. The transistor would have been on whatever the electrons' exact starting state had been, so long as the computer ran the same calculation, and A cannot show that. At most, A is an explanation in a thin, in-principle sense.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. On the third question a reductionist would say A is the complete and fundamental explanation and B only a convenient summary of it, so both explain. On the second question some would say that B adds no information, since A already settles every physical fact.
- FIX NEEDED: "Tell the asker" could mean "contains" or "conveys to this person in practice". A fills libraries, so the answer to Q1 turns on which is meant. Say which. It would also help to state whether "the computer was finding the factors of 15" refers only to the physical run or also to the programmer's purpose, since only the first could be said to be in A at all.

### N20 — Keeping count with string

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Can each method keep count of a flock of any size, through any number of joinings and splittings?* The first group's method cannot. Every goat measured out and back, every join and every cut adds an error of up to a tenth of an inch, and nothing ever removes it. With enough goats and enough joinings and splittings, the total error grows larger than a goat, and the string can no longer tell whether a goat is missing. The second group's method can, in principle. Every error is well under half an inch, so rounding to the nearest whole inch wipes it out each time. The string always holds the exact count, however large the flock and however many operations are done.
  - *What, if anything, limits it?* The first method is limited by the build-up of measurement error. The more goats and operations there are, the sooner the error swamps one goat's length. The number is in the hundreds of measurements at worst, and more if the errors partly cancel. The second method has no limit on accuracy, only practical limits: a very large flock needs a very long string, and the method depends on two conditions. The herder must round after joinings and splittings as well as after goats, and measuring even a very long string must stay within half an inch.
- CONFIDENCE: high on the main contrast (errors pile up in the first method and are corrected in the second). Medium on the second method's limits, which depend on details the case leaves open.
- FIX NEEDED: (1) The case says the second group rounds "after every goat". It does not say they round after joining or cutting strings. Several joins or cuts in a row without a goat in between could build up more than half an inch of error, so state that they round after every operation. (2) "Off by up to a tenth of an inch" should say plainly that this holds however long the string being measured is. The "any size" answer for the second method depends on it. (3) Optionally, say whether the strings are kept across days, since in the first method the goats' lengths change as they grow.

### N22 — Denying the inner experience

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does either theorist give any reason why people believe and say that they have inner experiences?* Wren gives none. He only says that people are mistaken. Yuri gives one: when people look inwards they are reading memories of what they have just perceived, and the machinery that does this produces reports of experiences that never happened, so people sincerely believe and say they had them. Whether that reason is good or true is another matter. The key step is only asserted: he never says why such machinery would have evolved. Still, he gives a reason.
- CONFIDENCE: high that Wren gives none. Medium-high that Yuri's counts as a reason. Some would say it is mostly a redescription ("people report experiences because their reporting machinery reports experiences"), and some would say it undermines itself, since a false memory of an experience seems to be an experience of some kind.
- FIX NEEDED: None essential. The question asks only whether a reason is given, not whether it is good, and a careful answer should keep those apart. Yuri's longer statement may nudge readers toward "yes" by length alone. Giving Wren a sentence of similar length that contains no reason (for example, a remark about how common the mistake is) would make the contrast fairer.

### N23 — The planets tonight and a thousand years ago

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does tonight's arrangement of the planets explain where they were a thousand years ago?* No. Tonight's positions let Priya work out the past positions and are good evidence of them, but they are not why the planets were there. The present cannot have made the past what it was. Priya's "because" is the "because" of calculation, not of cause.
  - *Does their arrangement a thousand years ago explain where they are tonight?* Yes, in the ordinary sense and together with the laws of motion. The planets are where they are tonight because of where they were earlier, how they were moving, and gravity acting since then. It is a thin explanation: most of the work is done by the laws, and the earlier arrangement is itself left unexplained.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Some careful people, especially physicists who stress that the laws run equally well both ways, would say the two directions are on a par: either both states "explain" each other in the same thin sense, or neither does, and only the laws explain. The common view, which I give above, is that explanation runs forward with cause.
- FIX NEEDED: The questions ask whether an "arrangement" explains, without saying whether the laws of motion are included. Asking "Does tonight's arrangement, together with the laws of motion, explain..." would stop respondents splitting over that point instead of over direction. Priya's quoted "because" invites readers to take her word for it and should be kept neutral.

### N24 — Two sealed sorts of matter

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Which of them is right about whether any machine in that universe can carry out every calculation its laws allow?* Bram is right. The "two computers together" are not one machine. They are two machines that cannot exchange anything, and calling them one changes nothing. No single thing in that universe can be given any calculation and return its answer: whoever writes the question can use only the computer made of their own sort of matter, and that computer cannot do the other sort's calculations. The universe as a whole can do every allowed calculation somewhere. No machine in it can.
  - *Does Bram's reason explain why or why not?* Yes, largely. The case already states that each computer is missing some calculations the other can do. Bram's point, that nothing can carry a question (or an answer) across from one sort to the other, explains why the two cannot be combined into one machine that does everything. Put together, those two facts are why no machine can. His reason leaves out that answers cannot come back across either, but that is the same point.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Some would side with Asha and treat it as a matter of words: between them, the pair covers every calculation, so why not call it one machine? That view is the main alternative.
- FIX NEEDED: "Why or why not" is awkward, because Bram's reason is offered for "not". Ask instead "Does Bram's reason explain why no machine can?" The case leaves "carry out any calculation" undefined, in particular whether one user must be able to pose any question to the same machine. That is fine if it is deliberate, since the case turns on it, but it should be a deliberate choice.

### N25 — What holds the universe up

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does any of the three explain what holds the universe up?* No. The dog and the single turtle each name a supporter that nothing could ever reach or check, chosen for no reason, and each leaves open what holds that supporter up. The endless turtles answer that question for each turtle in turn, but never for the whole stack, so the original question is only pushed back forever. None says why the universe needs holding up or why that holder could do it.
  - *Do the dog story and the turtle story say different things?* In words, yes: one says a dog and the other a turtle, and they cannot both be true. But the difference makes no difference to anything. Nothing in the universe could ever tell them apart, and neither explains anything the other does not. As accounts of what holds the universe up, they are equally empty.
- CONFIDENCE: high on the first question. Medium on the second, where thoughtful people split between "different claims, but the difference is idle" and "no real difference, since a difference nobody could ever detect is a difference only of words".
- FIX NEEDED: The first question assumes that the universe needs something to hold it up. A careful respondent may want to reject that assumption, so the case should allow it (for example, "Does any of the three explain what, if anything, holds the universe up?").

### N4 — The tilt and the midnight sun

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *When she proposed it, did her idea already account for the midnight sun, or did it come to account for it only once someone worked out the connection?* Already. The midnight sun follows from a tilted axis on a spherical Earth, and she held both of those ideas. Working out the connection changed nothing in the idea. It only revealed what the idea already implied, much as proving a theorem reveals what the axioms already imply.
  - *Had anyone, she included, explained the midnight sun before someone worked out the connection?* No. Nobody had heard of the midnight sun, and nobody had drawn the consequence, so no one had explained it. The explanation was sitting in her idea waiting to be drawn out, but no person had yet done so.
- CONFIDENCE: medium on the first question. Some would say an idea accounts for something only once someone has shown that it does, so her idea came to account for the midnight sun only when the connection was worked out. Medium-high on the second, though a few would say she had explained it without knowing, since the explanation was in her idea.
- FIX NEEDED: (1) "Account for" can mean "has it as a consequence" or "has been shown to cover it", and the case turns on that difference. Either define it or ask both versions. (2) The case does not say whether the connection was worked out before or after the travellers found the midnight sun, that is, whether it was a prediction or an explanation after the fact. That matters for the second question and should be stated. (3) Offering only two options in the first question may lead respondents to pick one of them.

### N9 — Dark moths

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Is there anything in the moths from which an observer could tell the colour of the bark in their district?* Yes: their wing colour. In the smoky district most moths are dark, and in the clean district most are pale, so looking at the moths tells you the bark colour.
  - *If so, where is it, what holds it, and how did it get there?* It shows in the wings of the population as a whole. Any single moth might be one of the odd ones, so it is the proportion of dark to pale that tells you. It is held in what the moths inherit, their genes for wing colour, which have become common or rare in each district. It got there by selection over many generations. Birds ate more of the moths that stood out against the bark, so moths that matched the bark lived to breed more often and passed their colouring on. No moth knew anything and nobody planned it. The mismatched ones were simply eaten more.
- CONFIDENCE: high. Some would answer "in the wings", others "in the genes", and others "in the population, not in any one moth". These are compatible, and a careful answer mentions all three.
- FIX NEEDED: "Anything in the moths" does not say whether it means each moth or the population. One moth is weak evidence and the population is strong evidence, so the case should say "in each moth, or in the moths of a district taken together". "What holds it" is vague: the wings, the genes and the population are all reasonable answers.

### N14 — The walking robot

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *What accounts for the robot walking well on the rocky ground: Dr Ferreira's design, the computer's search, or both?* Both. Her design supplied the body and a set of commands that already hold real knowledge of balance and leg geometry, and she chose the test courses. The search found the particular combination of those commands that works: her own program fell over within a few steps on rock. Neither would have produced the result alone.
  - *If both, in what share?* No fixed percentage can honestly be given, because each part is useless without the other. It is like asking how much of a rectangle's area is due to its width. It is a fair guess that much of what carried over to rock, which the search never tested on, comes from the general knowledge built into her commands and body. But slopes and small obstacles are parts of rocky ground, so what the search selected for may also have carried over.
  - *How could you tell?* By experiments that take one part away. Run the same search with a crude language (raw motor settings instead of "step over" and "veer left"), and see whether it still produces a robot that copes with rock. Run her language through the search on flat ground only. Test several different evolved programs on rock. Read the evolved program to see whether it is mostly her commands used in sensible combinations.
- CONFIDENCE: medium on "both". Low on any share. One alternative, which thoughtful people could hold, is "mostly her design": the search only tuned choices within a language she built, and only on courses she chose. Another is "mostly the search", since her own program failed and the search's program works.
- FIX NEEDED: "In what share?" assumes that the contributions add up and can be split. That leads respondents toward giving a percentage, so ask instead "Can the credit be divided, and if so how?". "Accounts for" is slightly vague: it could mean what causes the walking or where the knowledge came from.

### N18 — Two footbridges on opening day

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *On opening day, did each designer meet a problem, meaning something he or she must now understand and fix?* Yes, both did. Each has a bridge that sways badly under a crowd and must be made safe, and neither can fix it reliably without finding out why it sways. Rhea must find what her theory missed. Dov must find out, for the first time, how his borrowed design actually behaves.
  - *Did the swaying go against what each designer expected?* Yes, for both. Rhea expected her bridge to stay steady and Dov expected his to be trouble-free.
  - *Did it contradict anything either of them had reason to expect?* It contradicted what Rhea had reason to expect. Her worked-out calculations said the bridge would stay steady under a crowd this size, and it did not, so something in her theory or its use is wrong. It did not contradict anything Dov had good reason to expect. The old bridge's record covered only a few dozen people at a time, so it gave no reason to expect a copy to stay steady under a large crowd. He expected more than his evidence supported, without knowing that he did.
- CONFIDENCE: high on the second question and medium-high on the first. A few would say Dov faces only a practical fix and not something to understand, since he could stiffen the bridge by trial until it stops swaying. Medium on the third: some would say that eighty trouble-free years were an ordinary, reasonable basis for Dov's expectation, from what he knew, so the swaying did contradict something he reasonably expected.
- FIX NEEDED: "Had reason to expect" can mean "had grounds he knew of" or "his evidence actually supported". The Dov half of the third question turns on that, so say which is meant. State plainly that the opening-day crowd on Dov's bridge was larger than any the village bridge had carried; at present it is implied only by "large crowd". The definition of "problem" in the first question merges "understand" and "fix", which lets a respondent answer yes for Dov on the fix alone.

### N21 — The order of adjectives

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does Ngozi know the rule?* Yes, in the sense that matters for speaking. She follows it without fail in sentences she has never heard, which she could not do from memory of phrases alone. She knows it without being able to state it, much as a cyclist knows how to balance without being able to give the physics.
  - *Was her grasp of it handed to her, or did she form it herself?* She formed it herself. What she was given were examples of other people's speech, never the rule. The rule is not in any single sentence she heard, and she worked it out, without noticing, well enough to apply it to new cases. The materials came from other people, and probably an inborn readiness for language helped, but the grasp of the rule is her own construction.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. On the first question, a minority would say that knowing a rule means being able to state it, so she has a skill that conforms to the rule but does not know it. On the second, some would say the rule was handed to her by her speech community (she absorbed a convention that already existed), and others would say it is largely inborn.
- FIX NEEDED: "Know" can mean knowing how or being able to state, and the first question should either say which or ask both. "Handed to her, or did she form it herself" is a two-way choice that leaves out a third answer many would give, that the rule is largely inborn, and it also blurs "the rule came from others" with "her grasp of it came from others". Ask instead "Where did her grasp of it come from?".
