## Cases

### N1 - The seasons and the sun god

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** Asked why there are seasons, Tomas explains that the Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space while the Earth travels round the sun, as a spinning body keeps its axis steady. For half the year the northern half of the Earth leans towards the sun: the sun's rays strike it more directly and the days are longer, so it warms. Half a year later it leans away, the rays strike it at a slant, and it cools. Each piece can be checked on its own: a lamp warms a card that faces it more than a card tilted away from it, and a spinning top keeps its axis pointing one way. The same tilt also tells how high the noon sun will stand at each time of year. Then Tomas adds one sentence: "And the sun god, who approves of this arrangement, keeps the tilt steady." He says nothing more about the sun god, and the rest of what he says is unchanged.

**Question.** Does Tomas explain why there are seasons? Is the sentence about the sun god part of what explains them?

**Verdict.** Yes, Tomas explains the seasons. No, the sun-god sentence is not part of what explains them. Strike it out and the account works exactly as before. The steady tilt is already accounted for by the point about spinning bodies. Nothing about the seasons, and nothing anyone could check, depends on whether the god exists or approves. The sentence is an unsupported belief riding along with a good explanation, not a part of it.

**Confidence.** High (A1 high; A2 high).

**Source.** Adapted from Deutsch, Ch.1 "The Reach of Explanations", pp.24–25.

**Fix before use.** None essential.
- A1: "The rest of what he says is unchanged" gives away that the sentence is idle, but the case needs that fact stated. Keep it.
- A2, optional: the sentence gives the god the same job the spinning-top point already does (keeping the tilt steady). A reader may therefore take it as a rival cause that makes the account slightly inconsistent. Both readings give the same verdict. For a purely idle add-on, give the god no job the account already covers, e.g. "And the sun god looks on and approves."

### N2 - The myth amended

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** A storyteller tells the old Greek myth of the seasons. Hades, god of the underworld, carried off Persephone. Her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, won her release, but on terms that bind Persephone to go back to Hades for part of every year. While Persephone is away, Demeter grieves and makes the world cold and bare so that nothing grows: that is winter. When Persephone returns, Demeter rejoices and the world grows warm again. A sailor then tells the storyteller that in lands far to the south, summer comes at the very time the Greeks have winter, and winter when they have summer. The storyteller changes the myth: when Demeter grieves, she does not put out the warmth of the world but sends it south across the sea, and when Persephone comes back, Demeter calls the warmth home. The changed myth agrees with everything the sailor reported, north and south.

**Question.** Does the changed myth explain the seasons in both the north and the south?

**Verdict.** No. The changed myth agrees with the sailor's report only because it was altered to fit it. Nothing in the story of Demeter's grief says the warmth should go south rather than vanish or go anywhere else. Had the sailor reported something different, the storyteller could as easily have changed the story another way. What the myth now says about the south is the sailor's report retold in the myth's terms. It gives no reason why the seasons there are as they are.

**Confidence.** Medium-high (A1 high; A2 medium-high).
- A2: some would call the changed myth "an explanation, just a false and patched one". That is a difference over the word "explain", and on neither reading does the myth say why the seasons are reversed in the south.
- A1: a reader can answer "no" merely because the myth is false, which is not what the case is asking.

**Source.** The myth: Deutsch, Ch.1 "The Reach of Explanations", pp.19–20. The myth set against the southern seasons: pp.19 and 24–25. The storyteller's change is made for this set.

**Fix before use.** Both authors want to reword the question, but their rewrites separate different things.
- A1 keeps the reader from answering "no" just because Demeter is not real: "Leave aside that we know Demeter is not real. Judged as the storyteller's account, does the change make the myth explain the southern seasons, or does it only make the myth agree with them?" Expected answer: it only makes the myth agree.
- A2 separates offering an account from succeeding: "Does the changed myth give the real reason the seasons are reversed in the south? Is it a better explanation of the seasons than the original myth was?"

A1's wording sets the myth's falsity aside, and A2's "real reason" brings it back in. A round should pick one.

### N3 - Persephone at home

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** In ancient Greece a farmer asks why winter comes every year. He is told the myth of Persephone. Hades, god of the underworld, carried her off; her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, won her release on terms that bind Persephone to go back to Hades for part of every year. While she is away, Demeter grieves and in her grief makes the world cold so that nothing grows. Consider the myth only as it stood for the Greeks. Every land they knew had its winter at the same time of year, and they knew of no other lands. The myth agrees with all of that: winter comes once a year, at the same season, in every land they knew. Nobody has ever seen Persephone leave or return, or seen Demeter grieve.

**Question.** Does the myth explain why winter comes every year? Is it an explanation of winter?

**Verdict.** In form, it is an explanation of winter, an attempt at one: it says what brings winter about and why it comes once a year, namely the bargain that sends Persephone back each year. But it does not explain why winter comes. It is false. Even as the Greeks had it, nothing counted in its favour except winter itself, since nobody ever saw any of the events it describes. Fitting every fact the Greeks had is not enough. Its details (which gods, why grief brings cold) could be swapped for others that would fit just as well.

**Confidence.** Medium (A1 medium; A2 medium). Both authors name the same main alternative: "For the Greeks it did explain winter. It was their explanation, and by what they knew it explained winter." Both also note that a smaller group would say the myth is only a story and not an explanation at all.

**Source.** Deutsch, Ch.1 "The Reach of Explanations", p.19 (the myth) and p.24.

**Fix before use.** Both authors: the two questions use "explain" in two senses without saying so. One sense is offering an account of the cause; the other is giving the real reason. The merged split, with expected answers:
- (a) "Does the myth say what brings winter about and why it recurs every year, so that it is an attempt at an explanation?" Yes (both).
- (b) "Does it actually explain why winter comes?" No (A2's wording; both verdicts give this answer).
- (c) "Judged only by what the Greeks knew, did a careful Greek have good reason to think it gives the real reason winter comes?" No (A1). A2 offers (c) as optional and gives no answer.

A2 adds that the last sentence ("Nobody has ever seen...") leans towards "no". That is fair, but the case should be read knowing it is there.

### N4 - The tilt and the midnight sun

**Status.** DISPUTED. The authors agree in substance. The case is disputed under the rule because author 2 marks low confidence on the wording.

**Situation** (as sketched; author 2 judged this text). Over two thousand years ago, an astronomer on a Mediterranean island proposes that the Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space as the Earth goes round the sun. She proposes it to explain one thing: why the noon sun stands higher in the sky in summer than in winter. Put together with what was known about heat and about spinning bodies, it also explains the seasons she knows. She and everyone she knows have seen only the lands around the Mediterranean, and nobody has heard of the far north or the far south. It follows from her idea, though nobody at the time works it out, that far enough north the summer sun never sets, that near the middle of the Earth there are no seasons, and that far to the south the seasons are reversed. Centuries later, travellers find all three.

Author 1 judged a changed text. It adds "She knows the Earth is a sphere" and changes "there are no seasons" to "the seasons are slight".

**Question.** When she proposed it, did her explanation already explain the midnight sun of the far north, before anyone had seen it or thought of it? Or did it come to explain it only later, when someone worked out the connection or when travellers found it?

**Author 1's verdict** (confidence: medium).
> Her idea already accounted for the midnight sun when she proposed it. Nothing had to be added to it, and the midnight sun was a consequence waiting to be worked out. Working out the connection revealed that the idea covered it, and the travellers confirmed it. Neither changed what the idea says. What is true is that she did not explain the midnight sun, since she had never thought of it. The idea covered it; she did not.
>
> CONFIDENCE: Medium. Main alternative verdict: "Something is explained only once someone connects the explanation to it. Before that, the idea explained what it was used for, and the midnight sun was an unknown consequence of it, not something it explained."

**Author 2's verdict** (confidence: medium, and low on the wording).
> Her idea already contained the explanation of the midnight sun when she proposed it: the midnight sun follows from the idea as she stated it, and nothing had to be added later. But nobody had explained the midnight sun until someone worked out the connection. The travellers added nothing to the explanation; they only confirmed what the idea already implied. So the idea had it from the start, and the explaining, as something people do, came when someone worked it out.
>
> CONFIDENCE: medium, and low on the wording. Thoughtful people split on whether "explain" belongs to ideas or to people. The main alternative: "It came to explain the midnight sun only when someone worked out the connection; before that it was only true that it would explain it." Few would say it waited on the travellers.

**What it turns on.** The two verdicts say the same thing. The idea already had the midnight sun as a consequence when it was proposed. Working out the connection revealed it, and the travellers only confirmed it. Nobody, she included, had explained the midnight sun until the connection was worked out. Both authors also give the same alternative: something is explained only once someone connects the explanation to it.

The question uses "explain" of ideas ("did her explanation already explain") and of people ("when someone worked out the connection") in one breath. Whether the answer is "already" or "later" depends on which use the reader takes. That is why author 2 marks the wording low.

**Proposal.** Rewrite. Split the question as both authors propose. The first part uses author 1's wording, which asks about "accounting for" rather than restating the situation's "it follows from". Expected answers:
- (a) "When she proposed it, did her idea already account for the midnight sun, or did it come to account for it only once someone worked out the connection?" Already (both).
- (b) "Had anyone, she included, explained the midnight sun before someone worked out the connection?" No (both).
- (c), optional: "Did the travellers' finding add anything to the explanation?" No, it only confirmed it (both).

**Fix before use.**
- A1: add "She knows the Earth is a sphere". The midnight sun follows from the tilt only for a round Earth, and the Greeks knew the Earth was round.
- A1: change "there are no seasons" to "the seasons are slight". Near the equator there is still some seasonal change, often wet and dry seasons.
- A1: "It follows from her idea, though nobody at the time works it out" states the key fact outright and pushes towards "already". That is fair, since the case needs the fact. Keep it.
- A2: "nobody has heard of the far north" is a made-up condition, since Greeks of that era did have travellers' reports from the far north. It does no harm as fiction, but do not present it as history.

**Source.** Deutsch, Ch.1 "The Reach of Explanations", pp.26–29.

### N5 - A farmer's rule carried south

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** In a village on the northern shore of the Mediterranean, farmers follow a rule handed down for generations: "Sow the wheat after the first autumn rains, in October, and it ripens in early summer, in June." Nobody in the village knows why it works. It has held year after year, and fields sown at other times have done worse. A family from the village settles in a land far south of the equator. There the seasons fall in the opposite months: the cool rains begin in April and May, and the hottest months are December and January. The father means to farm by the village rule.

**Question.** What, if anything, does the village rule tell him about when to sow and when his wheat will ripen in the new land?

**Verdict.** Little or nothing he can rely on. At home, "after the first autumn rains" and "in October" were one instruction, but in the new land they point to different times.
- Read by its months, the rule misleads. October there is spring, wheat sown then would grow up into the hottest months, and "ripens in June" would now mean midwinter.
- Read by its seasons, it points to sowing in April or May and expecting a harvest around December.

The rule itself cannot tell him which reading to trust, because nobody knows why it works. To choose, he needs what the rule leaves out: why it worked (wheat grows through the cool, wet months and ripens as the weather turns warm and dry), or else trial. The authors differ only in emphasis. A2 calls the seasons reading "a sensible first guess". A1 says only an understanding from outside the rule could tell him to sow in April or May. Both agree that the rule alone cannot settle it.

**Confidence.** Medium-high (A1 high; A2 medium-high). Alternative (A2): "It tells him to sow after the first autumn rains, which there means April or May, so the rule carries over well enough."

**Source.** Made for this set.

**Fix before use.**
- A1, optional: say whether the new land's climate is otherwise like home (wet winters, dry summers). As written, a reader must assume it is, or conclude that the rule says even less.
- A2: the new land is described so that the rains cue lines up neatly with the right sowing time, which hints at the intended answer. That is acceptable if the case is testing which part of an unexplained rule carries over.
- A2: "What, if anything" is open-ended and makes verdicts hard to compare. Suggested questions, with the answer both verdicts give:
  - "If he sows in October, as the rule's months say, should he expect ripe wheat in June?" No.
  - "If he sows after the first cool rains, can the rule itself tell him that this will work?" No.

### N6 - Two astronomers and the unknown world

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** Two astronomers on a Mediterranean island, over two thousand years ago, hold that the tilt of the Earth's axis causes the seasons: the axis leans and keeps pointing the same way in space, so each half of the Earth leans towards the sun for part of the year and away from it for another part. Neither has any evidence about places beyond Europe, North Africa and the nearer parts of Asia. The tilt idea, taken as it stands, implies strange things about places far beyond: days and nights six months long in the far north, and seasons reversed in the far south. Each astronomer states the idea with a limit. Iras says: "The tilt causes the seasons in the Mediterranean and the lands around it. I make no claim about anywhere else." Leon says: "The tilt causes the seasons in the Mediterranean and the lands around it; everywhere else on Earth, the seasons also come at the same times of year as they do here." Each statement agrees with everything either of them has seen or heard.

**Question.** Does Iras explain the seasons in the Mediterranean? Does Leon?

**Verdict.** Iras explains the seasons in the Mediterranean. Leon, as he states it, does not give a coherent explanation.

Iras gives the real cause at home and only declines to say what she has not checked. That is caution, not a flaw in her account of home, though her silence cannot stop the tilt having consequences elsewhere.

Leon names the same cause at home but then says the seasons everywhere else come at the same times as here. The tilt itself rules that out for the far south, unless something he never mentions overrides it there. He keeps the cause while denying what it must do, so his account as a whole contradicts itself. Only the part he shares with Iras, taken alone, is right.

**Confidence.** Medium (A1 high for Iras, medium for Leon; A2 medium).
- Both authors give this alternative for Leon: "He explains the home seasons as well as Iras; his claim about elsewhere is an extra mistake that does not touch his account of home."
- A2 adds a second alternative: "Leon explains nothing even at home, because his second clause shows he doesn't really take the tilt to be the cause."

**Source.** Adapted from Deutsch, Ch.1 "The Reach of Explanations", pp.26–28. Iras's statement is made for this set. The sketch's source line says "Leon's statement is the book's", which both authors found to be wrong. See below.

**Fix before use.** Both authors independently found the same material mismatch with the source. Both report the book's modified theory (Deutsch, Ch.1, p.27) as: "In the known world, the seasons happen at the times of year predicted by the axis-tilt theory; everywhere else on Earth, they also happen at …" That states only when the seasons come, not what causes them. The sketch's Leon asserts the cause and then contradicts what it implies, which is a different test. A round must choose between two versions:

1. **Keep the sketch's Leon** (the case above). Correct the source line to: "Leon's statement is adapted from the book's (p.27); the book's version gives only the timing, not the cause."
2. **Use the book's case.** Rewrite Leon's line as both authors propose: "In the Mediterranean and the lands around it, the seasons come at the times of year the tilt idea predicts; everywhere else on Earth, they also come at those same times of year." Both authors expect the verdict then to be firmer, with high confidence: Iras explains the home seasons; Leon does not, even at home, because he gives a timetable, not a cause. Neither author wrote this version out as a finished case, so judge it as one before adopting it.

### N7 - Two bakers

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** Two bakers in the same town each explain why their bread dough rises. Yeast in the dough feeds on sugars in the flour and gives off gas; the gas is trapped in the stretchy dough, which swells. Maya adds: "This holds when the dough is kept between 18 and 35 degrees." She found those limits by experience and says nothing about why they are where they are. Bea gives the same explanation with the same limits, and adds why: below about 18 degrees the yeast works so slowly that the dough hardly rises in the usual time, and above about 35 degrees the yeast begins to be harmed, and nearer 50 it dies. Between those temperatures both doughs rise just as described.

**Question.** Does each baker explain why her dough rises?

**Verdict.** Yes, both do. The dough rises because yeast gives off gas that the stretchy dough traps, and both bakers say so. Maya's limits are an honest statement of where she has seen it work. Leaving them unexplained does not weaken her explanation of the rising itself: within the range, she has the reason right. Bea explains something more, namely why the rising works only within that range, so her account is fuller.

**Confidence.** High (A1 high; A2 high). A2 notes a minor alternative, "Maya's explanation is less complete", which is true but does not change the answer.

**Source.** Made for this set.

**Fix before use.** None (A1, A2).

### N8 - The machine that runs forever

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** At a fair, an inventor shows drawings of a wheel that would keep turning and driving a water pump forever, with no fuel, no wind, no flowing water and nobody pushing it. Visitors ask why no such machine exists. Pia answers: "Nobody has found the right design yet. Hundreds of designs have been tried, and every one has come to a stop. Perhaps someone will find one that works." Quentin answers: "A machine that keeps doing work with nothing going into it would be making energy out of nothing. The energy anything gives out must be matched by energy put in or used up somewhere. That holds for any design, including ones nobody has tried."

**Question.** Does each answer explain why there is no machine that runs forever without fuel?

**Verdict.** Only Quentin's does. Pia reports that every attempt so far has failed and hopes one may yet work. That is evidence plus a hope, but no reason, and it leaves open that the next design might succeed ("nobody has found it yet" could be said of anything not yet invented). Quentin gives a reason that holds for every possible design, tried or not. The pump's work would have to come from somewhere, and nothing is supplying it, so the machine would be making energy out of nothing.

**Confidence.** High (A1 high; A2 high). A2 notes a nitpicking alternative: Pia explains why no machine has been built so far, on the false assumption that one could be. Even so, she does not explain why none exists.

**Source.** Marletto, Ch.2 "Beyond Laws of Motion?", p.61; Ch.6 "Work and Heat", p.167.

**Fix before use.** None.
- A1: keep "driving a water pump" in any rewrite. A wheel doing no work could in principle spin for a very long time.
- A2: do not narrow the question to "why can't one be built?", which would tip it towards Quentin.

### N9 - Dark moths

**Status.** DISPUTED. Both authors mark low confidence. They do not disagree with each other.

**Situation** (as sketched). A species of moth rests by day on tree bark. In a district where smoke has blackened the bark, the moths change over many generations until most of them have dark wings. Pale moths stand out on the dark bark and birds eat more of them, and moths get their wing colour from their parents. In a clean district nearby, where the bark is pale, most moths of the same species are pale. No moth has any idea of the colour of the bark or of its own wings, and nobody planned the change.

**Question.** Is there knowledge of the colour of the bark anywhere in this story? If so, where is it, and what holds it?

**Author 1's verdict** (confidence: low).
> In the everyday sense, no. Nothing and no one in the story knows the bark's colour: not the moths, and nobody planned anything. In the looser sense biologists often use, yes. The moths' inherited make-up (the wing-colour genes, as they are now spread through the dark-district population) has come to match the dark bark, and it matches because the bark is dark, not by accident. That match is carried down the generations in the moths' heredity, not in any moth's mind. Whether to call it "knowledge" is a choice about how widely to use the word.
>
> CONFIDENCE: Low. Thoughtful people split mainly over the word. One side says there is no knowledge here, only a match produced by birds eating the conspicuous moths. The other says there is knowledge of the bark, held in the population's genes. A third point: the birds see the bark when they hunt, which a reader may count as the birds knowing its colour. This case may be set aside.

**Author 2's verdict** (confidence: low).
> In the ordinary sense, no: nobody in the story knows the colour of the bark, whether moth, bird or person. In a looser sense, yes: the moths' inherited colouring now matches the bark, and that match is carried in their heredity, the genes passed from parents to offspring across the population. Birds put it there by eating the moths that didn't match, so you could read the colour of the bark off the moths. Whether to call that "knowledge" or just a match produced by selection is a choice of words, not a fact about the moths.
>
> CONFIDENCE: low. Thoughtful people will split on the word. One camp says "yes, knowledge, held in the moths' genes"; the other says "no, a match or an adaptation, but knowledge needs a knower".

**What it turns on.** Both authors give the same substance:
- In the everyday sense, nothing in the story knows the bark's colour.
- In a looser sense, the moths' inherited colouring (the genes, spread through the population) matches the bark and carries that match from one generation to the next.
- Whether to call the match "knowledge" is a choice of words.

### N20 - Keeping count with string

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** Two groups of goatherds keep count of their flocks with string. Nobody can measure string exactly: every measurement is off by up to a tenth of an inch. In the first group, as each goat leaves the pen in the morning, a herder reels out a length of string equal to the length of that goat; in the evening, as each goat returns, its length is reeled back in, and when all the string is back, all the goats are home. In the second group, each goat stands for exactly one inch of string, and after every goat the herder trims or lengthens the string to the nearest whole inch. Both groups also add flocks together by joining strings, and split flocks by cutting a string in two.

**Question.** Can each method keep count of a flock of any size, through any number of such joinings and splittings? What, if anything, limits it?

**Verdict.** Only the second method can.

Past some number of goats or operations, a stretch of leftover or missing string might be a goat or might just be error. The first method also tracks the flock's total length rather than its number (A1, A2), and cutting a length string does not tell you which goats went where (A1).

In the second, each error is under a tenth of an inch and is wiped out by rounding to the nearest inch after every step. Errors never build up, and the count stays exact through any number of steps. Only practical things limit it: enough string and time, and handling very long strings. It also relies on no single measurement ever being off by as much as half an inch (A2).

**Confidence.** High (A1 high; A2 high).

**Fix before use.** None essential.
- A1, optional: "keep count" lets readers dismiss the first method merely because it records length, not number. Ask instead: "Can each method reliably tell whether all the goats are home, for a flock of any size, through any number of joinings and splittings?"
- A2: say how the second group tallies goats in and out (an inch reeled out for each goat leaving, an inch reeled in for each goat returning).
- A2: say whether the tenth-of-an-inch limit holds however long the string gets.

### N21 - A rule she cannot state

**Status.** DISPUTED. Both authors mark low confidence on the second question.

**Situation** (as sketched). Ngozi is a native speaker of English. She says "a lovely little old green French knife" and would never say "a green French little old lovely knife". She puts adjectives in this order in sentences she has never heard before, and gets it right every time. Asked why, she cannot state any rule; the other order "just sounds wrong". Nobody ever taught her such a rule; she picked it up as a small child from hearing people talk.

**Question.** Does Ngozi know the rule? Did learning it involve creating anything?

**Author 1's verdict** (confidence: high on the first question; low on the second).
> Yes, she knows it, without being able to say it. She applies it reliably to phrases she has never heard, which is more than remembering examples. On the second question: since nobody stated the rule to her, she must have worked it out for herself, without noticing, from the examples she heard. So yes, in the sense that her mind built something that wasn't handed to her ready-made. It was not new to the world, since the rule was already in the language around her.
>
> CONFIDENCE: High on the first question. Low on the second. Main alternative verdict: "Nothing was created. Children pick up their language's patterns by an inborn capacity, and 'creating' is the wrong word for absorbing a pattern that is already there." Some would also deny that following a pattern without being able to state it counts as knowing a rule. The second half may be set aside.

**Author 2's verdict** (confidence: medium on the first question; low on the second).
> Yes, she knows the rule in the sense that matters for speaking: she follows it reliably, even in sentences she has never heard, although she can't put it into words. Learning it did involve something like creating. Nobody gave her the rule, only examples, and to get new cases right she must have formed the general pattern herself from what she heard. Whether to call that "creating" or "picking up" is partly a matter of words; what is clear is that the rule was never handed to her.
>
> CONFIDENCE: medium on the first question (some insist that knowing a rule means being able to state it); low on the second.

**What it turns on.**
- **First question.** Both authors say yes: she knows the rule, in that she follows it reliably in new cases, though she cannot state it. Neither marks this low. A2 notes that some insist knowing a rule means being able to state it.
- **Second question.** Both lean yes: nobody gave her the rule, only examples, so she must have formed the pattern herself. Both add that it was not new to the world. Both mark this low because it turns on the word "creating". The word could mean making something new to the world, or building something oneself rather than receiving it ready-made. A1 names the alternative that picking up a pattern by an inborn capacity is absorbing, not creating.

**Proposal.** Rewrite the second question. Both authors give nearly the same wording: "Since nobody ever stated the rule to her, where did her grasp of it come from: was it handed to her, or did she have to form it herself from what she heard?" Expected verdict from both: she formed it herself from the examples she heard, "with whatever inborn aptitude for language children bring" (A2). Keep the first question. Optionally split it as A2 proposes: "Does she know how to follow the rule? Does she know what the rule says?" With the second question rewritten, the case would likely become AGREED, but it must be judged afresh.

**Source.** Adapted from Deutsch, Ch.16 "The Evolution of Creativity", p.405; see also p.412.

### N22 - Denying the inner experience

**Status.** DISPUTED. Both authors mark low confidence on the Yuri half.

**Situation** (as sketched). People commonly report that there is something it is like to see blue: an inner experience over and above the light entering the eye and the word "blue". Two theorists deny that any such inner experiences exist. Wren says only: "There are no inner experiences. People are mistaken about this." Yuri says: "There are no inner experiences. When people look inwards, they are consulting memories of what they have just perceived, and the machinery that does this evolved to report inner experiences that never took place. People have false memories of experiences, not experiences."

**Question.** Has either theorist explained why people seem to have inner experiences?

**Author 1's verdict** (confidence: high for Wren; low for Yuri).
> Wren has not. Saying people are mistaken gives no reason why they make this mistake, and the same move could deny anything. Yuri has at least offered an explanation of why people believe and report that they have inner experiences: false memories produced by machinery that evolved to report them. It is thin, though. He does not say why such machinery would have evolved, or how a false memory of seeing blue differs from seeing blue. Many would say that "seeming to have an experience" is itself an experience, so his account leaves the very thing in question unexplained.
>
> CONFIDENCE: High for Wren. Low for Yuri. Thoughtful people disagree sharply over whether his account explains the seeming or only the reports. Main alternative for Yuri: "Yes, he explains it. The seeming just is the false memory, and he says where that comes from." The Yuri half may be set aside.

**Author 2's verdict** (confidence: high for Wren; low for Yuri).
> Wren has not: saying people are mistaken gives no reason why they seem to have such experiences, and it could be said of anything. Yuri has at least offered an explanation (people consult memories, and the machinery that does so evolved to report experiences that never happened), but it is thin and disputed. He gives no account of how a false memory of seeing blue could seem exactly like having seen it, which was the puzzle in the first place, nor of why such machinery would evolve. So Wren has not explained it, and Yuri has made an attempt that is not, as it stands, convincing.
>
> CONFIDENCE: high for Wren; low for Yuri. This is a live dispute: those who hold Yuri's view say it does explain the seeming, and critics say it only restates the puzzle. Thoughtful people disagree, so the Yuri half may need to be set aside.

**What it turns on.**
- **Wren.** Both authors agree, with high confidence, that Wren has not explained it. Saying people are mistaken gives no reason why they make the mistake, and the same move could deny anything.
- **Yuri.** Both say he has at least offered an explanation, but a thin one that is not convincing as it stands. He does not say why such machinery would evolve, or how a false memory of seeing blue differs from, or could seem exactly like, seeing blue. Both mark this half low. It turns on "seem". If seeming to have an experience is itself an experience, Yuri's account leaves unexplained the very thing in question. If it is a belief or report, he has an answer. Both say the Yuri half may be set aside.

**Proposal.** Rewrite, and set part aside. Split the question as both authors propose, using A1's wording for (a), which avoids "seem":
- (a) "Does either theorist give any reason why people would believe and say that they have inner experiences?" Expected answer from both: Wren no, Yuri yes. A2 rates this high.
- (b) "Is Yuri's reason a satisfying account of why seeing blue seems to be like something?" Both authors call this contested. Set it aside.

Use (a) only.

**Source.** Deutsch, Ch.7 "Artificial Creativity", pp.153–154. Yuri's view is the one the book attributes to Daniel Dennett.

### N23 - The planets run backwards

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** Priya, an astronomer, knows exactly where each planet is tonight and how fast it is moving. The laws of motion work equally well run forwards or backwards in time, so she calculates where every planet was on this date a thousand years ago, and old records match her figures. She says: "The planets were where they were a thousand years ago because they are where they are tonight." Her colleague starts from the old records, calculates tonight's positions just as exactly, and says the reverse.

**Question.** Does tonight's arrangement of the planets explain where they were a thousand years ago? Does their arrangement a thousand years ago explain where they are tonight?

**Verdict.** The arrangement a thousand years ago, together with the laws of motion, explains where the planets are tonight. Tonight's arrangement does not explain where they were a thousand years ago. It lets Priya work that out and is excellent evidence for it, but it did not bring it about. The planets reached tonight's places by moving from their earlier ones; they were not where they were because of where they would later be. Priya's "because" is the "because" of "I know it rained, because the street is wet", not that of "the street is wet because it rained". She has confused a way of calculating, and a reason to believe, with a cause.

**Confidence.** Medium (A1 medium; A2 medium). Both authors give the same main alternative: "Since the laws run equally well both ways, neither arrangement explains the other any more than the reverse. Each only lets you calculate the other, and what explains both is the laws together with the history of the solar system." Both note that people who think hard about time-symmetric physics hold this view.

**Source.** Marletto, Ch.2 "Beyond Laws of Motion?", pp.58–59; Deutsch, Ch.5 "The Reality of Abstractions", p.118.

**Fix before use.** None essential (A1).
- A1, optional: to test whether calculating counts as explaining, add "Is there any difference between what Priya claims and what her colleague claims?"
- A2: the sketch has Priya stress that the laws run both ways, which invites the symmetric answer. That is fine as a test.
- A2: "does X explain Y" may be read as "can Y be worked out from X", and on that reading both answers are trivially yes. Suggested wording: "Is tonight's arrangement part of the reason the planets were where they were a thousand years ago? Is the arrangement a thousand years ago part of the reason they are where they are tonight?"

### N24 - Two sealed sorts of matter

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** Imagine a universe with two sorts of matter. Each sort can make atoms, machines and living things, but the two pass through each other without effect: nothing made of one sort can push, see, signal to or record anything made of the other. People made of the first sort have built a computer that, given enough time and memory, can carry out any calculation their sort of matter allows. People made of the second sort have built one that can carry out any calculation theirs allows. Some calculations can be carried out only in the first sort of matter, and some only in the second. Asha says: "Take the two computers together, and this universe has a machine that can carry out any calculation its laws allow." Bram says: "No machine in this universe can do that. A question written in one sort of matter can never be read by a computer made of the other."

**Question.** Which of them is right about whether any machine in that universe can carry out every calculation its laws allow? Does Bram's reason explain why or why not?

**Verdict.** Bram is right. His reason, together with the stated fact that some calculations can be carried out only in one sort of matter, explains why. "The two computers together" are not one machine; they are two machines that can pass nothing between them. A question has to be written in one sort of matter, so it can be given only to the computer made of that sort. The calculations only the other sort can do can never be applied to it, and no machine can join the two, since nothing of one sort can touch the other. Asha is right only in a weak sense: between them the two computers cover every calculation. But nobody in that universe can hand an arbitrary question to the pair and get it done.

**Confidence.** Medium (A1 medium; A2 medium-high). Both authors give the same alternative, that the dispute is partly about words. If "a machine" may be any collection of devices, and each question is posed to whichever computer suits it, Asha is right in that weak sense. A1 adds that this needs a user who can write in both sorts of matter, which the story rules out.

**Source.** Marletto, Ch.3 "Information", pp.93–95.

**Fix before use.**
- Both authors: say what counts as a machine that can carry out any calculation, which settles the verbal dispute. A1: "a single machine to which any question, however it is written, can be given, and which then carries out any calculation the laws allow on it". A2: "take a question from whoever poses it and give them back the answer".
- A2: the second question is garbled. Suggested: "Does Bram's reason explain why no such machine can exist?"
- A1: readers may stumble over the idea that some calculations are possible only in one sort of matter. One sentence would help, such as "the two sorts obey different laws, so each can do things the other cannot".
- A2: Bram's reason works only together with that stated fact. The case states it, so nothing needs to change there.

### N25 - What holds the universe up

**Status.** AGREED.

**Situation.** Three cosmologies each say what holds the universe up. The first: the universe rests, forever unmoving, on the head of a dog that stands outside it; the dog is eternal and will never change. The second is the same in every respect, except that it says a turtle. The third: a turtle holds the universe up, that turtle stands on another turtle, and so on down without end. In all three, nothing inside the universe can reach, see or affect whatever holds it up.

**Question.** Does any of the three explain what holds the universe up? Do the dog story and the turtle story say different things?

**Verdict.** None of the three explains what holds the universe up. The dog and the single turtle are arbitrary picks that could be swapped for any other creature. Since nothing inside the universe can reach or see what holds it up, no observation could ever favour one over the other. Each also leaves the same question about the holder: what holds it up? The endless stack of turtles only passes that question down forever, so it is never answered. All three also take for granted that the universe needs holding up at all, and none gives a reason to think so.

Do the dog and turtle stories say different things? In words, yes, since a dog is not a turtle. But nothing anyone could see, do or predict depends on which is true. As explanations they make the same empty claim, that something outside holds the universe up, and the difference between them is idle.

**Confidence.** High on the first question; medium on the second (A1 and A2 alike). On the second question, some would say flatly that the stories say different things, since one could be true and the other false. Others would say that two claims no possible observation could tell apart do not really say different things.

**Source.** Marletto, Ch.2 "Beyond Laws of Motion?", pp.47–48 and 55; Deutsch, Ch.8 "A Window on Infinity", p.174.

**Fix before use.**
- A1, minor: the question presupposes that the universe needs holding up, and a careful reader will want to reject that. Optionally add: "Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the universe does need something to hold it up."
- A2: split the second question into "Do the two stories make different claims?" (yes) and "Does the difference matter to anything either story explains or predicts?" (no).
