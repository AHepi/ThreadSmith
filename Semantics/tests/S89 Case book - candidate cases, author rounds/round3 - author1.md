### N1 — The seasons and the sun god

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q1 (Does Tomas explain why there are seasons?): **Yes.** He gives the tilt, says the axis stays pointing the same way, and shows how that gives more direct sun and longer days to one half of the Earth for half the year, and less for the other half. Each step can be checked with a lamp or a top, and the same idea also predicts how high the noon sun stands. That is a real explanation, and one extra sentence does not undo it.
  - Q2 (Is the sun-god sentence part of what explains them?): **No.** Delete it and nothing else changes: the seasons, the day lengths and the noon heights come out exactly the same. The steadiness it puts down to the god is already accounted for by the spinning-top point. It is an idle add-on and does no work.
- CONFIDENCE: High for both. A religious listener might say a god is the ultimate cause of everything. Even so, most would agree that this sentence adds nothing to the explanation of the seasons.
- FIX NEEDED: "keeps the tilt steady" repeats the job the spinning-top point already does. So a reader could take the sentence as a rival account of the steadiness, not as pure decoration. If the case is meant to show an idle add-on, have the god do something that plainly plays no part (e.g. "the sun god watches over the seasons"). If it is meant to show a redundant rival cause, say so. Also, Q1 could be read as "is his whole speech, sun god included, an explanation?" Consider "Setting aside the last sentence, does Tomas explain...?" or asking both versions.

### N2 — The myth amended

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q (Does the changed myth explain the seasons in both the north and the south?): **No.** The storyteller has only bent the story to fit the sailor's news. Nothing in Demeter's grief would have led anyone to expect reversed seasons in the south, and the story could just as easily have been bent any other way (warmth sent east, or into the sea). It now agrees with the facts, but it gives no reason why things have to be that way. It also never explained the northern seasons, beyond telling a story about them.
- CONFIDENCE: High in the sense of "does it really explain". Medium if "explain" is read loosely: some people would say a myth "explains" the seasons in the sense that it offers an account of them, and on that reading the answer is "it offers an account, but a false and made-up one".
- FIX NEEDED: "Explain" can mean "offers an account" or "gives a real reason". Say which one is meant, or ask "Does the changed myth give a good reason why the seasons are reversed in the south?" The question also packs two questions into one: whether the original myth explained the northern seasons, and whether the change adds an explanation of the southern ones. Consider splitting them. "The changed myth agrees with everything the sailor reported" is fine but slightly leading: it invites the reader to judge on fit alone.

### N6 — Two astronomers and the unknown world

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q1 (Does Iras explain the seasons in the Mediterranean?): **Yes.** She gives the right cause and how it works. Refusing to say anything about lands she knows nothing of is caution, not a weakness in her account of the Mediterranean. Her idea still implies things about far places, and she simply does not spell them out.
  - Q2 (Does Leon?): **Only partly. His account of the Mediterranean is undermined.** For the Mediterranean he says the same words as Iras. But the tilt belongs to the whole Earth, and when the north leans towards the sun the south must lean away. So his added claim, that seasons everywhere come at the same times, denies what his own cause must do. Either he has not understood the idea he is using, or he is quietly saying it does not work the way he says. A listener would trust his grasp of the Mediterranean seasons less because of it.
- CONFIDENCE: Iras high. Leon medium, and thoughtful people could split. The main alternative: "Yes, Leon explains the Mediterranean just as Iras does; his extra sentence is a separate wrong guess about other places and does not touch what he says about home."
- FIX NEEDED: Leon never says what makes the seasons come at the same times elsewhere. His claim can be read as "the tilt works differently there", which contradicts his idea, or as "some other, stronger cause takes over there", which is odd but not a strict contradiction. Say which is meant, or ask whether Leon's statement as a whole hangs together. "Explain" is also ambiguous between "states a correct account of the Mediterranean" and "holds and understands a coherent explanation". The second is where the two astronomers differ. "Each statement agrees with everything either of them has seen or heard" is accurate but steers readers towards "evidence cannot tell them apart", so keep it only if that is intended.

### N8 — The machine that runs forever

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q (Does each answer explain why there is no machine that runs forever without fuel?):
    - **Pia: No.** She reports that every attempt has failed, and even suggests the right design might still be found. A list of failures gives a reason to doubt such a machine will be built, but not a reason why it cannot be. Her answer treats the lack of such a machine as bad luck.
    - **Quentin: Yes.** He gives a reason that covers every possible design: work coming out needs energy going in, and a machine with nothing going in cannot keep giving work out. That tells you why all the designs failed and why untried ones will fail too.
- CONFIDENCE: High for both. A few might say Quentin only states a rule without saying why the rule holds. Most would still count "because it would have to make energy from nothing" as the explanation.
- FIX NEEDED: The question says "runs forever without fuel", but some things do keep moving without fuel (planets, or a wheel spinning freely with no friction). What cannot exist is something that keeps *doing work*, here driving the pump, with nothing going in. That is exactly what Quentin's answer addresses. Word the question to match: "...why there is no machine that keeps driving the pump forever with nothing going into it?" Also, "Does each answer explain" asks for two answers in one. Consider asking about Pia and Quentin separately.

### N12 — Consent by rephrasing

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q (Does each patient understand the risks of the knee operation?):
    - **Mr Okafor: Not as far as anyone can tell. Probably not.** He can repeat the words, but when the same point is put another way he can only repeat them again. He shows no sign of being able to use what he has heard. A careful person would stop short of "definitely not": he may be nervous, or may have taken the questions as a request to repeat the list. Either way, the surgeon should not treat him as having understood.
    - **Ms Varga: Yes.** She links a new symptom to the right risk and knows what to do about it. She also works out which risks go with any operation and which one is tied to operating on the leg. That is what understanding a list looks like, as opposed to memorising it.
- CONFIDENCE: Ms Varga high. Mr Okafor medium-high: people would agree he has not *shown* understanding, but some would say you cannot conclude he lacks it from two questions.
- FIX NEEDED: The title "Consent by rephrasing" is leading and brings in consent, which the question does not ask about. The calf question tests outside medical knowledge (that a leg clot shows up as a swollen, sore calf), which is not on the list the surgeon read. So a patient could understand the list and still miss it. The elbow question is the cleaner test. The question also mixes "understands" with "has shown understanding". Consider "Does each patient show that they understand the risks?", or add something that settles Mr Okafor's state of mind (e.g. he says he does not see what the calf question has to do with it).

### N15 — The miscopied notes

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q1 (Does Salma now have the teacher's explanation?): **Yes.** She can put it in her own words, it hangs together, and the teacher confirms it is exactly what he taught. The copying mistake did not stop her getting it; she caught the mistake.
  - Q2 (In coming to have it, did she create anything herself?): **Yes, in a modest way.** Nobody told her the word should be "more". She worked it out by seeing that "less" made the balloon sink, which contradicts the rest of the notes. That correction, and her own way of saying the idea, are her own work. She did not invent the explanation, though. It is the teacher's, and what she made was her own understanding of it and the fix to the copy.
- CONFIDENCE: Q1 high. Q2 medium, and thoughtful people could split. The main alternative: "No, she only fixed an obvious slip and got back what the teacher already had. Recovering something is not creating it."
- FIX NEEDED: "Create anything" is vague: a new idea, a new explanation, a correction, or her own grasp of the idea? The answer changes with the reading. Name what is being asked about, e.g. "Did she come up with any part of the explanation herself?" Q1 is also slightly ambiguous: she already "had" the explanation in the sense of holding a copy of the notes. "Does Salma now understand the teacher's explanation?" would be clearer. The case would sharpen if it said whether the slip was easy to spot, since an obvious typo makes the "she created something" reading weaker.

### N19 — The novelist's two demands

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - Q1 (Did Odile have a problem before anyone read the chapter?): **Yes.** She saw that two things she wanted pulled against each other at this point in the book: the description she wanted for its language would stall the story where it most needed to move. A writer does not need a bored reader to have a problem; seeing the clash in advance is exactly how good writers catch it.
  - Q2 (Does either draft solve it?): **The second does; the first does not.** The first gets rid of the clash by dropping one of the two things she wanted, which is giving something up, not solving the problem. The second keeps both by changing how the valley is shown: seen by the heroine as she runs, it stays rich and the chapter still moves fast.
- CONFIDENCE: Q1 high. Q2: high for the second draft. Medium for the first. The main alternative: "The first draft also solves it, at a price: it solves the pacing problem by sacrificing the lyrical passage."
- FIX NEEDED: "Problem" could mean her own clash of aims or a flaw in the finished chapter. Q1 is clearest if it asks about the first. "Before anyone read the chapter" slightly signals the expected answer. Q2 asks two questions in one: ask about each draft separately. The second draft also changes the plot (the heroine now flees across the valley), and the case says nothing about whether that fits the story Odile wants. Stating that "the language stays rich, and the chapter moves fast" makes the second draft an easy yes. That is fine if intended, but it means the case tests only whether giving something up counts as solving.
