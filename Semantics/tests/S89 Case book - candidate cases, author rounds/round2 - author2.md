Independent verdicts for the fourteen round-2 cases. Written from the situations and questions alone, as a careful, fair-minded, well-informed person would answer them, without any theory of explanation. The format follows the case book: title, then verdict, confidence and fixes.

### N3 — A myth about winter

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does the myth explain why winter comes every year?* No. The myth gives a cause, but the yearly return is simply written into the terms of Persephone's bargain. That is the fact of the yearly winter restated as a divine agreement, not a reason for it. Swap in another god, another grievance or another bargain and the story would fit the Greeks' facts just as well, and nothing in it could ever be checked. It is also false.
  - *Is it an explanation of winter?* Yes, but only in form. It is an attempt at an explanation, a mythical one, and it served the Greeks as one, but it is a bad and false one. Fitting every fact the Greeks knew is not enough to make it a real explanation, because it was built to fit them.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Thoughtful people could split. One main alternative is that, given everything the Greeks knew, it did explain winter *for them*, so the first answer would be a qualified yes. The other is that it is not an explanation at all, only a story, so the second answer would be no.
- FIX NEEDED: "Is it an explanation of winter?" can mean "an attempted explanation" or "a genuine, successful explanation", and the answer flips with the reading. Asking two nearly identical questions hints that they should get different answers, which is leading. "Nobody has ever seen Persephone leave or return" pushes the reader towards testability. It may be intended, but it tilts the case.

### N13 — The parrot in the lecture hall

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Has the parrot acquired Professor Marsh's ideas?* No. It has acquired some of his sentences as sounds, the way a tape recorder would. It cannot answer questions about them or do anything with them, and it would have copied recipes just as readily. The ideas are not its own in any sense.
  - *What part did the parrot play in the student's learning them?* It was a carrier, like a recording or a notebook. It kept the professor's words intact and passed them on. All the understanding was done by the student, who worked out what the sentences meant, and the parrot added nothing of its own.
- CONFIDENCE: high. There is a minor split on the first question: some would say the parrot "holds" the ideas in stored form the way a book does, but it does not have them.
- FIX NEEDED: "acquired" can mean "stores the wording of" or "understands". Say which is meant, or accept both readings.

### N16 — The domino that never falls

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does each answer explain why that domino never falls?* Answer B does: the domino never falls because 641 has no divisors and the domino only falls when one is found. That tells the observer why the waves never reach it, and it would let him predict what happens with other inputs. Answer A is true but hardly explains anything. As worded, it comes down to "it never falls because nothing ever knocks it over", which is the observer's own question restated in terms of dominoes. It gives no idea why no wave ever reaches that domino.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Thoughtful people could split. The main alternative is that both answers explain at different levels: A is a correct mechanical explanation and B a higher-level one, and B is simply more illuminating.
- FIX NEEDED: Answer A is a one-line summary ("in sum, none of the patterns ... includes it"), not a full trace of the dominoes' motion. That makes it close to a tautology and pushes the verdict towards "A does not explain". If the case is meant to set a complete mechanical account against a high-level one, give A as the full trace (as N17 does) and say so. "Both answers are true" is fine.

### N17 — The transistor at the end of the run

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does Answer A tell the asker anything that Answer B does not?* Yes, a vast amount: the exact physical path, the state of every electron at every moment, and a demonstration that the physics really did produce this outcome. B simply assumes the hardware worked as designed. In practice nobody could read A, but the information is in it.
  - *Does Answer B tell the asker anything that Answer A does not?* Yes. B says what the process was: finding the factors of 15. It also says why this transistor matters: it holds part of the answer, 3 and 5. A never mentions numbers, factoring or answers. You could not get any of that out of A without already knowing how the machine encodes numbers.
  - *Does each explain why the transistor is on?* B does, in a form the asker can understand and use. A accounts for it only in a thin, technical sense. It is a complete and correct derivation of the outcome from the starting state, but no one could take it in, and even a reader who did would not learn why this transistor ended up on rather than some other one. Both are "explanations" in some sense, but only B answers the question as the asker meant it.
- CONFIDENCE: high on the first two questions, medium on the third. On the third, thoughtful people could split two ways. Some say A is a full explanation in principle and simply impractical. Others say A is not an explanation at all, only a derivation or prediction.
- FIX NEEDED: "tell the asker" can mean "contains the information" or "conveys it to a human who reads it". Since A fills libraries, the two readings give different answers to the first question, so say which is meant. On the second question, someone may argue that A "implicitly contains" everything B says. The question should make clear whether that counts as telling.

### N20 — Keeping count with string

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Can each method keep count of a flock of any size, through any number of joinings and splittings?* The first group's method cannot. Every measurement adds up to a tenth of an inch of error, and these errors pile up with every goat, every join and every cut. With a big enough flock, or enough operations, the accumulated error grows larger than a small goat, and the string can no longer show whether a goat is missing. The second group's method can. Each error is well under half an inch, so rounding to the nearest whole inch after every step removes it before it can build up. The string stays exactly as many inches long as there are goats, however large the flock and however many joins and cuts are made.
  - *What, if anything, limits it?* The first method is limited by accumulated measurement error compared with the size of the smallest goat. It also never records a number at all, only a total goat-length. The second method is limited only by practical things: the amount of string, space and time. It also depends on no single error ever reaching half an inch and on the herders not blundering. If measurement error grew with string length in real life, very long strings would eventually defeat it too.
- CONFIDENCE: high.
- FIX NEEDED: "keep count" fits the first method poorly, because it tracks total goat-length rather than a number of goats. Splitting a flock under the first method is also underspecified: where do you cut? Say whether the tenth-of-an-inch bound holds however long the string is. The whole verdict on the second method depends on that.

### N22 — Denying the inner experience

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does either theorist give any reason why people believe and say that they have inner experiences?* Wren gives none. She only says people are mistaken, with nothing about where the mistake comes from. Yuri gives one, though it is incomplete. He says the belief comes from introspection consulting memories, done by machinery that reports experiences that never happened. That names a source for the belief. But he gives no reason why such machinery would have evolved to report non-existent experiences, and the notion of a "false memory of an experience" arguably brings back what it denies.
- CONFIDENCE: high for Wren. Medium-high for Yuri. The alternative is that Yuri merely relocates the mystery ("the machinery reports experiences that never occurred" is the error redescribed), so neither really gives a reason.
- FIX NEEDED: Only "any reason" is asked, so Yuri's answer is a trivial yes, and the contrast between a one-line denial and a detailed one gives the answer away. If the case is meant to test whether Yuri's reason is a good one, ask that directly.

### N23 — The planets tonight and a thousand years ago

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does tonight's arrangement of the planets explain where they were a thousand years ago?* No. Tonight's positions let Priya work out where the planets were, so they are evidence of the past and a way to calculate it. But the planets were not where they were *because* of tonight, since tonight could not have made the past what it was.
  - *Does their arrangement a thousand years ago explain where they are tonight?* Yes, together with the laws of motion: the planets got here from there. It is a thin explanation, because it only pushes the question back a thousand years, but it runs in the right direction.
- CONFIDENCE: medium-high. The alternative is that, because the laws run equally well both ways, the two cases are exactly symmetric: either both count as explanations, or neither does (the real explanation being gravity plus the history of how the solar system formed).
- FIX NEEDED: "arrangement" can be read as positions only, but positions alone determine nothing. Say "positions and motions" in both questions. Also make clear that "explain" means more than "lets one calculate". Priya's "because" invites the reader to decide exactly that, which may be the point, but the word "arrangement" should not carry an extra ambiguity.

### N24 — Two sealed sorts of matter

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Which of them is right about whether any machine in that universe can carry out every calculation its laws allow?* Bram. Two computers that cannot pass a single question or answer between them are not one machine. No one in that universe, being made of one sort of matter, could use the pair to carry out any calculation the laws allow. Asha is right only in the weaker sense that every allowed calculation can be done by *some* machine somewhere in that universe.
  - *Does Bram's reason explain why or why not?* Largely, yes. Because nothing crosses between the two sorts of matter, the two computers cannot be joined into one, and a question can never reach the computer able to answer it. On its own, though, the reason is not enough. If both sorts allowed the same calculations, each computer alone would already do everything. Bram's reason explains the outcome only together with the stated fact that each sort allows calculations the other cannot do.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Thoughtful people could split. The main alternative is that Asha is right, because between them the two computers cover every allowed calculation, and the dispute is only about the word "machine".
- FIX NEEDED: "machine" is undefined, and whether two non-interacting computers count as one machine decides the case. Say whether the question means "one device a single user could set any allowed calculation" or "every allowed calculation can be done somewhere". Also say explicitly that no calculation needs both sorts of matter at once, since none can exist.

### N25 — What holds the universe up

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does any of the three explain what holds the universe up?* No. The dog and the single turtle are interchangeable and could never be checked, and nothing about them does any work. The endless tower of turtles never reaches anything that holds itself up, so it puts off the question forever instead of answering it. All three also take for granted that a universe needs holding up.
  - *Do the dog story and the turtle story say different things?* In words, yes: a dog is not a turtle, and both stories cannot be true. But the difference makes no difference to anything anyone could ever observe or infer. It is an idle difference, not one that matters.
- CONFIDENCE: high on the first question. Medium on the second: some would say that in every sense that matters the two stories say the same thing, so the answer is no.
- FIX NEEDED: The first question presupposes that something holds the universe up. The second question's "say different things" can mean literal content or any difference in consequences, and the answer flips with the reading.

### N4 — The tilt and the midnight sun

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *When she proposed it, did her idea already account for the midnight sun, or did it come to account for it only once someone worked out the connection?* It already did. With the round Earth she already accepted, the midnight sun followed from her idea from the start. Working out the connection revealed that consequence; it did not add it.
  - *Had anyone, she included, explained the midnight sun before someone worked out the connection?* No. Explaining is something people do, and no one had drawn the connection. No one she knew even knew there was a midnight sun to explain. Her idea contained an explanation that nobody had yet given.
- CONFIDENCE: medium. Thoughtful people could split on the first question. Some hold that an idea accounts only for what has actually been derived from it, so before that it accounted for the noon sun and the seasons only. A minority would say on the second question that she had in effect explained it without knowing.
- FIX NEEDED: The two questions use different verbs ("account for" and "explained"), and "anyone, she included" hints that the idea and the person should be judged differently, which is leading. It is also unclear whether one can "explain" something nobody knows exists. Say whether "explained" means "gave the explanation" or "held an idea that explains it".

### N9 — Dark moths

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Is there anything in the moths from which an observer could tell the colour of the bark in their district?* Yes: their wing colour, or more precisely the proportion of dark to pale moths in the district. A single moth is weak evidence, because a pale moth can turn up on dark bark, but the population as a whole is a reliable guide.
  - *Where is it, what holds it, and how did it get there?* It shows in the wings. It is held in the moths' inherited make-up, the factors (genes) that set wing colour and pass from parent to offspring, spread across the population rather than located in any one moth. It got there through selection. Birds found and ate the moths that stood out more often, so moths matching the bark left more offspring, who inherited their colour, and over many generations the matching colour became common. Nobody put it there, and no moth knows it.
- CONFIDENCE: high on "yes", on where it shows, and on how it got there. Medium on "what holds it", where reasonable answers include the wings, the genes and the population as a whole.
- FIX NEEDED: "what holds it" is ambiguous between the individual moth, its hereditary material and the population. "Leaving aside the birds' eyesight" could be misread as saying the birds must be left out of how it got there. Make clear that it only means not counting what is in the birds' or people's heads as the thing in question.

### N14 — The walking robot

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *What accounts for the robot walking well on the rocky ground: Dr Ferreira's design, the computer's search, or both?* Both. Neither would have done it alone. Her own program fell on rough ground, and without her body, sensors and commands the search would have had nothing to vary. The search chose how to combine her commands, and it only ever optimised for her test courses. So the fact that the result copes with unfamiliar rocky ground suggests that much of what makes it work there comes from her commands, which were built from real knowledge of balance and legs. The skills the search found on slopes and obstacles may also carry over to rocks.
  - *If both, in what share?* There is no meaningful percentage, because the two contributions work together rather than adding up, the way a rectangle's length and width both make its area. The most one can say is a rough lean: the success on terrain the search never saw points more towards the design than towards the search.
  - *How could you tell?* By controlled comparisons. Run the same search with a bare, low-level command set (raw motor settings) and see whether the result still handles rocky ground. Try her own hand-written programs in her language on the rocks. Repeat the search from different random starts and check whether every result generalises. Test on many more kinds of new terrain. Look inside the evolved program to see whether it relies on her "step over" and "veer" commands in ways her geometry makes sensible.
- CONFIDENCE: medium on "both". Low on the share, where thoughtful people split between "mostly design, the search only tuned it" and "mostly the search, since her own program failed", and many would refuse to give a share at all.
- FIX NEEDED: "in what share" invites a percentage that probably has no meaning. The case does not say how much the evolved program differs from her original or how heavily it uses her higher-level commands, and without that the share question cannot be answered. "Unlike any of the test courses" does not say in what respects, which matters for judging whether the search's skills carry over.

### N18 — Two footbridges on opening day

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *On opening day, did each designer meet a problem, meaning something he or she must now understand and fix?* Yes, both. Each bridge sways badly under a crowd and must be fixed, and each designer needs to find out why. Rhea needs to find where her theory went wrong. Dov never knew why his design worked in the first place.
  - *Did the swaying go against what each designer expected?* Yes, both. Rhea expected her bridge to stay steady under crowds of this size, and Dov expected his to be as trouble-free as the original.
  - *Did it contradict anything either of them had reason to expect?* For Rhea, yes. Her calculations gave her real grounds to expect steadiness under a crowd no larger than this, so the swaying shows a flaw in her theory or her calculations. For Dov, not really. The village bridge's record covered only a few dozen people at a time, and he did not even know that, so it gave him no reason to expect a large crowd to be safe. He expected it, but he had no reason to.
- CONFIDENCE: high on the first two questions. Medium-high on the third: some would say eighty trouble-free years gave Dov a weak general reason to expect success, and the swaying goes against that.
- FIX NEEDED: The case says Rhea's crowd was within her calculations but does not say outright that Dov's crowd was larger than anything the village bridge ever carried. State it so the third question does not rest on inference. Q1's definition of "problem" is stipulative. It is fine as long as it is meant to be.

### N21 — The order of adjectives

- THOUGHTFUL PERSON'S VERDICT
  - *Does Ngozi know the rule?* Yes, tacitly. She knows it the way someone knows how to keep their balance on a bicycle: she follows it reliably, even in phrases she has never heard, which shows she has more than a store of remembered phrases. She does not know it in the sense of being able to state it.
  - *Was her grasp of it handed to her, or did she form it herself?* She formed it herself, from examples. Nobody gave her the rule, only speech that happened to follow it. The examples she heard cannot have included most of the phrases she now builds correctly, so her own mind worked out the pattern. In a weaker sense the rule is inherited: it is her community's convention, which she rebuilt rather than invented, and her ability to rebuild it rests on capacities she was born with.
- CONFIDENCE: medium-high on the first question. The alternative is "she follows the rule but doesn't know it", from people who reserve "know" for what can be stated. Medium on the second: some would say she simply absorbed it from the speech around her, so in effect it was handed to her.
- FIX NEEDED: "handed to her, or did she form it herself" is a false choice: examples were handed to her and the rule was rebuilt by her. "Does Ngozi know the rule" is ambiguous between knowing that (being able to state it) and knowing how. Say whether tacit knowledge counts.
