### 1. How error correction is handled now

**What corrects an account that fails.**
- [TEXT] Standing is fixed by the world, not by anyone's acceptance, on every pair of the contract, tested or not. See realism (D3:L67). (F1), (F2) and (A) quantify over every pair of C (D3:L233–251), and "Every conjunct is a condition on how supplied relations behave under the changes in C" (D3:L265).
- [TEXT] A failed test refutes the conjunction of theory, background and the claim that the test did what was intended, "and nothing narrower" (K3, D3:L389). File 12 adds "which conjunct fails is a further question" (F12:L411).
- [TEXT] The failed claim stays failed: "A proposition indexed to a contract remains that proposition … A new index is a new claim" (D3:L363). A narrowing after a failure "is a new claim at a new index" (D3:L159). Changing the index without recording it is goalpost-moving, "a failure of the record" (D3:L602).
- **[CHANGED: label and source.]** [FOLLOWS] A patched account is a different candidate. A candidate is the tuple (E,p,t,Γ) (D3:L231), and "A changed rule is a changed component" (D3:L103; F11:L105). [TEXT] The text says so outright only for a component reassigned after a deletion, which "belongs to a new candidate … and the new candidate's success is not the old one's" (D3:L307).
- [TEXT] Detection and response. A violation is defined for every transport to the simulation layer, and surprise for a selected one outside its history. The two responses are a selection response (re-tune within the population) and a construction response (D3:L217–225). A represented failure of a claimed obligation is a "recognized difficulty" that opens a critical episode (D3:L423). Derivation 10 is the worked example (D3:L620).
- [TEXT] Repair. Claimed obligations O and protected obligations P are "fixed for the comparison", and a protected condition must hold "on every occasion it covers" (D3:L429–435). Created knowledge (EK) needs an account on the contract fixed at e_c, and "A later narrowing … does not retroactively satisfy (EK)" (D3:L447).
- [TEXT] Criticism is held to the same test as explanation: it bears only if its own connection is an account of the defect (K1, D3:L371–375). "A record reconstructed from the claim it is meant to support is not a receipt for that claim" (D3:L391).
- **[CHANGED: added.]** [TEXT] What a question asks is fixed by its query and the shape of its contract. "A production question has a Q that reads an output port and a C containing interventions on upstream ports". A measure with a reliable prediction "answers the identification question; whether the measured part also produces the outcome is the production question, and the first answer is not the second" (D3:L151; F11:L153).

**What this constrains.**
- **[CHANGED: "every part" narrowed to the active parts.]** [FOLLOWS] The content of any patch. Every active component (every commitment in Γ) must match its anchor on every change of its contract. (F1) is part by part: "(F1) prevents an assembled match from hiding a wrong decomposition" (D3:L245). Background parts, meaning boundary values and components that assign inputs (D3:L231), are held only through the assembled organization (F2), the answers (A) and non-circular dependence. No part may carry the answer (D3:L255).
- [FOLLOWS] The record. Every change of question is a new, recorded claim, and the old verdict stands.
- [FOLLOWS] Repairs. They are measured against obligations declared first, so a fix that trades away a protected condition fails (P).

**What it leaves open.**
- [TEXT] Which of theory, background or test to blame (K3; F12:L411).
- [TEXT] A stated narrowing after a failure. It is recorded, and "the semantics … supplies no rule that certifies it". Meeting (E) on it "does not certify the restriction as appropriate". With no stated ground, a verdict that turns on appropriateness has "a missing declared input" (D3:L159), so that verdict is unsettled (D3:L516).
- [TEXT] A choice between candidates the contract does not tell apart: "the contract does not contain the distinction … The remedy is a finer contract, which is a new question" (D3:L562; F11:L558).
- [TEXT] Idle parts, which are tolerated: "(E) has no condition that each commitment do work … it grades nothing" (D3:L313).
- **[CHANGED: "nothing marks a save as having no reason" was too strong.]** [FOLLOWS] Nothing marks a *part* changed to fit a failure as having no reason behind it. For a *narrowing* with no stated ground, draft 3 marks only that the verdict on its appropriateness is unsettled (D3:L159, L516). It does not mark the save as absorbing the failure. Nothing reads a run of saves: O1 ("the series as a whole is a retreat") is SILENT because W32(c) was left out (PL:L274).
- **[CHANGED: the missing lemma described correctly.]** [TEXT] There is no lemma that unbounded composition at a fixed accuracy grade needs a correction step (W42, left out, PL:L274). AU obs 14 finds correction "present for ideas and missing for computation".

### 2. The crux: can an idle or newly added adjustable part save a failed account unnoticed?

Three situations. The first two change a part; the third changes the question as well.

#### Situation 1. The saint and the Saturday bread (an idle part given a job)

*A baker explains why her bread sells out by noon on market Saturdays: the market brings crowds past the door. She adds, "and Saint Honoré watches over the shop." Nothing else depends on the saint. One market Saturday, a third of the bread is left at closing. She says: "The saint was displeased that week." Now the saint's mood decides the sales, and the only sign of the mood is the unsold bread.*

- **Before the patch.** [TEXT] The saint does no work: adding or removing it leaves every support a support, so "a candidate carrying d then meets (E) exactly when it meets (E) without d" (D3:L313). [FOLLOWS] As an idle part it could not have rescued anything. Deutsch p.24 says the same of the sun god: "no story about how anyone felt would put it right".
- **The patch** is a changed component, so it makes a new candidate (above).
- **Does it pass (E)? No.** Every component is assigned an anchor ("λ assigns each component of E a subnetwork of D", D3:L189). The mood part is active, since it now decides the sales. If the mood is tied to nothing real, its relation differs from its anchor's, and (F1) fails (D3:L233–236). If it is tied to the sales themselves, the target's answer appears "as a component", and non-circular dependence fails (D3:L255; F11:L257). Both failures hold on any contract that contains the failed Saturday, however narrow. Putting the mood in the background as a boundary value does not help: then the active sales part reads the mood port, and that port still needs an anchor.
- **Index:** no change of question, so there is nothing to record beyond the new candidate.
- **Provenance:** constructed or declared makes no difference to (E), which has no provenance conjunct (D3:L261). A declared patch could not ground (EK) or a Part XV (A) counterexample (D3:L530; F11:L528).
- **Repair:** she may repair an agreement obligation for that Saturday, but (EK) needs an account (D3:L437), so no knowledge is credited.
- **Criticism:** "the mood is read off the sales" bears (K1), since its connection is an account of the defect that non-circular dependence names. The mood's only record is the unsold bread read as displeasure. That is a record reconstructed from the claim, and so "not a receipt" (D3:L391).
- **Result: blocked, by (E) itself.** [FOLLOWS] If she ties the saint to something real, such as a rival stall that opened that week or rain, the label drops out ("None inspects a label", D3:L265; Derivation 1), and the case becomes Situation 2 or 3.

#### Situation 2. The north bed, with the question left open (a new adjustable part)

*A gardener explains why the tomatoes on the south wall ripen first each year: the wall gets the most sun. It has held for four summers. This year the north bed ripens first. She adds a part: "in a wet spring the north bed ripens first", and this spring was wet. (In fact the neighbour who shares the north bed planted an early variety there this year, and rain has nothing to do with it.) The new part fits all five summers.*

- **Contract.** **[CHANGED: marked as a reading, with the alternative.]** [INFERENCE] She states no scope. Non-vacuity requires "every physically admitted edit excluded from C" to be "excluded by a stated scope, not silently" (D3:L257). So either C holds every physically admitted change, or C excludes some of them silently and non-vacuity fails. A second reading is possible: the scope a claim states is a declared input (D3:L516, L518), so a reader could call it missing and the verdict unsettled (D3:L528). On neither reading is the patched account certified.
- **Does it pass (E)? No, on the first reading.** **[CHANGED: separating pairs.]** [FOLLOWS] (F1) is checked part by part at every pair. Two pairs separate rain from the variety. At a wet spring with the usual planting, the rain part says north first and the world says south. With the early variety planted in a dry spring, the rain part says south first and the world says north. So (A) and (F1) fail, and the patched account is not an account. (03's pair, "soak the north bed", separates them only if τ reads soaking a bed as a wet spring.)
- **But nothing in the record marks it.** [FOLLOWS] All five tested summers fit. The verdict is true as a fact about the world, but nothing in the theory lets an assessor who holds only those summers say it. Deutsch would reject the patch before any test ("rejected out of hand without any experiment", D p.25), because "rain" could as well be "the late frost" or "the new compost".
- **[CHANGED: added.]** [FOLLOWS] **The one way it passes.** Suppose the transport anchors her "wet spring" to the early variety rather than to rain, and τ leaves it untouched by watering. Then every pair fits, and the account passes. But that account is the true one under a wrong name (Derivation 1), not a save. Her words anchor "wet" to rain.
- **Index:** same question, same index. [FOLLOWS] Every after-the-fact rule applies to indices, declared inputs, candidate identity or the record, never to the content of a part (01, A8).
- **Provenance:** constructed after a recognized difficulty (D3:L423). That is the normal route to Origin and (EK), not a mark against the patch (D3:L399–417).
- **Repair:** take O = "say which bed ripens first" and P = "keep the four earlier summers right". The patch repairs O and keeps P, so (P) holds for that agreement obligation (D3:L429–435). Protected obligations block only trade-off fixes, and this is not one.
- **Criticism:** [TEXT] "(E) does not exclude a true mechanism guessed for bad reasons; the reasons … are assessed elsewhere (Part IX)" (D3:L277). So "you fitted it to one spring" names no defect under (E). A criticism that supplies the separating change bears if it is right (Grievance 4, D3:L43).
- **Receipts:** the wet spring is a genuine record, and no rule discounts the data a part was fitted to (D3:L391).
- **Non-circular, non-vacuity:** both met. Rain is an independent condition, and deleting the part loses the contrast.
- **Result: blocked as a fact, unmarked in the record.** [INFERENCE] On a question left open, the fitted patch fails because it is unfaithful, not because it is easy to vary. Its swaps (frost, compost) fail the same way, so the set of variants that stay accounts (Pres) is small.

- [TEXT] Derivation 4: "A system can be surprised only if it holds a transport selected on a history H strictly smaller than the contract C" (F11:L570). Its consequence: "The two responses, extend H and re-tune, or construct a new transport, are the difference between learning and creating" (F11:L574).
- [TEXT] Derivation 3, qualified: "the value of t at (a,b) is underdetermined by H" only where some co-surviving t′∈T differs there, and "Where T contains no such transport, H is silent on the value at (a,b) and the population fixes it." (F11:L562–564) Draft 3 adds that t′ "must be a member of T, a transport the physics and the stated construction admit (Part XII), and it must survive on H (Part IV)" (D3:L566). It also defines survival (D3:L195): "fidelity on H without membership in T is not survival."
- [TEXT] Derivation 10 is the theory's own worked example of error correction. A selected S₀ is surprised by occlusion. Under a selection response, "If the population's transports can only predict from occupancy, no member survives the extended history: the fidelity failure is structural, not parametric" (F11:L616). A construction response builds S₁. "If it repairs the obligation … while protecting 'predict displacements correctly,' then (P) holds; and since the account is adequate on its contract and deployable, (EK) holds." (F11:L618)
  - Draft 3 drops F11:L616's gloss "and this is Derivation 3's qualification seen from the other side…" (D3:L620; CL:L2207–2214, an erratum).
- [FOLLOWS] In file 11 a *constructed* account that fails a test has no "violation" in the text's terms. It meets its error only through K1 and K3 and an undefined "recognized difficulty". Draft 3 gives it a violation and routes a represented violation into a critical episode. File 12 goes further and calls it surprise.
- [FOLLOWS] Selection is error elimination bounded by the population: a test removes members, and what stays open is only where co-survivors differ. Past the population ("structural, not parametric"), only construction corrects.

##### A5. Critical and creative episodes (Part X)

- [TEXT] "A complete critical episode contains a recognized difficulty, a target available before its criticism, a conjectural objection, and a content-sensitive response. A creative critical episode contains an instance of (G) connected to its inquiry. Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof." (F11:L421; F12:L439 is the same)
- [TEXT] Draft 3 adds (D3:L423): "A **recognized difficulty** is a failure of a claimed obligation, or a conflict in which what the system holds meets a claimed obligation only by failing a protected one (Part XI), when the system represents it."
- [FOLLOWS] In draft 3, a failed test becomes the start of an episode only through the obligations of Part XI: the failure of a *claimed obligation* (for example, "answer correctly at this pair") that the system represents. Error correction is thus routed through declared obligations, not through (E).

##### A6. Repair (Part XI)

- [TEXT] (P): "For claimed obligations O and protected obligations P, fixed for the comparison, Repair_{O,P}(ξ,ξ′;Δ) ⟺ ∃o∈O[¬o(ξ)∧o(ξ′)] ∧ ∀r∈P[r(ξ)⇒r(ξ′)] ∧ ProducedBy(Δ,ξ,ξ′;O)." (F11:L427–430)
- [TEXT] "The obligations are declared inputs: O says what is to be repaired and P what is to be protected, each as a stated condition over stated occasions, and a protected condition is lost exactly when it fails on an occasion it covers. Their declaration makes no claim that the aims are worth pursuing, and (P) does not rank alternatives. Losses outside P must be exposed. ProducedBy holds when an active route (Part IX) runs from Δ to the repair … A correct account that produced nothing, an act that repaired without an account, and a repair produced through use of an account are three different attributions." (F11:L433)
- [TEXT] Draft 3 adds two things.
  - "The contribution Δ of a repair is a subhistory together with the changes of content it makes." (D3:L429)
  - "In (P), accordingly, r(ξ′) says of a protected condition r that it held on every occasion it covers from ξ to ξ′, not only at ξ′." (D3:L435)
- [TEXT] Worth: "Repairing an obligation establishes that it was repaired; it establishes nothing about whether the obligation, or the question that led to it, was worth having." (F11:L447)
- [TEXT] Active routes: "A route that started and did no work, or that was already at rest when the result occurred, is not active for that result" (F11:L371). Draft 3 ties this to Part VI: ProducedBy "credits a repair to the contributions whose active routes ran to it, not to the routes a candidate contains" (D3:L307).
- [FOLLOWS] Repair is a relation between states of a *system* relative to obligations fixed in advance. It is not an operation on an account.
  - A patch that fits a failed test is a Repair of whatever agreement obligation was declared, if it protects P and produced the change.
  - It is a repair of an *epistemic* obligation only through (EK), which needs Account.
  - The anti-trade-off force of (P) is exactly as strong as the declared P.

##### A7. Created explanatory knowledge (EK) and newness

- [TEXT] (EK) needs CreativeCriticalEpisode ∧ Repair_{O,P} ∧ an epistemic o repaired ∧ Origin(s,c,p_c,h,e_c) ∧ Account(c,p_c) ∧ c∈Result(Δ) ∧ Deploy ∧ ProducesVia (F11:L437–442). "The scope of Account(c,p_c) is the contract fixed at e_c. A later narrowing of that contract to rescue adequacy is a new claim at a new index, and does not retroactively satisfy (EK)." (F11:L445) Draft 3 defines Result(Δ) and ProducesVia (D3:L447).
- [TEXT] Newness: New(s,c,h,e) ⟺ ¬∃d∈R_{<e}(s,h), d≡_ℓ c (F11:L405–409). Draft 3 defines the equivalence (D3:L407): "d≡_ℓ c when there are transports from d to c and from c to d, both faithful on c's contract at grain ℓ. The equivalence is structural at the stated grain, not string equality or similarity."
- [TEXT] Build: "Reconstruction by a learner is construction; relay is not. … A small binding newly prepared inside received content is construction of that binding, and the rest of the content keeps its inherited provenance." (F11:L401) "A dimension of variation mentioned in passing is not thereby a port of the account; it becomes one when the account admits changes to it, and adding it is construction." (F11:L417)
- [FOLLOWS] A patch fitted after a failure and built in an owned episode is New, Built and an Attempt, so (G) holds. If it is also an Account on the contract fixed at e_c and repairs an epistemic obligation, (EK) holds. The history of "fitted after the fact" is the normal route to (EK), not a bar to it.
- [INFERENCE] Under draft 3's ≡_ℓ, swapping one idle part for another (both constrain nothing) plausibly gives contents that are structurally equivalent at the grain. The result is not New, so it has no Origin and no (EK). The theory would then be blind to idle variation in newness as well as in (E).

##### A8. Historical index, scope and records: everything that treats "after the fact" differently

- [TEXT] Scope: "An account at a stated scope answers the question asked at that scope; it does not answer a broader question that failed, and a narrowing adopted after a failure is a new claim at a new index (Part VIII). Where the claim states the ground of its restriction, a verdict on the restriction is given with that ground; where it states none and a verdict turns on one, the ground is a missing declared input (Part XIV)."

- [TEXT] Grievance 4: "A contract that quietly excludes physically possible changes to protect an account is not caught by a rule about intentions; it is caught by the requirement that the exclusion be stated (non-vacuity, Part V), and then by any criticism that supplies the excluded change. - [TEXT] Grievance 10: "An assessment is an event with a frozen contract. An episode is a history in which contracts change, and every change carries a provenance record." (F11:L57)

(F11:L309 = D3:L307) Interference: "Success of a subset does not imply success of the whole; b is not made 'not a commitment' to repair this." (F11:L311)

Draft 3 has "the supports assessed are subsets of the written Γ, whether or not anyone has set them out, not a support someone could write with commitments outside Γ" (D3:L299).

  - obligations "fixed for the comparison" (F11:L427);

- [TEXT] The predecessor was more explicit on two points, neither of which is carried word for word into file 11 or draft 3 as far as I found:

  - "It must not be dismissed by inventing a favorable new grain after the fact." (F00:L1380)
- [FOLLOWS] Every "after the fact" rule in file 11 and draft 3 applies to one of four things:

- [FOLLOWS] The historical index gives "a new claim". It does not give "a false claim" or "no explanation". Whatever verdict the new claim gets comes from (E) on its own index.

##### A10. Idle parts and hard-to-vary (Part VI)

- [TEXT] File 11: "**Hard-to-vary.** For explanatory jobs F⊆F′, Pres(F′)⊆Pres(F) … More reach constrains variation; the containment need not be strict; counting jobs is not a warrant." (F11:L315) Also "It does not prefer an elegant account to a less elegant one with the same fidelity." (F11:L279)
- [TEXT] Draft 3, W33.1 (D3:L313): "The **reach** of E is the set of jobs f with Account(E,f); it is fixed by E and the world, not by which jobs anyone has checked. … (E) has no condition that each commitment do work. A commitment d does no work by itself in the candidate when every support stays a support after d is added to it and after d is removed from it: a candidate carrying d then meets (E) exactly when it meets (E) without d, and {d} is critical in no support (B). - [TEXT] Draft 3 types Γ (D3:L231): "The commitments Γ are components of E, those the candidate offers as doing the work, whether or not anyone has described their work".
- [TEXT] Non-circular dependence in draft 3 (D3:L255) adds two things.
  - "Identity of that assertion with the target's answer is structural at the declared grain".

- [INFERENCE] If a candidate has no support (it fails on C), "every support stays a support…" holds vacuously, so every commitment "does no work by itself". W33.1's test tells you nothing about a failed candidate. Idleness must be read on a contract where the candidate is an Account.

#### Part B. Answers

##### (1) An account meets a failed test. What does the theory say happens?

- [TEXT] Standing is fixed on the whole contract and independent of acceptance (F11:L69, L249–253, L267). The test refutes only T∧B∧I (F11:L391). The claim keeps its index (F11:L365, L596–598).
- [FOLLOWS] If the failed pair lies inside C, E is not an Account on C, and never was, provided B and I hold. If it lies outside C, E is still an Account on C and does not answer the broader question (F11:L161).
- [TEXT] What comes next is a history.
  - For a selected transport: violation or surprise, then either a selection response (re-tune within the population) or a construction response (F11:L227).
  - For a constructed one in draft 3: a violation, which "can be a recognized difficulty" (D3:L223, L423) and so opens a critical episode.
- [FOLLOWS] The account is not "repaired" as an object. The system's content changes (Δ, D3:L429). The changed content is a new candidate: F11:L105 and L309 say a changed or reassigned component makes a new candidate "and the new candidate's success is not the old one's". A narrowing is "a new claim at a new index" (L161) and "does not retroactively satisfy (EK)" (L445).
- [FOLLOWS] **The repair is constrained in three ways.**
  1. The new candidate must meet (E) on its own contract. (F1), (F2) and (A) quantify over every pair of that contract, tested or not. So a patch that fits only the failed pair fails wherever the contract holds a change at which the patch's anchor behaves differently. A patch that just writes in the observed answer fails as a table (F11:L271) or under non-circular dependence (D3:L255).
  2. If the repair is a Repair_{O,P}, it must keep every declared protected condition on every covered occasion (F11:L433; D3:L435).
  3. Nothing about indices may change unrecorded (F11:L163, L598).
- [FOLLOWS] It is *not* constrained by any requirement that the new content keep the old parts, be minimal or principled, or be hard to vary (F11:L279, L315; D3:L313). Nor is it constrained on which conjunct of T∧B∧I to revise (F11:L391; F12:L411).

##### (2) What happens to an idle part (draft 3's "does no work") during this?

- [FOLLOWS] **Changing or adding an idle part cannot rescue the account.** By D3:L313, a candidate carrying an idle d "meets (E) exactly when it meets (E) without d". Adding or varying d leaves the verdict where it was.
- [FOLLOWS] **A part that rescues is, by definition, no longer idle.** Suppose d is re-worded into d′ so that the candidate now fits the failed pair.
  - d′ is a changed component (F11:L105), which makes a new candidate (L309's logic).
  - d′ does work on the new contract, since removing it loses the support.
  - d′ is then held to (F1): its relation must equal its anchor's "for every (a,b)∈C" (F11:L235–237).
  - A god who "intervenes where needed" has no such anchor. On any contract with the relevant changes, it fails (F1) or (F2), or it is the answer written in and fails non-circular dependence.
  - A d′ that does track an anchor across the whole contract is, by Derivation 1, of one kind with that anchor on C, "whatever labels anyone attaches". It is a faithful component under another name (F11:L13, L546–550; D3:L119).
- [FOLLOWS] **Would the theory count the result as an Account?** Yes, whenever it meets (E) on its contract, whatever its history and however it was motivated (F11:L279: "does not exclude a true mechanism guessed for bad reasons").
- [FOLLOWS] **What it records.**
  - A new claim at a new index, if the contract changed (F11:L161, L365).
  - A new candidate (L309).
  - The patch's provenance: constructed at e, with a witness (F11:L199, L401, L411–415). This is the route to (G) and (EK), not a mark against the patch.
  - The Part VI profile: which parts are critical, and Pres. Pres "grades nothing" (D3:L313).
- [FOLLOWS] **It records nothing that marks the fit as arbitrary or evasive.** Grievance 4 says so for evasion: it "is not caught by a rule about intentions" (F11:L45). The loose-limit retreat measure W32(c) is left out, and O1 is SILENT (PL:L274).
- [INFERENCE] The real gap sits on thin contracts. On a contract narrowed (and stated) to the tested history, an easily varied candidate and its after-the-fact patch can both meet (E) if neither restates the answer at the grain. Pres is then large, and it grades nothing. On a contract that holds the physically admitted changes, (F1) over untested changes does most of the work hard-to-vary does for Deutsch. The easily varied myth fails there because it is unfaithful, not because it is variable.
- [INFERENCE] An idle *false* part is harmless on C (F11:L71). On a wider C′ it can start to constrain and interfere (F11:L311), and deleting it then leaves a support of the written Γ (D3:L299) while the full candidate fails. That is the one way an idle part matters to error correction in this theory.

##### (3) Is there a rule that an explanation must be correctable, or one that stops an unconstrained save?

- [TEXT] **No correctability condition on Account.**
  - (E) has five conjuncts, none about variability (F11:L263; D3:L261).
  - "(E) has no condition that each commitment do work … it grades nothing" (D3:L313).
  - A claim needing a merit function is unsettled (D3:L25, L516).
  - "Recursive scrutiny with operative return" (F11:L75) and scrutinizability (F11:L479) are about a system's practice and define the recursive class (F11:L520), not Accounts.
- [TEXT] **The nearest thing to exposure** is non-vacuity: a contract "excluding every change under which the active commitments could matter to Q … is not a contract on which an account can be claimed" (F11:L259).
- [TEXT] **What does stop an unconstrained save:**
  - (a) unrecorded index changes: "What is prohibited is changing C or Q during an assessment without recording" (F11:L163), and goalpost-moving (F11:L598);
  - (b) backdated adequacy (F11:L445) and borrowed success (F11:L309, L311);
  - (c) inputs chosen from the verdict: obligations "fixed for the comparison" (L427), boundary and continuity "declared before the attribution, not chosen after it" (L465), "rather than choosing the input from the verdict wanted" (L514);
  - (d) patches that are tables of observed answers (L271) or restate the answer at the grain (L257; D3:L255);
  - (e) patches unfaithful anywhere in the contract (F1, F2, A).
- [FOLLOWS] **What is not stopped:**
  - (f) a stated narrowing after failure. It is a new claim, "recorded" and not certified (L161; D3:L159), and O1 stays SILENT.
  - (g) a faithful patch fitted after the fact.
  - (h) idle parts.
  - (i) blaming B or I instead of T. K3 leaves this open, and file 12 says it "is a further question".

#### Part D. Bearing on the owner's answer [INFERENCE throughout]

"It depends on how error correction is handled" can be answered from the text. The theory handles error correction by:

- **indexing claims**, so that a failed claim stays failed and a save is a new claim;
- **holding every candidate to fidelity over every admitted change of its contract**, so that a point patch fails wherever the contract reaches;
- **fixing obligations before a repair**, so that trade-off fixes fail against a declared P.

It does not handle correction by any property of content that makes errors correctable, and it keeps no mark of after-the-fact, arbitrary or evasive fitting.

The yes-or-no test therefore does Deutsch's work exactly where contracts are rich, and not where they are thin and stated. The places to press are three:

- stated narrow contracts: O1 is SILENT, and the appropriateness of a restriction is an input;
- idle parts, which are tolerated;
- K3's open choice of which conjunct to revise.

A grading would enter as a merit function, which draft 3 calls "unsettled" (D3:L25, L516). A non-grading alternative would be a record-level rule on revisions, in the style of the index rules: "a component introduced after a failure is held to …". Nothing like it is in the text.

#### 1. Deutsch, Chapter 1: the argument in order

**1a. The myth is an explanation, and it is testable (pp.19–20).**
- Q: "That myth, though comprehensively false, does constitute an explanation of seasons" (D p.19). "It is also eminently testable" (D p.19).
- Q: The escape variant "accounts for the same observations as the original, and it is testable (and in fact refuted) by the same observations" (D p.20).
- So for Deutsch, being easy to vary does not stop a myth from being an explanation or from being testable. What it lacks is something else.

**1b. The details have no function: they are swappable (pp.20–21).**
- Q: "the role of all the details could be played equally well by countless other things" (D p.21).
- Q: "none of those differing attributes has any function in the myths’ respective accounts of why seasons happen." (D p.21).
- Q: "The reason those myths are so easily variable is that their details are barely connected to the details of the phenomena." (D p.21).
- I: In Deutsch, a detail that lacks "any function" is one whose *specifics* do no work. Demeter's sadness does carry the prediction (it causes winter), but any other god's mood would carry it as well. This is a wider notion than draft 3's "does no work" (W33.1: adding or removing *d* leaves every support a support). The myth's details are swappable, not idle in W33.1's sense.

**1c. Rationality, with no mention of correction (p.21).**
- Q: "Whenever a wide range of variant theories can account equally well … there is no reason to prefer one of them over the others" (D p.21). He goes on: "advocating a particular one in preference to the others is irrational." (D p.21).
- I: This is a reason against easy variation that does not depend on error correction. It concerns choosing among equally fitting variants.

**1d. The fundamental flaw, stated as a point about correction: the tweak (pp.21–22).** This is the passage the task asks for, where a testable but easy-to-vary theory is saved by a tweak.
- Q: "That freedom to make drastic changes in those mythical explanations of seasons is the fundamental flaw in them." (D p.21).
- Q: The reason he gives follows: "whenever it is easy to vary an explanation without changing its predictions, one could just as easily vary it to make different predictions" (D p.21).
- Q: The tweak itself: "when Demeter is sad she banishes warmth from her vicinity, and it has to go elsewhere – into the southern hemisphere." (D p.21).
- Q: The prophet and the gambler: "they can easily accommodate the new experience without changing the substance of the explanation." (D p.22). "they can simply reinterpret the omens, pick a new date, and make essentially the same prediction." (D p.22).
- Q: The consequence: "testing one’s theory and abandoning it when it is refuted constitutes no progress towards understanding the world." (D p.22).
- Q: "If an explanation could easily explain anything in the given field, then it actually explains nothing." (D p.22).

**1e. "Bad explanation" is named at the point where correction fails (p.22).**
- Q: "when theories are easily variable … experimental testing is almost useless for correcting their errors. I call such theories bad explanations." (D p.22).
- Q: "Being proved wrong by experiment, and changing the theories to other bad explanations, does not get their holders one jot closer to the truth." (D p.22).
- Q: "Nor is a person capable of making progress merely by virtue of being willing to drop a theory when it is refuted" (D p.22).
- Q: "By adopting easily variable explanations, the gambler and prophet are ensuring that they will be able to continue fooling themselves no matter what happens." (D p.22).
- I: These are the lines that most directly ground badness in error correction. For Deutsch a refuted theory that is easy to vary still *changes*. It is swapped for a variant, so something that looks like correction happens, but it makes no progress. What is lost is not the change but the progress.

**1f. In the other direction, the quest comes first and correction follows from it (pp.22–23, 26).**
- Q: "The quest for good explanations is, I believe, the basic regulating principle not only of science, but of the Enlightenment generally." (D p.22). "it implies all those other conditions for scientific progress I have discussed" (D p.22). "And hence it also implies the need for a tradition of criticism." (D p.23).
- Q: "the standard scientific methodology of dropping theories when refuted by experiment is implied by the requirement for good explanations." (D p.26).

**1g. The good explanation: refutation has nowhere to go (pp.24–25).**
- Q: "That is a good explanation – hard to vary, because all its details play a functional role." (D p.24).
- Q: "if the axis-tilt theory had been refuted, its defenders would have had nowhere to go." (D p.25). "No easily implemented change could make tilted axes cause the same seasons all over the planet." (D p.25).
- Q: "it is only when a theory is a good explanation – hard to vary – that it even matters whether it is testable." (D p.25). "Bad explanations are equally useless whether they are testable or not." (D p.25).

**1h. The idle add-on: the sun god (p.24).**
- Q: "Nor could one easily incorporate any stories about how the sun god feels about all this" (D p.24). The reasons he gives: "how anyone feels about it is irrelevant" (D p.24), and "if there were some flaw in that explanation, then no story about how anyone felt would put it right." (D p.24).
- I: This is Deutsch's only worked example of a purely idle part, and it is the source of case N1. He does not say the idle story makes the account dangerous to correct. He says the reverse: an idle story could not repair a flaw, so it cannot serve as a patch. He treats it as not incorporated into the explanation ("irrelevant"), which matches N1's agreed verdict (it is "not part of what explains them").

**1i. Superfluous features: bad by definition, and the repair is to delete them (pp.25–26).**
- Q: "a common way in which an explanation can be bad is by containing superfluous features or arbitrariness, and sometimes removing those yields a good explanation." (D p.25).
- Q: "while assumptions ‘beyond necessity’ make a theory bad by definition, there have been many mistaken ideas of what is ‘necessary’ in a theory." (D p.26).
- I: Here the case against superfluous parts is definitional ("bad by definition"). No reason from error correction is given. The claim is also qualified: people have often been wrong about what is "necessary".

**1j. Hard-to-vary is itself a form of criticism: most errors are corrected without tests (pp.25–26).**
- Q: "the overwhelming majority of false theories can be rejected out of hand without any experiment, simply for being bad explanations." (D p.25).
- Q: "the rule of testability is an insufficient check on such mistakes. But the quest for good explanations does the job" (D p.26).
- Q: "inventing falsehoods is easy, and therefore they are easy to vary once found" (D p.26).
- Q: "The best explanations are the ones that are most constrained by existing knowledge" (D p.26).

**1k. A good explanation resists your own tweaks (pp.27–28).**
- Q: "That is what a good explanation will do for you: it makes it harder for you to fool yourself." (D p.27).
- Q: The escape clause ("everywhere else on Earth, they also happen at those times") leaves a theory that "is no longer an explanation of seasons, just a (purported) rule of thumb." (D p.28).
- Q: "because it is a good explanation – hard to vary – it is not yours to modify." (D p.28).
- Q: "because the harder it is to vary an explanation, the harder it is in particular to construct a variant with a different reach" (D p.29).

**1l. Summary and glossary (pp.29–33).**
- Q: "Old ways of thought, which did not seek good explanations, permitted no process such as science for correcting errors and misconceptions." (D p.29).
- Q: The glossary defines the grade by variation alone: "An explanation that is hard/easy to vary while still accounting for what it purports to account for." (D p.31).
- Q: The glossary entry "Rational": "Attempting to solve problems by seeking good explanations; actively pursuing error-correction by creating criticisms of both existing ideas and new proposals." (D p.31).
- Q: The summary: "acknowledging that we may always be mistaken, and trying to correct errors. We do so by seeking good explanations" (D p.32). "This, not experimental testing, was the decisive factor in the scientific revolution" (D p.32).
- Q: Fallibilism and justificationism: "Fallibilists expect even their best and most fundamental explanations to contain misconceptions in addition to truth" (D p.9). "the logic of justificationism is to seek (and typically, to believe that one has found) ways of securing ideas against change." (D p.9).

#### 4. Marletto

**Knowledge is information that keeps itself in existence, and error correction keeps it there.**
- Q: "Unless something intervenes to prevent and correct those errors, the structure will eventually fade away or collapse." (M p.3). She names the property of keeping oneself in existence "resilience" (M p.3).
- Q: Bacterial DNA is "potentially preserved for a much longer time than the bacterium’s life span thanks to error-correction enacted by the cell." (M p.11).
- Q: Knowledge is "information that is capable of keeping itself instantiated in physical systems. It is resilient information." (M p.13). "‘Knowledge’ merely denotes a particular kind of information, which has the capacity to perpetuate itself and stay embodied in physical systems" (M p.13).

**Criticism is error correction, and what it produces is hard to change.**
- Q: "Criticism is the act of seeking and correcting errors in an attempt to improve on the solutions" (M p.15).
- Q: The finished work "is hard to change further, while still meeting the criteria, because it has been obtained by tentatively removing flaws in previous versions" (M p.15).
- Q: "any good solution to a problem may also contain some errors." (M p.16). "Fallibilism makes progress feasible because it allows for further criticism to occur in the future" (M p.16).
- Q: "Particularly detrimental to knowledge creation are the immutable limitations imposed by dogmas, as they restrain the ability to conjecture and criticise." (M p.18).
- I: Marletto runs the arrow from correction to the trait: correction produces the hard-to-change product. Deutsch's Ch.1 mainly runs it the other way, from hard-to-vary to effective correction. His Ch.4 (p.78, p.86) agrees with Marletto: error-correcting processes are what produce hard-to-vary knowledge.

**Ch.5. Catalysts, repair and the appearance of design (pp.148–155).**
- Q: The factory: "If it is a well-run factory, there is a way to fix the problem and thus restore the production process." (M p.152). By contrast, losing "the instructions for repairing the factory, the factory might have to shut down." (M p.152).
- Q: The recipe works because "the final product is checked against the procedure until it meets the criteria set by the quality control of the company." (M p.153).
- Q: A part dedicated to a function is one where "if you change it slightly, it no longer meets the criterion of being able to perform that function." (M p.155).
- Q: Of "something with the appearance of design that can last a long time", she says "you can rightly assume that some abstract catalyst is contained in it." (M p.155).
- Q: "those who know the philosopher Karl Popper will recognise the chief features of his epistemology" (M pp.155–156).
- I: Here hard-to-vary (every part dedicated to its function) is a sign that knowledge is present, and it is paired with lasting, which comes through repair and copying. The parts in question belong to constructors and recipes, not to explanations.

**Explanations and testing.**
- Q: The traits of a viable theory: "physical theories about the universe that count as viable explanations must at least have certain traits that guarantee they are free of basic flaws." (M p.23). The first trait is exactness. An approximate recipe invites the question "why approximately 100 grams of sugar, and not exactly 99?" (M p.23).
- Q: Such theories "must also be testable so that they can be checked against reality to find potential errors." (M p.24).
- Q: "The quality of a prediction depends, ultimately, on the underlying explanation." (M p.45).
- Q: The dog myth: "you could substitute any other entity for ‘dog’" (M p.48), and "the choice of the universe-supporting animal is arbitrary." (M p.48). She treats this as a separate problem from the myth's untestability.
- Q: Why testability matters: "it provides a particularly efficient way to find mistakes in the explanations and correct them." (M p.48). And "the testability of predictions in physics, and in science in general, is central for the possibility of error correction" (M p.51). "Explanations whose predictions are found wrong in an experiment automatically become problematic" (M pp.51–52).
- Q: From the interlude "Alexandros", a fictional dialogue: "try to be prepared to create knowledge and improve on things tentatively, by making mistakes and trying to correct them as fast as you can." (M p.233).
- I: Marletto links error correction to *testability*, not to hard-to-vary. She never says that an easy-to-vary theory defeats testing. Her complaint that the dog is arbitrary, a swappable part, is not tied to correction. She states exactness and universality as requirements a viable explanation "must" meet. That is threshold language, not a grade, but it concerns exactness, not variation.

#### 5. The two questions

##### 5a. Is hard-to-vary valuable in itself, or because it enables error correction?

**Both. Deutsch never ranks them, and in Chapter 1 each implies the other.**

Lines that make hard-to-vary valuable *because of correction*:
- D p.21: the "fundamental flaw", and the reason given for it: "one could just as easily vary it to make different predictions".
- D p.22: "experimental testing is almost useless for correcting their errors. I call such theories bad explanations." "Bad explanation" is named right after the failure of correction.
- D p.25: "it is only when a theory is a good explanation – hard to vary – that it even matters whether it is testable."
- D p.32: "trying to correct errors. We do so by seeking good explanations".
- D p.94 and p.257: being hard to vary makes errors in transmission detectable and correctable.

Lines that make it prior, or valuable on other grounds:
- D p.22: "the basic regulating principle", which "implies" the other conditions. D p.23: it "implies the need for a tradition of criticism". D p.26: dropping refuted theories "is implied by the requirement for good explanations". Here correction is derived *from* the demand for hard-to-vary, not the reverse.
- D p.21: preferring one of many equally fitting variants "is irrational". This is a reason about choice, with no correction in it.
- D p.29: being hard to vary fixes reach. D p.353: theories are hard to vary "because they correspond closely with an objective truth". D p.26: falsehoods are easy to vary.
- D p.26: superfluous assumptions make a theory "bad by definition". D p.31: the glossary defines good and bad by variation alone, with no mention of correction.

**I:** The answer the book supports is this. The *definition* of good and bad is structural (p.31). The *argument* that the structural property matters is mainly about error correction (pp.21–25, 32). Deutsch also treats the quest for such explanations as the root from which criticism and error correction follow (pp.22–23, 26). Marletto reverses the causal order (p.15): correction produces the hard-to-change product.

##### 5b. Are idle parts dangerous to correction because they can be adjusted to absorb a refutation?

**Not in these words, and not for idle parts in draft 3's sense.**
- The absorption argument (D pp.21–22) is about details whose *specifics* are arbitrary but which *carry the prediction*: Demeter's mood, the omens, the date. The tweak changes such a working detail ("she banishes warmth … into the southern hemisphere", p.21). These are swappable parts. Deutsch calls them parts with no "function" (p.21), meaning their specifics do no work.
- For a purely idle add-on to a good explanation (the sun god, D p.24) he says the opposite of absorption: "no story about how anyone felt would put it right."
- Superfluous features are called a defect, "bad by definition" (D pp.25–26), with deletion as the repair. No reason from correction is given for this.
- Ad hoc *restrictions* are called "very bad explanations" (D pp.118–119: "arbitrary qualifiers, workarounds"), and the escape clause on p.28 turns the explanation into "a (purported) rule of thumb". These are working parts added to evade a prediction, not idle ones.
- **I:** On Deutsch's own example, a part that does no work cannot absorb a refutation until it is *given* work. It then stops being idle, and it becomes a free, swappable working part: the case pp.21–22 condemn. N1's sentence ("keeps the tilt steady") sits on this line. As written it duplicates work the spinning-body point already does. If the tilt were found to wander, it is the clause a teller would reach for. The books do not discuss this case.

#### 6. What the books do not say

1. Neither book says that an *idle* part (one that does no work) threatens correction by absorbing refutations. Deutsch's absorption argument concerns swappable parts that carry predictions. His idle example (p.24) says an idle story cannot repair a flaw. D uses "idle" once, as "not just an idle preference" (p.113), and M never uses it. The word "superfluous" occurs once in D (p.25) and never in M.
2. Deutsch never says whether hard-to-vary is valued "in itself" or "as a means". He gives both kinds of reason and never ranks them (section 5a).
3. Neither book makes hard-to-vary a condition for *being* an explanation. D p.19 calls the easy-to-vary myth an explanation. Only the limiting cases lose the name: an account that "could easily explain anything … explains nothing" (p.22), and the escape clause leaves "a (purported) rule of thumb" (p.28).
4. Deutsch uses hard-to-vary as a matter of degree. Examples: "most constrained" (p.26), "The better an explanation is, the more rigidly its reach is determined" (p.29), "isn’t as hard to vary as the full theory" (p.306), "not yet hard to vary" (p.342), and neutral changes in adaptations (p.78). Only the glossary is two-valued (p.31). Neither book gives a scale or a measure.
5. Deutsch never connects the digital error correction of Chapter 6 to hard-to-vary. The analogy in section 2 is this note's. The book's own bridges are p.94 and p.257 (transmission) and p.342 ("discrete").
6. Marletto never uses "hard to vary" ("hard to change" occurs once, p.15), "good explanation" as a defined term, or any form of the argument that easy variation makes testing useless. She links error correction to testability (pp.24, 48, 51) and to repair and copying (pp.3, 11, 152–153).
7. Neither book says that every part of a good explanation must be hard to vary. D p.342 allows details "not yet hard to vary", and p.78 allows changes that make "no practical difference".
8. Neither book discusses a yes-or-no test of "is this an explanation" set against grading. That is the owner's question, and the books neither pose nor answer it.

#### 7. Bearing on the owner's question (inference only)

- **I:** In the sources, the strongest reason to care about hard-to-vary is that it makes correction effective: refutation then forces a real change instead of a swap (D pp.21–25). That reason concerns what happens to an account *under refutation*. It does not concern whether the account is an explanation now. So if the theory handles error correction in its own right, the sources' main reason for grading can be met there without changing (E). One example is the lemma proposed in observation 14 of the S89 audit, or something in Repair (P) and K3. Such a lemma would say that a refutation corrects an account only if the account has no free working part that can be reset to fit.
- **I:** The sources give no reason from correction against *idle* parts in W33.1's sense. Their reason against idle or superfluous parts is definitional ("bad by definition", p.26). Their own idle example is harmless to correction (p.24). The danger they describe belongs to swappable working parts. If the owner's "depends entirely on how error correction is handled" is about idle parts absorbing refutations, the books support it only for parts that have been given work.
- **I:** Marletto's direction (correction produces the hard-to-change product, M p.15), with D p.78 and p.86, suggests a reading in which hard-to-vary is *evidence* that correction has taken place, not a requirement on what counts as an explanation.
