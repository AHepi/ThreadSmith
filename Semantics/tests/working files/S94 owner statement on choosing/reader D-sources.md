## 1. The short answer, in plain words

**Does the statement add anything, seen from the books?** Mostly it says again, in the owner's own words, what the two books already say and what the theory already carries:

- A problem is a clash of ideas; two answers can both look right; if both survive, nothing is settled yet (Deutsch pp.16–17, 341; Marletto p.14). The theory: Part VI, L315–317.
- Nobody is infallible; every answer is tentative and may be wrong (Deutsch pp.31–32, 446, 448; Marletto pp.16, 24). The theory: Part I, L67 and L71.
- A problem can be wrongly posed and replaced (Deutsch pp.182, 209–210, 332). The theory: Part III, L161.
- Inquiry can stop, and people leave problems aside (Deutsch p.215; Marletto p.17). The theory: "Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof." (L429).
- In science, reality is what an idea must answer to, with no infallible yardstick (Deutsch p.32; Marletto p.24). The theory: L67, L25, L522.

**Where it adds, or leans.** Two places.

1. **Ruling out by argument.** The owner speaks of choosing "the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument", in fields where there is no experiment. Both books agree that argument alone can rule an idea out: Deutsch says most false theories are rejected "without any experiment" (p.25), and Marletto shows Galileo refuting Aristotle by deriving a contradiction (p.50). The theory leaves open whether a failure found by argument counts as "established" (file 93, first choice). The statement and the books lean toward counting it. They do not settle it on their own (finding D1).
2. **Not saying what must happen.** The owner writes "Notice I never once claimed what must happen." Deutsch does say what a rational chooser should do (pp.209, 340–341). The theory, like the owner, prescribes nothing (L429, L441, L522). Here the statement sides with the theory against Deutsch's way of putting it, though not against his substance (finding D6).

**For the note of sources and departures:** the statement touches two of its lines. A small clarification is proposed for the *Hard to vary* line, and an optional sentence for *Surprise and problems* (findings D14, D4). No line of the note is contradicted by the books.

## 2. What the books say on the topics asked

Each list below keeps one quotation word for word, or two short ones that together stay within 25 words; the other points are paraphrased and marked "paraphrased", with their pages (lesson S29: quotations in consecutive bullets count as one quotation).

### 2.1 Problems as conflicts between ideas

- Deutsch names the notion on p.17: “I shall call a situation in which we experience conflicting ideas a problem.”
- His chapter glossary defines a problem the same way, as an experienced conflict between ideas (p.31, paraphrased).
- He says a problem can arise from theory alone, with no observation at all (p.17, paraphrased).
- He says what solves one, which is not a choice between the conflicting ideas but a new idea: an explanation that lacks the conflict (p.17, paraphrased).
- He treats every problem as a sign that what we know is flawed or inadequate somewhere (p.18, paraphrased).
- Of philosophy, he says experience supplies problems, by setting ideas already held against each other, but not tests (p.120, paraphrased).
- Marletto, following Popper, says the making of knowledge starts from a problem, a clash between the ideas someone holds about reality (p.14, paraphrased).
- Her example is an author whose two criteria, lyrical language and a gripping plot, pull against each other (p.15).
- **The theory.** Part VI names one kind of such conflict: two rivals that both fit pose “a conflict between ideas that what the assessor has established has not settled.” (L317). The wider sense is carried by the recognized difficulty of Part X (L429). The note records the narrowing (CL403; section 4).

### 2.2 Criticism by argument where experiment cannot reach

- Deutsch says that in science most false theories are dropped with no test at all (p.25): “the overwhelming majority of false theories can be rejected out of hand without any experiment, simply for being bad explanations”.
- In chapter 5 he makes the point again, to argue that the same method works outside science: nearly all discarded theories go for being bad explanations, never having been tested (pp.119–120, paraphrased).
- He counts experiment as one method of criticism among many in science, not above the others (p.120, paraphrased).
- Outside science he holds that objective progress is possible, in politics and morality as in science (p.209, paraphrased).
- His example is the claim that slavery is providential: a philosophical theory, which no experience could refute (p.121, paraphrased).
- Yet, he goes on, an argument could undo the explanation by which many people understood that claim (p.121, paraphrased).
- On Fermi's problem, a live scientific one, he reports many proposed candidates and none yet very good (p.101, paraphrased), and rejects the anthropic candidate by argument, not by any test (p.101).
- Marletto shows Galileo refuting Aristotle's law of falling bodies in thought, before any test: assume the law, derive a contradiction, and conclude that the assumption is false (p.50, paraphrased).
- She also names economics and medicine as fields where testing is hard (p.211, paraphrased).
- **The theory.** (E)'s conditions are, in L231's words, “each a condition on supplied relations under the changes in \(C\)”, so a failure of non-circular dependence, say, is found by examining the candidate. Whether such a finding is “established” (L315: “A result is **established** for an assessor who holds a usable receipt for it (Part IX)”) is open. The receipt's leaves are worded for events (L397: “An evidence leaf is a reference to an event with an interpreted claim.”), and the (K3) clause for tests (L315: “the background and instruments of the test that yields it”). This is file 93's first choice.

### 2.3 Fallibilism: no infallible authority or source

- Deutsch's glossary defines fallibilism as recognizing that knowledge has no authoritative source, and no reliable way of being justified as true or probable (p.31, paraphrased).
- His chapter summary makes it a practice: accept that we may always be wrong, and try to correct errors (p.32, paraphrased).
- He ties it to how knowledge is made: errors cannot be avoided, and only processes that correct them last (p.192, paraphrased).
- In his Socrates dialogue, Hermes tells Socrates that he will stay fallible (p.242, paraphrased).
- His last chapter denies infallibility and finality to science (p.446) and, a page later, to every field (p.448) (both paraphrased).
- He quotes Popper denying that ideal sources exist, any more than ideal rulers do (p.209, paraphrased).
- He contrasts the abstract computer, which never errs, needs no upkeep and has endless memory and time, with every real one, and then counts our brains among the real ones (p.119, paraphrased).
- Marletto says the same of solutions (p.16): “There are no absolute sources of certain truth: any good solution to a problem may also contain some errors.”
- She says of physical theories that one which seems to work may turn out false at any time (p.24, paraphrased).
- She notes that a physical machine's abilities are only approximate: even a phone's ability to add wears out in the end (p.60, paraphrased).
- **The theory.** “Systems can be wrong about their transports, their observations, their criticisms and their own capacities.” (L67). “A thinker may act on an appraisal without certifying it.” (L71).

### 2.4 Choosing between rivals, and what happens when both survive

- Deutsch says a test needs at least two live theories on the issue, whose conflicting predictions an experiment can tell apart (p.16, paraphrased).
- Chapter 13, "Choices", says what a choice is (p.340): “To choose an option is to choose its explanation.”
- He denies that rational decision-making is a weighing of evidence; it is an explaining of it (p.341, paraphrased).
- He says that, once a good explanation is created, the alternatives lose their pull (p.341): “They have been not outweighed, but out-argued, refuted and abandoned.”
- He puts the creation of new options, and the dropping or changing of old ones, at the heart of decision-making (p.341, paraphrased).
- He says the usual state is a hard search for a single good explanation, not two equal survivors (p.341, paraphrased).
- He says a compromise halfway between two rivals is usually worse than either of them (p.342, paraphrased).
- He holds that a good explanation, being hard to vary, has no twin: there is only one (p.341, paraphrased).
- He uses the owner's phrase in the "has" form, "have no option", first of seeing the world through our best existing explanations, misconceptions and all (p.199; paraphrased apart from the three words quoted).
- He uses it again in a dialogue on quantum theory, where the reader says the theory's implications must be accepted because it is the only known explanation, and David asks what other option one would want (p.305, paraphrased).
- Marletto says a principle can be tested only once there are at least two rival theories of a physical situation it is meant to govern (p.211, paraphrased).
- **The theory.** Part VI: two rivals that both fit pose a problem for p (L317). For the first kind, a test “solves the problem whatever it shows” (L317). For the second kind, “where both meet (E) on \(C\) both are accounts of \(p\).” (L317), and “the problem for \(p\) stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting.” (L317). Being easy to vary “says nothing about which of them is right.” (L317). On choosing anyway, L317 ends: “as when choosing one of two rivals meets a claimed obligation only by dropping the other, which fits as well, where keeping every rival that fits is among the protected obligations (Part XI).”

### 2.5 Problems badly posed, or the wrong problem

- Deutsch quotes Popper proposing to replace the question of the sources of knowledge by a wholly different question (p.209, paraphrased).
- He says the political questions of who should rule rest on the same misconception (p.210, paraphrased).
- He says Zeno's paradoxes come down to one mistake, a confusion between two senses of infinity (p.182, paraphrased).
- Of apportionment, where experts disagreed without finding errors in each other's mathematics, he concludes (p.332): “this problem is not really about mathematics”.
- He shows a problem swapped for a better one: the condemned prisoner may think of a feasible task that would please the king more than a talking horse (p.214, paraphrased).
- He says the constraints of a problem are themselves made: the clients had to settle, creatively, what their priorities and constraints should be (p.342, paraphrased).
- Marletto's author holds two criteria that may pull apart in some situations (p.15, paraphrased).
- **The theory.** “A question may fail to pick out its alleged target, assume an incompatible baseline, or combine incompatible requirements. Its formulation is still an event. Exposing the defect is another question with its own contract.” (L161). And, in the same paragraph: “Supplying a meaning for a replacement query can make a coherent new question; it does not answer the original one.” (L161). Questions “can be found as well as answered” (L15), and “When it is a contract, the originative act is the finding of a question.” (L425).

### 2.6 Creativity

- Deutsch's glossary defines creativity as the ability to make new explanations (p.30, paraphrased).
- He says its working is not yet understood (p.163): “we do not understand how creativity works”.
- He adds that no one knows how to model a creative process, since such a model would itself be one: an AI (p.345, paraphrased).
- Marletto describes the first step as proposing several trial solutions, which criticism then works on (p.15, paraphrased).
- She denies that conjecture and criticism guarantee progress, while holding that progress can be made (p.16, paraphrased).
- **The theory.** “Creativity lives in construction.” (L13); “question-finding is at least half of creativity.” (L15).

### 2.7 A criterion, measure or metric of good explanation

- Deutsch rejects the picture of decision as picking from existing options by a fixed formula, in the chapter and in its summary (pp.341, 352, paraphrased).
- He allows that a formula can come at the end of a creative process, not in place of it (p.342): “The decision-making process may end with such a calculation, but it begins creatively”.
- He does speak of a standard, though not of a measure: science, he says, keeps to the criterion of good explanation (p.333, paraphrased).
- What he rejects is a justifying criterion: his glossary calls justificationism the mistaken view that knowledge counts as real or reliable only when some source or criterion justifies it (p.31, paraphrased).
- Marletto's standard is the problem's own criteria, met tentatively: a masterpiece is one that is hard to change further while it still meets them (p.15, paraphrased).
- **The theory.** It supplies no “merit function, a measure of worth, or a ranking of thinkers” (L25), and “The semantics supplies no probability of truth, no merit function and no ranking of thinkers, and a claim that needs one is unsettled.” (L522). A caution on words: the theory uses "metric" once, in the mathematical sense of a distance for approximate transport (L363: “and let \(d\) be a metric on \(X_E\)”); that is not a measure of how good an explanation is.

### 2.8 Reality as the judge in science

- Deutsch's chapter summary gives observation its role (p.32): “The role of experiment and observation is to choose between existing theories, not to be the source of new ones.”
- He says a good explanation is hard to create because there is an objective reality, not because of anyone's decision (p.314, paraphrased).
- He draws the line between science and the rest at testability (p.13, paraphrased).
- He adds that the label matters less than the explanation, and calls myths and superstitions unscientific even where they can be tested (p.22, paraphrased).
- Marletto says physical theories must be testable, so that they can be checked against reality for errors (p.24, paraphrased).
- She says the most a test lets us say is that a theory has not yet been found false (p.24, paraphrased).
- **The theory.** “Whether a transport is faithful on a contract is independent of whether anyone accepts it.” (L67). “Objectivity lives in the physics and the fidelity facts; scope-honesty lives in the record.” (L43).

### 2.9 Also found: leaving a problem, priorities and limits

- Deutsch says many factors decide whether mathematicians take up a problem at all, and how hard they work at it (p.215, paraphrased).
- He says, on the same page, that easy problems may be left alone for good (p.215): “If an easy problem is not deemed to be interesting or useful, they might leave it unsolved indefinitely”.
- He quotes Popper on living with a problem until a more fascinating one comes along, or a solution (p.18, paraphrased).
- Marletto says physics allows the making of knowledge but does not guarantee it, so it may stop at any point (p.17, paraphrased).
- She says the same of thinking in one mind, a few lines later: it can stagnate and stop progressing (p.17, paraphrased).
- In her closing story, Aristotle says of a task not yet done that he must get round to it, since it takes time (p.232; a character in a story, not the author's argument; paraphrased).
- Both books warn against one kind of stopping. Marletto names the fixed limits of dogma as especially harmful to the making of knowledge (p.18, paraphrased).
- Deutsch describes static societies that answered children's questions about pointless duties with 'Because I say so' (p.391; paraphrased apart from the four words quoted).

## 3. Claim by claim

Each entry: what the books do (agree, depart, silent), with pages, then what the theory already says.

**C1.** "In the case of non scientific theories: candidate explanations attempt to solve a problem. Both may appear to solve it."
- *Two readings of "non scientific theories".* (a) Theories in fields where experiment cannot decide, such as philosophy, morality and politics (Deutsch pp.119–121, 209; Marletto p.211 on economics and medicine). (b) Deutsch's own usage, in which myths and superstitions are “unscientific even when they make testable predictions” (p.22). The contrast the owner draws in C17 ("The case for science is reality itself") favours (a), but (b) would bring the myth of file 93's examples under C1–C2.
- *Books: agree.* A problem is a clash of ideas (Deutsch pp.17, 31; Marletto p.14). Candidates are proposed as attempts (Marletto p.15, “one conjectures several tentative solutions”; Deutsch p.101, “Many candidate explanations have been proposed”). "Both may appear to solve it" matches Deutsch's “two apparently viable theories” (p.16).
- *Theory: already said.* "appear to solve" corresponds to "fits": “A candidate **fits** what is established for an assessor when no result established for that assessor shows it failing a condition of (E).” (L315). Two that fit pose a problem for p (L317).

**C2.** "But choosing one, for whatever reason, means that the person doing the choosing sees no option but to choose the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument (note: “sees no option”, not “has no option”)."
- *Two readings of "its best argument".* (a) The best argument that bears on the candidate, that is, the strongest criticism of it: the chooser takes the one that survives it. (b) The candidate's own best supporting argument: the chooser takes the one whose case, at its strongest, still stands. Either way, what rules a candidate out is an argument, not only an observation.
- *Books: agree on substance.* Choosing is choosing an explanation, and the alternatives are “not outweighed, but out-argued, refuted and abandoned” (Deutsch pp.340–341). Argument can rule out without experiment (Deutsch pp.25, 119–121; Marletto p.50).
- *Books: the parenthesis.* Deutsch writes "have no option" (pp.199, 305). The owner's "sees no option" is a deliberate change. Two readings: (a) a refinement Deutsch would accept, since his own p.199 sentence says our best explanations “include our existing misconceptions”, and he holds that choosing turns on creating new options (p.341) and that a person's options may be far more than the person can see (the prisoner, p.214); (b) a denial that reason compels the choice at all, so that the chooser could still choose otherwise; on this reading the owner departs from the tone of Deutsch's p.305 (“What other option would you like to have?”). C6–C11 fit either reading.
- *Theory: already said.* The chooser-relative view is the theory's "for an assessor" (L315); “A thinker may act on an appraisal without certifying it.” (L71); systems “can be wrong about … their criticisms” (L67). Whether an argument's finding counts as "established" is file 93's first choice (finding D1).

**C3.** "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached."
- *Books: agree.* A problem is solved only when the conflict is gone (Deutsch p.17); while two rivals stand, one is still “struggling to create even one good explanation” (p.341); Fermi's problem is an unsolved case with many candidates (p.101). *Depart in one respect:* Deutsch holds that of good explanations “there is only one of them” (p.341), and would judge the axis tilt hard to vary before any rival is offered (pp.24–25). The theory allows two accounts of one question when they conflict only outside it (L317). The owner's claim does not decide between these: "no resolution" is true on both views.
- *Theory: already said.* “the problem for \(p\) stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting.” (L317).

**C4.** "Therefore further investigation is required the conflict and potentially solve the problem."
- *A word seems missing after "required"; none is supplied here.*
- *Books: agree.* Investigation may be a test between the two (Deutsch p.16; Marletto p.211), or the creation of a new idea without the conflict (Deutsch p.17), or of new options (p.341).
- *Theory: already said.* A test for a problem of the first kind; for the second kind, “A finer contract that contains a change at which two rivals conflict makes a new question (Part III)” (L317). Deutsch's route, a new explanation that removes the conflict, is construction and repair (Parts X–XI: L405, “A first representation may be constructed from an available problem without prior observation of what it represents.”; L435, “The contribution \(\Delta\) of a repair is a subhistory together with the changes of content it makes.”), not a word of Part VI; see finding D4.

**C5.** "The problem may, for whatever reason, be ill posed."
- *Two readings.* (a) The question the candidates answer is defective. (b) The conflict itself is illusory: the candidates do not really conflict.
- *Books: agree.* Deutsch pp.182, 209–210, 214, 332, 342; Marletto p.15 (criteria that clash).
- *Theory: already said.* (a) L161, L159 (“What makes a restriction appropriate to the question asked is a substantive, criticizable part of the claim”), L15, L425. (b) Candidates that conflict at no admitted pair are not rivals (L315: “neither pair is a pair of rivals”).
- *A word to watch.* The owner's "problem" here is what the candidates attempt to solve, which the theory calls a question (Part III, which begins at L135 “A question is” and lists its parts). The theory's "problem for p" (L317) is narrower. File 92 already says so; any plain-words answer should keep the two apart.

**C6.** "Therefore, whatever happens to the candidates is up to the person doing the choosing."
**C8.** "Resolution is up to the person and the person's choice."
**C11.** "Whatever happens is always the choice of the person."
- *Two readings.* (a) What the person does with the candidates and the episode, pursue, choose, set aside, is the person's choice; what is true is not. (b) Resolution itself, which candidate is right, is settled by the person's choice. The owner's C16 ("may be wrong") presupposes something to be wrong about, which favours (a).
- *Books.* On (a), agree: Marletto p.16 (no guarantee) and p.17 (may stop); Deutsch p.215 (which problems get addressed). On (b), depart: Deutsch holds that good explanation is hard “not because of what anyone has decided, but because there is an objective reality” (p.314), and that there is “an objective difference between right and wrong” (p.122), with objective progress in morality and politics (p.209).
- *Theory: already said on (a).* “Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof.” (L429); “(P) does not rank alternatives.” (L441). On (b) the theory would conflict: “Whether a transport is faithful on a contract is independent of whether anyone accepts it.” (L67).

**C7.** "Notice I never once claimed what must happen."
- *Books: depart in register.* Deutsch does say what one should do. On policies he writes that we should choose between them by how good they are as explanations, not by where they came from (p.209, paraphrased). On choice in general he ties rationality to explanation; his own sentence is (p.341) “To choose an option, rationally, is to choose the associated explanation.” Marletto also speaks in "should": she says we should hope to find more errors, as fast as possible (p.16, paraphrased). The owner states no such norm.
- *Theory: sides with the owner.* The theory prescribes nothing to a chooser: obligations are declared inputs whose “declaration makes no claim that the aims are worth pursuing” (L441); worth is an input (L455); no merit function or ranking (L522). Its "must"s are conditions on claims, not on choosers, e.g. “A criticism that a candidate is easy to vary must supply such a rival (Part IX).” (L317). See finding D6.

**C9.** "If the person decides the problem is a low priority, then this whole process may be abandoned."
- *Books: agree.* Deutsch p.215 (problems left unsolved when not deemed interesting or useful); Popper via Deutsch p.18 (a more fascinating problem). Marletto p.17 (knowledge creation may stop).
- *Theory: already said.* L429 (closing is a decision). The problem for p still “stands” (L317): leaving it does not solve it.

**C10.** "If the person is told by its parents to “hurry up and clean your room”, this entire episode may never resolve, and fade into recesses of that person's history."
- *Two readings of the example.* (a) A benign interruption: other duties end the episode. (b) An authority ending an inquiry.
- *Books.* On (a), silent: neither book discusses a benign interruption; Marletto p.17 allows that knowledge creation may stop. On (b), depart: both books count authority that stops criticism as harmful (Marletto p.18, dogma; Deutsch p.391, “Because I say so”).
- *Theory: already said on (a).* L429. The owner's "fade" matches the theory's split between a problem for p, which stands whatever anyone attends to (L317), and a recognized difficulty, which exists only “when the system represents it” (L429). The warning in (b) is not the theory's business (finding D8).

**C12.** "Choice is always important because there is no such thing as an infallible creative agent."
- *Books: agree.* Deutsch pp.31, 32, 192, 242, 446, 448; Popper via Deutsch p.209; Marletto pp.16, 24.
- *Theory: already said.* L67, L71; “a finite performance record does not establish it” (L509, of universality).

**C13.** "Creative agents are always constrained in some way: not enough time, the crop needs harvesting, I need to recharge my electronic brain, whatever. They're all valid choices."
- *Books: agree on constraint.* Real computers go wrong, need maintenance, and have finite memory and time (Deutsch p.119, which he applies to brains); a calculator's ability “will wear out” (Marletto p.60); “It takes time.” (Marletto p.232, a character). *Silent on "valid".* Neither book says whether a choice driven by a harvest or a flat battery is valid; Deutsch's "Rational" (glossary, p.31) is about seeking good explanations, and C14 keeps validity apart from rationality.
- *Theory: already said.* Every attribution of a capability declares the system's boundary and resources (Part XII, L473: “which processes and resources are the system's”). A constraint such as the harvest can be a protected obligation, and dropping an inquiry to keep it is the theory's recognized difficulty, “a conflict in which what the system holds meets a claimed obligation only by failing a protected one” (L429), with no ranking of the two (L441).

**C14.** "Whether they're rational may or may not ever be opened and examined by the creative person/agent."
- *Books: agree.* Nothing is immune from criticism (“Is there literally no idea that we may safely hold immune from criticism?”, Deutsch p.233, where the answer is no); nothing says every idea will be criticized.
- *Theory: already said.* “Any aspect of a system's practice … can become a target” (L73); scrutinizability is a "can" (L487: “criticism can be directed at it, and the result can affect its operative use.”).

**C15.** "The question of “does it need to match the problem”: yes, if resolution is the goal."
- *Two readings of "match".* (a) File 92's plain word for fidelity: an account "matches" the target on the question's range (file 92, lines 415, 514–521). (b) What "fits" what is established (file 93, first choice). The quoted question appears nowhere else in the project.
- *Books: agree.* A solution must meet the problem's criteria (Marletto p.15, “The author has to find a way of meeting both these criteria”); a good explanation accounts “for what it purports to account for” (Deutsch p.31).
- *Theory: already said.* The need is conditional, as the owner says: “An epistemic obligation requires a correct account, or the correction of a use through one, to be deployable.” (L443), and obligations are declared inputs, with no claim that they are worth pursuing (L441).

**C16.** "But “matches the problem” is always tentative and may be wrong."
- *Books: agree.* Marletto says a solution can look right at first and prove inadequate later (p.16, paraphrased). Of physical theories she says the most we can claim is provisional (p.24): “all we can say is that it has so far not been found to be false”. Deutsch agrees (pp.32, 446).
- *Theory: already said.* A candidate "fits" only for an assessor (L315); a receipt can cease to be usable, and then “the exclusion ceases to be established” (L369); “Withdrawing a premise removes a license; it does not make the conclusion false.” (L393). That something may be wrong presupposes a fact of the matter (L67).

## 5. Where the statement sides with the books against the theory, and with the theory against the books

**With the books, where the theory is open.**
1. *Ruling out by argument* (C1, C2). The owner's "ruled out by its best argument", in fields without experiment, agrees with Deutsch (pp.25, 119–121, 101) and Marletto (p.50). The theory neither affirms nor denies that a failure found by argument is "established" (L315, L397): file 93's first choice. No sentence of the theory is contradicted; the statement and books lean one way on an open point (D1).
2. *Problems as conflicts of ideas in the wide sense* (C1, C4, C5). The owner's "problem" is the wide sense of Deutsch (p.17) and Marletto (p.14). The theory keeps the word for a narrower thing (L317) and carries the wide sense as a question (Part III) or a recognized difficulty (L429). The note records this (CL403). Not a conflict: a matter of words (D2, D5).

**With the theory, against the books' way of putting it.**
3. *No "must"* (C7, with C6, C8, C11). The theory, like the owner, prescribes nothing to a chooser (L429, L441, L455, L522). Deutsch says what one should do and what rational choice is (pp.209, 340–341). On reading (a) of C6–C11 this is a difference of register, not of substance (D6).
4. *"sees", not "has"* (C2). The owner's chooser-relative "sees" matches the theory's "for an assessor" (L315) and L71, where Deutsch writes "have no option" (pp.199, 305). Deutsch's own qualifications (p.199 “which include our existing misconceptions”; p.341 on creating new options) keep this close to his substance (D3).
5. *Stopping without blame* (C9, C10, C13). The owner calls constraint-driven stopping valid; the theory records a closed episode as "a decision, not a proof" (L429) and ranks nothing. The books add warnings about stagnation and authority (Marletto p.18; Deutsch p.391) that the theory does not carry and need not (D8).

**Neither.** On *both surviving* (C3) the statement agrees with the books and with the theory at once: all three hold that two surviving rivals leave the matter unresolved. The theory's further claim, that two such rivals can both be accounts of a question (L317), and Deutsch's contrary "only one of them" (p.341) are not touched by the statement (D4, D15).
