## 1. The three choices in file 93

File 93's section 6 puts three choices. The S93 tabulation files them as (c2), (d) item 4, and (c1). The record of decision S21 files the statement as written "Answering file 93's choices" (the recorder's words), and the owner ends with "I don't really understand the rest of the conflicts." That sentence is read here as saying that the statement answers at most part of what file 93 asked; which parts is not said. Nothing below reads a ruling into the owner's words where a second reading is open.

### 1.1 First choice: does a failure found by examining a candidate count as established?

**What file 93 asks.** "*Should a failure found by examining a candidate itself, rather than by looking at the world, count as established?*" The example: the myth of winter assumes its answer (Persephone's yearly return is written into her bargain), and that failure "was found by examining the myth, not by looking at the world". If such a failure counts, the myth does not fit, and the myth and the tilt pose no problem; if not, they pose a problem outside the range, and each is easy to vary against the other.

**What the theory says now.** A result "is **established** for an assessor who holds a usable receipt for it (Part IX); by (K3), a result that tells against a candidate tells against it only together with the background and instruments of the test that yields it" (L315). A candidate "**fits** what is established for an assessor when no result established for that assessor shows it failing a condition of (E)" (L315). "An evidence leaf is a reference to an event with an interpreted claim." (L397). The checkers found that this leaves the choice open and says nothing false either way (X09 ruling 5; X10 ruling 2; X14 "For the owner").

**The owner's words that bear on it.**

- "But choosing one, for whatever reason, means that the person doing the choosing sees no option but to choose the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument (note: “sees no option”, not “has no option”)."
- "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached."
- "In the case of non scientific theories: candidate explanations attempt to solve a problem."
- "The case for science is reality itself. It must match reality, but not by some fixed infallible metric."
- "But “matches the problem” is always tentative and may be wrong."

**Reading.**

1. *The direction is "yes", on every reading.* What rules a candidate out, for the chooser, is an argument. Nothing in the sentence asks that the argument report something seen in the world. The theory's counterpart of "ruled out", for a chooser, is *not fitting what is established for that assessor* (L315). So the owner's words point to counting a failure that an argument finds, the myth's assumed answer included, for the assessor who holds that argument. None of the six outside replies argued the other way (tabulation §6.2), and nothing in the statement gives a ground for "no".
2. *"its best argument" has more than one reading, and all of them rule out by argument.* (a) The chooser's best argument: the owner uses "its" for a person in the same statement ("told by its parents"), which favours this reading. (b) The best argument bearing on the candidate. (c) The candidate's own best case for itself, which may fail, as the myth's bargain fails. Readers A and B give the same readings. On none of them is "best" a grade the theory could supply: the theory ranks nothing ("Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates.", L317), so "best" stays the chooser's own appraisal (lesson S26).
3. *It does not settle file 93's example.* The owner says this "In the case of non scientific theories" and gives science a sentence of its own. The myth and the tilt are offered on a question about the world (why winter comes). If the owner means the split by kind of theory, the statement settles the choice only for non-scientific candidates, and the myth may or may not be one. If the owner means that argument rules out a candidate in any theory, while "It must match reality" is said of the requirements that compare a candidate with the world, the statement settles the choice "yes" for every candidate. Reader B names these R1 and R2. The words do not decide between them.
4. *What kind of settlement the words favour, if the answer is yes.* The owner writes "sees", not "has"; "there is no such thing as an infallible creative agent"; and "“matches the problem” is always tentative and may be wrong". An examination can misread a candidate: whether a component is the answer written in is judged "at the declared grain" (L255), and an examiner can get that wrong. So the owner's words favour settlements in which such a finding is a receipt, usable only while its premises are live, and so able to lapse (ruling X09's proposals 1, 2 and 6), over one that exempts it from (K3) (proposal 3). This agrees with reader B's section 2.6. It is a bearing, not a ruling.
5. *The first choice decides the example of the third.* If a failure found by examining counts, then for anyone who has examined the myth it does not fit, the myth and the tilt pose no problem on the Greeks' question, and the tilt is not easy to vary against it. The third choice then has no bite on its own example, though it still governs pairs such as the gardener's two rescues (section 1.3).

**Status: partly settled.** Direction yes; not settled for the myth; the six settling proposals' differences (whether an examination is itself a receipt, whether (K3) qualifies it, where the words go) are not touched by the owner's words.

**For the owner, one plain question** (combining readers A and B): *"When a person rules out one of two candidates by an argument they hold, and not by looking at the world, as when they see that the myth about winter assumes its own answer, does that candidate count as ruled out for them, until the argument is shown wrong? And does that hold for explanations about the world, such as the seasons, as well as for non-scientific ones?"*

### 1.2 Second choice: is "offered" what the owner means by "discovered"?

**What file 93 asks.** "you said, "If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem." *Is "offered … in place of" what you mean by "discovered"?*" The example is the gardener's two rescues, each put forward to save her explanation, neither put forward to replace the other.

**What the theory says now.** Two candidates are rivals "when one of them has been offered as an answer to \(p\) in place of the other and they conflict at some admitted edit–boundary pair of the target that both their transports translate, in \(C\) or outside it" (L315); and "No list of all rivals is supposed: a candidate's rivals are among the candidates someone has offered, and a candidate that nobody has offered is no one's rival." (L315). The checker found that the first clause alone can be read narrowly (one must be offered to replace that particular other), the second sentence reads it broadly (both offered for one question and conflicting), and no case's verdict turns on the difference (X09, "For the owner", (d) item 4). File 92 takes the broad reading. On either reading, a variation someone has thought of but not put forward is no one's rival.

**The owner's words that bear on it.**

- "candidate explanations attempt to solve a problem. Both may appear to solve it."
- "choosing one, for whatever reason, means that the person doing the choosing sees no option but to choose the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument"

**Reading.**

1. *The broad reading fits the owner's picture.* The owner's two candidates are both attempts at one problem, and the person chooses one of them. Neither is said to have been put forward to replace the other. Choosing one of two attempts at the same problem is choosing it in place of the other, whoever proposed each. So the owner's picture is the broad reading. This is a lean, not an answer: the owner was not addressing the wording.
2. *It raises a point file 93 did not ask.* The owner's word in decision S20 was "discovered", and the S94 statement centres on "the person doing the choosing". A variation that the chooser has thought of, and weighs against another, is on the most natural reading "discovered" by her. The theory's "someone has offered" does not say whether a person's own thinking of a candidate as an answer counts as offering it. If it does not, a variation the chooser has discovered but told no one would be no one's rival, which is at odds with the owner's word. If it does, "offered" renders "discovered" closely. Nothing in draft 5 decides this: "offered" is not defined.
3. *Nothing in the statement touches whether a variation nobody has thought of could matter.* Decision S20 already answers that: the owner said that listing them all is not possible, "even in principle", and "Whether anyone can list all variations that still fit is beside the point."
4. The owner says "I don't really understand the rest of the conflicts", which most plausibly covers this choice. So the choice is **left open**, with a plain explanation in section 4.2 and a question there.

**If the owner answers yes to both halves (broad, and the chooser's own thinking counts)**, a possible wording, which would be a change of claim needing an entry, a declaration and a checker: at L315 replace "when one of them has been offered as an answer to \(p\) in place of the other and they conflict" with "when each has been offered as an answer to \(p\), by anyone, the assessor included, and they conflict", and add after "a candidate that nobody has offered is no one's rival.": "An assessor who forms a candidate as an answer to \(p\) has offered it, to that assessor." This is a sketch for a checker, not tested wording. Two cautions for that entry: pieces offered together as one answer are one candidate, not two answers, so the wording keeps interference (L309) out of rivalry; and "a candidate that nobody has offered is no one's rival" stays as it is. If the owner answers yes to the broad reading only, the smaller change is none: the checker found the next sentence already states the broad reading.

### 1.3 Third choice: should "easy to vary" point at one side?

**What file 93 asks.** "*Are you content that, on the Greeks' own question, the tilt counts as easy to vary against the myth for as long as the myth fits, with a finer question as the place where a test decides?*"

**What the theory says now.** "A candidate is **easy to vary**, in the sense used here, when it and a rival pose a problem of the second kind; the rival is then easy to vary too, and the term says nothing about which of them is right." (L317). "A finer contract that contains a change at which two rivals conflict makes a new question (Part III), on which, offered for it, they pose a problem of the first kind while both fit" (L317). "Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates." (L317). All four S93 verdicts called this harmless, and the checkers found the wording states it truly (tabulation §6.1; X10 "For the owner").

**The owner's words that bear on it.**

- "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached."
- "Therefore further investigation is required the conflict and potentially solve the problem."
- "Therefore, whatever happens to the candidates is up to the person doing the choosing. Notice I never once claimed what must happen."
- "the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument"

**Reading.**

1. *The shape matches the present wording.* While both survive, the owner says, no resolution has been reached, and further investigation may solve it. That is L317: while both fit, "easy to vary" is said of both and "says nothing about which of them is right", and the finer question is where a test can decide. The owner gives the theory no ground to pick a side while both survive: what happens to the candidates is the chooser's business.
2. *Where the owner puts the one-sidedness.* The owner's chooser picks "the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument". The asymmetry between tilt and myth comes from an argument that rules the myth out, not from a label. In the theory that is whether the myth fits, which is the first choice (section 1.1, point 5), and it is what Mimo argued on X10: the asymmetry is carried by (E) and by the finer question, not by the label.
3. *What is not settled.* The owner did not say *yes, content*, and says they do not understand "the rest of the conflicts". Reading the statement as an answer would over-read it.

**Status: partly settled, towards *yes, content*.** No change proposed. A plain explanation is in section 4.3.

## 2. The chooser

The owner puts "the person doing the choosing" at the centre: fallible ("there is no such thing as an infallible creative agent"), constrained ("not enough time, the crop needs harvesting, I need to recharge my electronic brain, whatever"), free to set a problem aside ("If the person decides the problem is a low priority, then this whole process may be abandoned"), and choosing "the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument" as they see it.

### 2.1 Who stands where the chooser stands

Draft 5 has no "person", "agent", "chooser" or "choice" (a search of the text finds none). Four figures share the chooser's place.

- **The assessor** (Part VI and Part VIII): the one for whom a result "is **established**" by "a usable receipt" (L315), and for whom a candidate "**fits**" (L315); two rivals that both fit "pose, for that assessor, a **problem for \(p\)**" (L317). This is the owner's "sees".
- **The system** (Parts IX to XIII): it holds representations, represents difficulties, runs episodes and claims obligations. "A **recognized difficulty** is a failure of a claimed obligation, or a conflict in which what the system holds meets a claimed obligation only by failing a protected one (Part XI), when the system represents it." (L429).
- **The thinker** of Part I: "A thinker may act on an appraisal without certifying it." (L71).
- **The modeller**, who declares what the semantics takes as given: "A **declared** contract is stipulated by the modeller." (L155). The modeller is not a chooser among candidates; it is whoever writes the model down.

The owner's single person is split by the theory into the assessor (what it sees) and the system (what it does). That split does no harm to the owner's picture: one person can be both, and the theory never says they must differ.

### 2.2 "sees no option", not "has no option"

The theory has this distinction exactly, for fitting against being an account.

- *What the chooser sees* is what fits what is established for them (L315). It is relative to the assessor and to the receipts the assessor holds, and it can lapse: "If a premise about them ceases to be live, the receipt is not usable and the exclusion ceases to be established" (L369).
- *What is the case* does not depend on anyone: "Whatever the target does there, at most one of them is an account of \(p\), whether or not anyone can establish what it does" (L317); "whether a candidate is an account of a question is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world, not by when anyone first asks the question or by whether anyone has checked the candidate against it" (L317); "Whether a transport is faithful on a contract is independent of whether anyone accepts it." (L67).

The owner's parenthesis, "(note: “sees no option”, not “has no option”)", can be read two ways, and the theory has a counterpart for each.

- *Reading 1, about knowledge: what the chooser sees may not be how things are.* Then "sees no option" is the theory's *fits, for that assessor*, and the warning against "has no option" is the theory's refusal to let fitting decide what is an account. The owner's science sentence, "It must match reality, but not by some fixed infallible metric", is the same split again: being an account is fixed by the world; anyone's showing of it is fallible.
- *Reading 2, about freedom: the chooser is not compelled, and could choose otherwise.* Then the counterpart is that the theory contains no rule of choice: "A thinker may act on an appraisal without certifying it." (L71), and nothing in draft 5 says which candidate anyone must adopt (sections 2.3 and 2.4).

The two readings do not compete; the owner may mean both, and the theory agrees with both.

### 2.3 Does the theory model choosing, adopting or holding a candidate?

It models putting a candidate forward, using it, holding it and deciding to stop. It does not model choosing one candidate over another, and it gives no rule for which to choose.

- *Putting forward:* rivals need that "one of them has been offered as an answer to \(p\) in place of the other" (L315). "Offered" is not defined.
- *Using:* "\(\operatorname{Attempt}(s,c,p,h,e)\) the relation that \(c\) is actually used to address \(p\)" (L419); a system deploys a content when it "holds a representation of \(c\) … integrated into problem-directed activity" (L403).
- *Holding:* "what the system holds" (L429).
- *Responding to reasons:* a response uses a reason when, among other things, it "sends content changes to the changes specified by the operative deliberative rule" (L385). The theory takes the system's own way of deciding as it finds it and never supplies or grades it. That is the owner's "for whatever reason".
- *Adopting:* "the reasons for adopting it are assessed elsewhere (Part IX)" (L277), and Part IX assesses them only as criticisms and arguments (bearing, reason use, standing), never as a verdict on the adoption.
- *Stopping:* "Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof." (L429).

### 2.4 Does it make any choice "wrong" or "irrational"?

No. "rational" and "irrational" do not occur. "wrong" occurs five times, never of a choice: of what a system can be wrong about, its transports, observations, criticisms and capacities (L67); of a question (L157); of a decomposition (L245); of an anchor (L281); and of a claim that one rival is right and the other wrong (L317). The sentences that sound like rules bind claims and records, not what a person does with candidates:

- "What is prohibited is changing \(C\) or \(\mathcal Q\) during an assessment without recording that the claim has changed." (L161): a rule on how an assessment is recorded.
- "Losses outside \(P\) must be exposed." (L441): a condition on a claim of repair.
- "A criticism that a candidate is easy to vary must supply such a rival" (L317), and a claim "that one is right and the other wrong … must supply it" (L317): what a criticism or claim must contain to be what it says.
- Goalpost-moving "is a failure of the record" (L608).

Reader A's sweep of every "must" (its section 3) reaches the same result, and this reading repeated it (section 6).

### 2.5 Parts X and XI against "They're all valid choices"

**The one mention of choosing.** "A problem that a system represents can be a recognized difficulty (Part X), as when choosing one of two rivals meets a claimed obligation only by dropping the other, which fits as well, where keeping every rival that fits is among the protected obligations (Part XI)." (L317).

- *It is conditional on the chooser's own safeguard.* Choosing between two fitting rivals is a recognized difficulty only "where keeping every rival that fits is among the protected obligations". The obligations are the system's own: "claimed" (L429), and "The obligations are declared inputs" (L441). The theory does not say anyone should protect keeping every fitting rival.
- *What follows is an attribution, not a verdict on the choice.* A contribution that drops a protected rival makes no repair under those obligations, because (P) asks that every protected condition held throughout (L441). That says which attribution holds. It does not call the choice invalid, and "(P) does not rank alternatives" and "Their declaration makes no claim that the aims are worth pursuing" (L441).
- *The chooser may change its obligations.* They are "fixed for the comparison" (L435), as a contract is fixed for an assessment. Changing them makes a new comparison, as a new contract makes a new question; within one comparison they are fixed by definition, as a contract is within an assessment (L161).

**Abandoning, and fading away.** The owner: "this entire episode may never resolve, and fade into recesses of that person's history". The theory separates two things here. A problem for \(p\) "stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting" (L317): it is a relation between two rivals and what that assessor has established, and it stays unsolved whether or not anyone is working on it. A recognized difficulty needs the system to represent it ("when the system represents it", L429): a problem the person has put aside and forgotten is no longer one. So the theory already has the owner's "may never resolve" (the problem stands) and "fade" (no longer represented), and it counts neither as a fault. It defines only a complete episode; an incomplete one is not called a failure.

**Constraints.** The owner's time, harvest and recharging have a counterpart in Parts XII and XIII: a capability is attributed "under a declared boundary (which processes and resources are the system's)" (L473) and "under the same continuity and resource contract" (L475), and a person who leaves a problem for the harvest shows no inability: "A narrow retained use is what it is: it establishes neither the wider understanding it falls short of nor a permanent inability to reach it." (L403); "A finite list of failures is not a barrier proof" (L495).

**Whether the choices were rational.** The owner: "Whether they're rational may or may not ever be opened and examined by the creative person/agent." The theory: any aspect of practice "can become a target" (L73), and is scrutinizable "when there is an owned, admitted continuation in which a description of \(d\) becomes a represented target" (L487). That is a capacity, not a duty; nothing requires the examination to happen. The "must" in L73 ("the result must be able to change how the system proceeds") is part of what counts as operative scrutiny, not an order to scrutinize.

**Result: agreement, no clash.** The theory neither validates nor invalidates choices; the owner's word "valid" has no counterpart in it and needs none. The one place a reader could mistake for a clash is L317's last sentence, if read without its condition. In the final answer to the owner it should be put plainly: choosing one of two candidates that both survive is a difficulty, in the theory, only for a person who has set themselves the rule of keeping every candidate that survives.

### 2.6 What the statement adds on the chooser

One thing, and small. The theory never says outright that it says nothing about what is to be done with candidates, and never says outright that choosing one of two rivals that both fit leaves the problem unsolved. Both follow from its definitions: choosing shows neither rival failing, so both still fit, and the problem stands (L317). But the only sentence that mentions choosing (L317, the last) presents it as a possible difficulty, and the owner's emphasis ("Notice I never once claimed what must happen", "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached") is the missing counterpart. Reader A proposes a sentence for L317 (its A3). This reading agrees, and offers a variant that states only what the theory does and does not say, without a new assertion about who owns choices:

> "Nothing here says what anyone is to do with rivals or with a problem. Choosing one of two rivals that both fit shows neither of them failing, so the problem for \(p\) stands; to stop inquiring is a decision (Part X)."

Where it goes: at L317, after "Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates." and before "A problem that a system represents can be a recognized difficulty". Placed there, it also frames the last sentence, so that the one mention of choosing is read with its condition. It is true under the present definitions (L315, L317, L429), names no list, count, grade or record, and moves no case verdict that this reading can find; as a new explicit sentence it is a change of claim and needs an entry and a declaration.

## 3. The leftover: "Boundary" in Part VI

### 3.1 What draft 5 says

L299 ends: "For a declared family \(\mathcal V\) of organization edits," and L302 reads:

> \(\operatorname{Boundary}_{E,p}=\{(v,w)\in\mathcal V^2:\operatorname{Account}(E_v,p)\neq\operatorname{Account}(E_w,p)\}. \tag{D}\)

In words: take a family of changed versions of the candidate, declared in advance; Boundary is the set of pairs of versions of which one is an account of \(p\) and the other is not. \(\mathcal V\), \(E_v\) and "organization edits" occur nowhere else in draft 5 (checked by program, section 6; the \(\mathcal V_A\) of L455 is a different symbol, the aesthetic values). The label (D) occurs once more, in the dependence order: "(S), (B), (D) depend on (E)." (L526).

### 3.2 Where it came from

It glossed it: "The boundary records where an account survives or fails; it does not assign a probability or a score." (file 00, L294). File 00 then defined, over the same kind of family, "Pres" and the hard-to-vary containment (H).
- **Files 10 and 11** kept (D) and put the hard-to-vary lemma over the same \(\mathcal V\) a few lines later: "\(\operatorname{Pres}(F)=\{v\in\mathcal V:\forall f\in F,\operatorname{Account}(E_v,f)\}\)" (file 11, L315). The gloss "records where an account survives or fails" was not carried into them, and is not in draft 5.
- **The correction-sticks analysis (24 September)**, whose lemma is the one lesson S26 records as Claude's mis-framing, used (D) as the record of a correction: its "Correction in a common family" concluded "\((1,v_0)\in\operatorname{Boundary}_{E_1,f^*}\) (D)", and its check of that clause said the "link to correction" "exists already" in (D) because the boundary "records where an account survives or fails" (analysis, around its lines 2915 and 3045). That is the only use of (D) this reading found in the project's analyses since file 00.
- **Draft 4** dropped Pres and kept (D). The change list says why the gap was only narrowed: "\(\mathcal V\) now occurs only as the domain of (D), \(\operatorname{Boundary}_{E,p}\), where whether a pair \((v,w)\) belongs is fixed by \(v\) and \(w\) alone. No claim about a candidate or a pair of edits turns on which family is declared, and no case or other definition uses (D). It is still not among Part XIV's declared inputs (carried forward)." (change list, L1243).
- **The S93 readers** noticed it and did not press it. Atria: the text "still has `For a declared family 𝒱 of organization edits` in (D)", and with it "a family-of-versions framing", but "Boundary is not used to state it anywhere in the excerpts given" (it being hard to vary; the packet held excerpts, not the whole text). Mimo: (D) "compares \(\operatorname{Account}(E_v,p)\) across \(v\) — a family of versions in effect", but "states no variation, no hard-to-vary, counts and grades nothing" (S93 returns, part D, Atria point 1 and Mimo point 4).

### 3.3 What it is

Let \(P=\{v\in\mathcal V:\operatorname{Account}(E_v,p)\}\), the versions in the family that are still accounts of \(p\). This is file 11's \(\operatorname{Pres}(\{p\})\). Then a pair belongs to Boundary exactly when one of its two versions is in \(P\) and the other is not:

\[
\operatorname{Boundary}_{E,p}=\big(P\times(\mathcal V\setminus P)\big)\cup\big((\mathcal V\setminus P)\times P\big).
\]

## 4. Plain explanations

*For the final plain-words answer. Everyday example first, then what the theory says, then what hangs on it. The words below are used in one sense each.*

- **candidate**: an explanation someone puts forward as an answer (the owner's own word).
- **question**: what the candidates try to answer. In the statement the owner calls this "a problem" ("candidate explanations attempt to solve a problem"). Here "problem" is kept for the theory's sense, below, so that it has one meaning.
- **situation**: one particular way things could be, such as a dry spring with an early-ripening variety planted.
- **range**: the situations a question covers.
- **the person choosing**: the owner's "the person doing the choosing". (The theory's word is "assessor"; file 92's is "judge". The final answer should pick one and keep it.)
- **fits**: nothing the person choosing has found out shows it failing. This is the owner's "survive".
- **account**: a candidate that meets all four of the theory's requirements for the question; what the theory counts as a real explanation of it. Whether a candidate is an account is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world, not by what anyone has found out.
- **test**: finding out what actually happens in one situation.
- **conflict**, **rival**, **problem**, **easy to vary**: explained below.

### 4.1 What "conflict" means in the theory

**The example.** A gardener's south bed always ripens first, because the south wall catches the most sun. One summer the north bed ripens first. Two rescues are put forward. Her son says: the neighbour planted an early-ripening variety along the north side. Her sister says: in a wet spring the north bed ripens first. Both agree with every summer the gardener has seen. Now picture a dry spring in a year when the early variety is planted. The son's rescue says the north bed ripens first; the sister's says it does not. In that situation they cannot both be right. **That is a conflict: two candidates conflict in a situation when both say something about it and they cannot both be right about it.**

There are two ways to conflict.

- They give different answers there, as the two rescues do in a dry spring with the early variety.
- Or they give the same answer, but disagree about how it comes about, so that whatever the world is really like, at most one of them is right. Two explanations of why a lamp is lit might both say "lit", while one says the current runs through a certain wire and the other says it does not. The wire either carries the current or it does not.

Some pairs do not conflict anywhere: the same explanation in other words ("a wet spring" and "a rainy spring"); a fuller and a shorter telling of the same thing; or a dog and a turtle said to hold up the universe from outside it, where nothing anyone could ever see or do depends on which is true, so the difference is in the words only.

**What hangs on it.** Two candidates are rivals only if both are put forward as answers to the same question and they conflict somewhere. And where they conflict decides what could settle it.

- If they conflict in a situation the question covers, a test there settles it: plant the early variety and wait for a dry spring. Afterwards at most one of them fits, whatever happens.
- If they conflict only in situations the question does not cover (say the gardener's question is only about the summers she has already seen), nothing inside that question can settle it. A wider question, one that covers the dry spring, is a new question, and there a test can decide. On the narrow question itself the two stay unresolved, unless one of them is shown failing for a reason of its own.

**The link to your statement.** Your "Both may appear to solve it" and "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached" are, in the theory, two rivals that both fit. The theory calls that a **problem**. Your "further investigation" is, in the theory, a test in a situation where they conflict, or a wider question that covers one.

**A note on the word.** In the theory, "conflict" means only this: two candidates that cannot both be right in some situation, whether or not anyone has noticed. It is not a disagreement between people, and it is not the name of the open points in file 93, which file 93 calls "choices".

### 4.2 The second choice: is "offered" what you mean by "discovered"?

**The example.** The gardener again. Her son and her sister each put their rescue forward, separately, to save the gardener's explanation. Neither put theirs forward to replace the other's. The two rescues conflict (a dry spring with the early variety). Are they rivals?

The theory's words: two candidates are rivals when one "has been offered as an answer to \(p\) in place of the other", and they conflict.

- *Read narrowly*, the sister would have had to put hers forward specifically instead of the son's. She did not, so they are not rivals, and there is no problem.
- *Read broadly*, both were put forward as answers to the same question (why did the north bed ripen first?), so each stands in place of the other. They are rivals, and, since both fit, they make a problem.

The theory's next sentence ("a candidate's rivals are among the candidates someone has offered") supports the broad reading, and file 92 uses it. No verdict on any of the project's cases changes between the two.

**A second part.** That night the gardener thinks: "or perhaps it was the warm wind that spring". She tells no one. As the theory is worded now, this third rescue is nobody's rival, because nobody has put it forward. Your word, on 24 September, was "discovered": "If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem." She has discovered it. Does thinking of it count?

**What hangs on it.**

- Whether two rescues put forward separately, for the same question, make a problem; and so whether each can be called "easy to vary" against the other.
- Whether a variation the person choosing has thought of, but not said aloud, can make a problem for her.
- It changes no case's verdict. A variation nobody has thought of stays outside either way, as your decision says.

**What your statement says about it.** Your picture, "candidate explanations attempt to solve a problem" and "the person doing the choosing" chooses one, fits the broad reading: two attempts at one question, one chosen in place of the other. It does not say whether a variation the person has only thought of counts.

**The question, in two parts.** (a) Do two candidates put forward separately for the same question count as rivals, if they cannot both be right? (b) Does a variation that the person choosing has thought of count, even if she has told no one?

### 4.3 The third choice: should "easy to vary" point at one side?

**The example.** The Greeks' question: why does winter come every year, in the seasons they knew? One candidate: the Earth's axis is tilted, so for part of the year Greece leans away from the sun. Another: Persephone spends part of each year underground, and the world grows cold. For every year the Greeks saw, both say the same: winter comes in the same months. They come apart only far south of the equator, which the Greeks' question did not cover. There the tilt says winter comes in the months of the Greek summer, while the myth says the whole world is cold at once.

Suppose the myth still fits for someone: nothing they have found out shows it failing. (Whether it does is the first choice.) Then on the Greeks' question both survive, and nothing inside that question can tell them apart. The theory then calls each of them "easy to vary" against the other: the tilt as well as the myth. The label means only this: on this question, as it stands, nothing can tell these two apart. It says nothing about which one is right, and the tilt can be an account of the Greeks' question throughout; the myth is not one, since it assumes its answer.

What decides between them is a wider question, one that covers the southern seasons. There they conflict inside the question, and a test decides: find out when winter comes in the south. On the Greeks' own question the label stays for as long as the myth fits, because what happens in the south lies outside that question.

**Why not point the label at the myth alone?** To say, on the Greeks' own question, "the tilt is hard to vary and the myth is easy to vary", the theory would need a way to mark one of two survivors as worse. The only ways anyone has found are counting versions or grading, which your decision of 24 September ruled out. Both outside readers argued this. Neither found another way, though no one has shown that none exists.

**What hangs on it.**

- Only the label on the Greeks' own question. No verdict about which candidate is right changes, and the tilt can be an account either way.
- If your answer to the first choice is "yes" (an argument showing that the myth assumes its own answer rules it out), then the myth no longer survives for anyone who has seen that argument, and this example goes away: the tilt is not easy to vary against it. The label would still apply to pairs like the gardener's two rescues, on the summers she has seen.

**What your statement says about it.** "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached. Therefore further investigation is required": that is how the theory handles it now. While both survive, the label picks no side, and the wider question is where further investigation can decide. You also put the one-sidedness where the theory puts it: in the argument that rules one candidate out ("the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument"), which is the first choice, not in the label.

**The question.** Are you content that, while two candidates both survive on a question, the theory calls both "easy to vary" on that question and picks no side, and leaves the deciding to a wider question and its test?
