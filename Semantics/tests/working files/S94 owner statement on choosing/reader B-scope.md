## 0. In short

**Does the statement add anything? Through this lens, yes on one point, and it brings two others into view.**

1. **It gives a direction on file 93's first choice.** The owner writes that the chooser "sees no option but to choose the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument". On every reading, what rules a candidate out there is an argument, and nothing asks that the argument come from looking at the world. That is the "yes" answer to the first choice: a failure found by examining a candidate counts. Whether it settles the choice *for file 93's own example* (the myth against the tilt) depends on a reading the owner's words leave open (section 2.5). One line back to the owner would close it.
2. **It speaks of "non scientific theories", which the theory never names.** The theory has one definition of an account for every question. It already reaches mathematics, rules and purposes, and it reaches worth only by taking the standard of worth as given. Its wording ties every range of changes to "the physically admitted changes", and that reads awkwardly for a target that is not physical, such as a proof (finding B2).
3. **Its science clause matches the theory.** "It must match reality, but not by some fixed infallible metric" is what the theory already holds. Its test for an account is a yes/no condition, not a measure. Whether a candidate meets it is a fact about the candidate, the question and the world. Anyone's knowledge of that fact is fallible and withdrawable. The theory does not say in words that "the adopted physics" it relies on is itself fallible, and on one reading the owner's "reality itself" adds that (finding B8).

The rest of the statement, on choosing, abandoning, constraints and tentativeness, restates in the owner's words what the theory already says, or stays outside what it claims (findings B4–B7, B9–B11). Three of the owner's words are used in senses the theory does not give them: "problem", "survive" and "match" (finding B5, section 2.5).

## 1. Scope: what draft 5 covers, and what it assumes about the world

### 1.1 The target

An explanation is "held to a target by a **transport** that is checked only by **what changes when things are changed**" (L11). A question names its target: "\(D\) is the target" (L141). The target is an organization, meaning slots, pieces and admitted edits (Part II). The theory ties the admitted edits to the physics in four places:

- "The physical theory fixes which changes are possible at all. A contract is a declared subset of those." (L43)
- "A contract is a declared subset of the physically admitted changes, and a stated scope is what makes it one." (L159)
- Non-vacuity: the contract "is a declared subset of the physically admitted edits, and every physically admitted edit excluded from \(C\) is excluded by a stated scope, not silently." (L257) Also: "An account whose only substantive contrast is one that no physically admitted edit realizes fails non-vacuity by having a silently narrowed contract." (L275)
- Rivals conflict at a pair when each could meet (F1), (F2) and (A) there "under some relations of the target that the adopted physics admits (Part I)" (L315).

"Objectivity lives in the physics and the fidelity facts; scope-honesty lives in the record." (L43)

### 1.2 Which non-scientific explanations it covers

The theory never says "scientific" or "non-scientific". It applies one definition, (E), to every question. By kind of target:

- **Mathematics: covered.** "An admitted change need not be a physical intervention. Removing an axiom, dropping a constraint or changing a dimension are edits on an organization, and fidelity under them is well defined." (L49) Part VII works a proof as an account; the "Contract: remove skewness; remove oddness; remove both" (L343) ends "The expansion is an account." And: "depth is question-relative and (E) measures none of it" (L277).
- **Rules and constitutive status: covered.** "a rule's application changes when the rule is edited and not when the world is intervened on" (L57). "Whether the rule governs a practice, and whether it should, are separate questions with separate contracts." (L347)
- **Practical questions: covered in form.** "What a question asks, production, identification, obstruction, rule-status or purpose-achievement, is fixed by the type of" query (L151). Part XI gives achievement of a purpose, (AR).
- **Worth, aesthetics and, by the same route, morals: covered only with the standard of worth taken as given.** The normative relation is "taken as an input wherever a question invokes worth" (L31); "The semantics does not derive \(\mathcal N\)" (L455); "It is taken as an input and never derived" (L518); and the theory "does not supply an objective aesthetics, a probability of truth, a merit function, a measure of worth, or a ranking of thinkers" (L25). The theory can say, *given* a standard, whether a candidate is an account. It cannot say which of two candidates that differ on the standard itself is ruled out. Moral explanation is not named.
- **History: not named.** "Values of ports may be paths, functions, fields, proofs or histories." (L105) A historical explanation is a candidate like any other, with a physical target (what happened).
- **Philosophy: not named, but the theory is one.** It answers eleven objections by argument (Part 0, "Grievances, anticipated"). It also counts an argument against itself: its list of defeats includes "**A mathematical error.** A counterexample to the finite monotone theorem" and the other listed proofs (L546). "None is protected by notation, by the availability of this document, or by any version label." (L534) So the theory already treats its own claims as open to being ruled out by argument, without any observation.

### 1.3 The theory's split is by condition, not by kind of theory

The owner's statement opens "In the case of non scientific theories" and gives science its own clause: "The case for science is reality itself." The theory makes no split by kind of theory. Its split runs *inside* (E), between kinds of condition:

- **(F1), (F2) and (A)** compare the candidate with its target under the changes. For a physical target, that means with the world.
- **Non-circular dependence** is read off the candidate's own structure: "The target's answer does not appear, at the declared grain, as an unanalysed boundary input or as a component" (L255), and deleting a block of commitments must lose the contrast. **Non-vacuity** is about the target and the stated range.

A scientific candidate can fail the second kind (Carla's almanac in file 92, section 4.3, is a physical question). A mathematical candidate is checked on the first kind against a mathematical target. So "ruled out by argument" and "held to reality" are not, in the theory, two classes of theory. They are two kinds of condition that every candidate faces. This is what makes the owner's opening restriction matter for the first choice (section 2.5).

### 1.4 A tension the owner's scope brings into view

L49 says an admitted change "need not be a physical intervention". L159, L257 and L275 say every contract is a subset of "the physically admitted" changes or edits, and L315 reads conflict through "relations of the target that the adopted physics admits". File 92 carries the same pair: "Changes need not be physical" (section 3.2), and the range "is a stated selection from the **possible** changes, those the laws of physics allow" (section 4.3). Two readings:

- **(a)** "Physically admitted" means an edit that a carrier of the organization can undergo, as the physics permits (Part I: "Every attribution of an organization to a physical system must be permitted by the adopted physics", L75). Removing skewness from a written matrix is then physically admitted, and the skew-symmetric account meets non-vacuity.
- **(b)** "Physically admitted" means a change the laws of physics allow *in the target*. A mathematical target then has no physically admitted edits at all. L275, read literally, would fail the skew-symmetric account for non-vacuity ("no physically admitted edit realizes" its contrasts), against L343's "The expansion is an account."

For a proof, a rule, a moral question or any other non-physical target, which reading holds decides whether the non-vacuity and conflict clauses apply as written. The statement does not settle this. It raises it only by speaking of non-scientific theories. See finding B2.

## 2. Ruled out "by its best argument": receipts, "established", and the first choice

### 2.1 How the theory rules a candidate out

"Ruled out" is not a term of the theory. Three notions sit near it.

1. **In fact.** A candidate that fails a condition of (E) is no account, whether anyone knows it or not: "whether a candidate is an account of a question is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world, not by when anyone first asks the question or by whether anyone has checked the candidate against it (Parts I and V)" (L317).
2. **For an assessor.** "A result is **established** for an assessor who holds a usable receipt for it (Part IX); by (K3), a result that tells against a candidate tells against it only together with the background and instruments of the test that yields it. A candidate **fits** what is established for an assessor when no result established for that assessor shows it failing a condition of (E)." (L315) A candidate is ruled out for an assessor when it stops fitting. Two rivals that both fit pose a problem, which "stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting" (L317). "A failed answer stays failed" (L369) is the case where the result is the target's answer at a pair.
3. **By criticism.** "A criticism has target \(z\), alleged defect \(\delta\), grounds \(g\), and a connection." (L377) Its connection is taken as a candidate for "the question whether \(z\) has \(\delta\) in respect of \(p\)" (L377), and the criticism *bears* exactly when that connection is an account of that question (K1). This is the theory's own form for an argument against a candidate. On file 92's reading its target is the criticized item itself ("The fault question's target is her draft", section 11.3). But (K1) is **not linked** to "established" or "fits". No sentence says that a criticism which bears, held by an assessor, is a result established for that assessor.

### 2.2 Is an argument a receipt?

A receipt is itself an argument in the theory's sense. "An evidence leaf is a reference to an event with an interpreted claim. A receipt is a derivation tree over leaves." (L397) Receipts must be usable, and (K2) is stated "For argument application \(u\) with essential premises" (L387). So the question is not whether an argument can be a receipt, since every receipt is one. The question is **whether an argument's leaves may point at the candidate** rather than at the world.

An argument that the myth assumes its answer has one leaf: the myth as offered, with the interpreted claim "the yearly return is written into the bargain". Its derivation runs from that leaf and the definition of non-circular dependence to "the myth fails non-circular dependence". It is not "reconstructed from the claim it is meant to support" (L397), because it reads the myth, not the verdict.

### 2.3 Under the present wording, is a failure found by examining a candidate "established"?

**No sentence says so, and none says not.** The S93 checkers found the same (ruling X09, ruling 5; ruling X10, ruling 2; ruling X14, "For the owner"). The definitions admit it, and three textual points support that. The first two were not among the points the S93 papers cite:

- "An assessment is an event with a frozen contract." (L55) A question's "formulation is still an event" (L161). So the offering and the examining of a candidate are events that a leaf can point at.
- (K1) already treats a finding about a candidate as the answer to a question *about that candidate*. Establishing it would follow L369's pattern ("a usable receipt for the target's answer"), with the candidate as the target.
- Receipts are general over propositions: "For \(\phi\), …" (L397). The "fits" clause reaches "a condition of (E)", and non-circular dependence is one, which no test of the world shows (L315).

Two phrases lean the other way: the (K3) gloss speaks of "the test that yields it" (L315), and L369 ties "established" to "a usable receipt for the target's answer at \((a,b)\)". So under the present wording such a failure is established only on a reading the definitions admit, not by anything the text states.

### 2.4 What the owner's words say

The relevant sentences, exactly:

- "But choosing one, for whatever reason, means that the person doing the choosing sees no option but to choose the one that isn't ruled out by its best argument (note: “sees no option”, not “has no option”)."
- "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached."
- "Therefore further investigation is required the conflict and potentially solve the problem."

In the theory's words, the owner's "survive" is **fits**. The theory's own "survives" is said only of selected transports ("A transport **survives on \(H\)** when it is a member of \(\mathcal T\) that meets the survival condition on \(H\)", L195), a different sense. "Ruled out", for the chooser, is **shown failing a condition of (E) by a result established for that chooser**. The owner names an argument as what rules a candidate out, and names no observation. So, on the owner's words, a candidate that argument has ruled out no longer survives, which is to say it no longer fits. That is the "yes" answer to the first choice.

**"Its best argument" has two readings.** On both, a failure found by argument counts.

- *(i) The strongest argument that bears on the candidate.* The candidate is chosen against when the best argument about it rules it out.
- *(ii) The candidate's own best case for itself.* The candidate is ruled out by its own best argument, as the myth is: its bargain, the part meant to explain winter, already contains the yearly return it is meant to explain.

**"Best" is not a grade on either reading.** The theory ranks no candidates ("Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates", L317), and a criticism is judged as a candidate is (K1). So "best" can only be the chooser's own judgement of which argument bears, the owner's "sees". It cannot be a ranking the theory supplies. (This is the check lesson S26 asks for before building on a new framing. No list, count, grade or record enters.)

### 2.5 Does it settle the first choice?

**The direction, yes. The file-93 example, not by the owner's words alone.** The owner opened with "In the case of non scientific theories" and gave science its own clause: "The case for science is reality itself. It must match reality, but not by some fixed infallible metric." Two readings:

- **R1, a split by kind of theory.** In non-scientific theories argument rules a candidate out; in science, reality is "the case". Then the statement settles the first choice "yes" for non-scientific candidates. For file 93's example it settles it only if the myth counts as a non-scientific theory, and the owner does not say that. The question the myth is offered for, why winter comes every year, is a question about the world. The science clause neither admits nor excludes a failure found by examining.
- **R2, a split by kind of condition.** In file 92, which the owner reads, "Matching" is the name of the first requirement, (F1) and (F2), and "No assumed answer" is the third (file 92, section 4.3). On this reading, "It must match reality" is said of the requirements that compare a candidate with the world. "Ruled out by its best argument" covers failures that argument finds, such as an assumed answer, in any theory. Then the first choice is settled "yes" for every candidate, the myth included. The myth does not fit once someone has examined it and holds the finding, so it and the tilt pose no problem, and the tilt is not easy to vary against it (file 93, first and third choices).

The owner's words do not decide between R1 and R2. Neither reading gives any ground for the "no" answer, and nothing in the statement says a failure found by argument should not count. (None of the six outside replies argued that either; file 93, section 6.) **Suggested one-line question back to the owner:** *"When you said a candidate is ruled out 'by its best argument', did you mean that for every explanation, including one about the world such as the myth about winter, or only for non-scientific theories?"*

### 2.6 What else the owner's words bear on: whether such a finding can be withdrawn

The six settling proposals differ on whether (K3) qualifies a finding by examination (ruling X09, "For the owner"). Proposals 1 and 6 say yes: the examination is a receipt, usable only while its premises are live, and it tells against the candidate only together with them. Proposal 3 says no, because the failure "rests on no background or instrument".

The owner writes that the chooser "sees", that "there is no such thing as an infallible creative agent", and that "“matches the problem” is always tentative and may be wrong". An examination can misread a candidate. Whether a component is the answer written in is judged "at the declared grain" (L255), and an examiner can get the grain or the reading wrong. The owner's words therefore favour a settlement in which such a finding is withdrawable like any other (proposals 1 and 6) over one that exempts it from (K3) (proposal 3). This is a bearing, not a ruling.

### 2.7 Wording, if the owner confirms "yes"

This is a change of claim, not a rewording. It needs an entry, a declaration and a checker. It brings back no list, count, grade or record: the receipt is an assessor's evidence of a failure in the candidate's own content, not a record of rescues, and L397 already refuses "A record reconstructed from the claim it is meant to support".

- In L315, "of the test that yields it" → "of the test or examination that yields it".
- After L315's "fits" sentence, insert: "Such a result may come from examining the candidate as well as from a test of the target, as when an argument shows that the candidate assumes its own answer: the leaves of its receipt refer to the candidate as offered, and, like any receipt, it is usable only while its premises, among them the reading of the candidate at the declared grain and the conditions of (E), are live (K2)."
- Alternatively, or as well, in Part IX after (K1): "An assessor who holds a usable receipt that a criticism of a candidate bears on it (K1) holds a result established against that candidate (Part VI)." This ties the theory's own form for an argument against a candidate to "established". A checker should confirm that it adds no circle to the dependence order: (K1) depends on (E), and "established" on receipts.

## 3. The science clause: a criterion is not a metric, and a fact is not anyone's knowledge of it

The owner wrote: "The case for science is reality itself. It must match reality, but not by some fixed infallible metric. Creativity is a process that may or may not lead to a metric."

### 3.1 Criterion, not metric

A criterion is a yes/no condition. A metric is a grade or a measure. The theory's test for an account is a criterion: \(\operatorname{Account}(\mathcal E)\) holds exactly when (F1), (F2), (A), non-circular dependence and non-vacuity all hold (E, L262), and "Every conjunct is a condition on how supplied relations behave under the changes in \(C\). None inspects a label." (L265) Nothing in it measures:

- "It does not supply an objective aesthetics, a probability of truth, a merit function, a measure of worth, or a ranking of thinkers." (L25)
- "depth is question-relative and (E) measures none of it" (L277).
- "Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates." (L317)
- "The semantics supplies no probability of truth, no merit function and no ranking of thinkers, and a claim that needs one is unsettled." (L522)

The words "metric" and "grade" occur in the theory only in two senses that are not measures of explanations. One is a mathematical distance declared for an approximate question ("let \(d\) be a metric on \(X_E\)", L363, with "An exact question is not silently replaced by an approximate one."). The other is the physical module's accuracy grades of *tasks*, which "form a directed preorder \(Q_\Theta\), increasingly demanding and short of perfection" (L479). Neither grades how well an explanation matches.

### 3.2 The fact of matching, and anyone's knowledge of it

- **The fact.** "Whether a transport is faithful on a contract is independent of whether anyone accepts it." (L67) Whether a candidate is an account "is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world" (L317). This is the owner's "It must match reality".
- **The knowledge.** "Systems can be wrong about their transports, their observations, their criticisms and their own capacities." (L67) "A criticism has a target and is itself conjectural." (L71) A receipt is usable only while its premises are live (K2), and "Withdrawing a premise removes a license; it does not make the conclusion false." (L393) A failed test "yields \(\neg(T\land B\land I)\) and nothing narrower" (K3, L395), which means blame is shared. Even the paragraph on failed answers says "If a premise about them ceases to be live, the receipt is not usable and the exclusion ceases to be established, for every such candidate alike; no candidate is thereby shown to be an account (K2)." (L369)
- **The criterion itself.** (E) is fixed in this draft, but it is not held infallible. It is part of "The **constitutive conjecture**" (L17). Part XV lists what would refute its sufficiency and necessity, and says "None is protected by notation, by the availability of this document, or by any version label." (L534)

So the theory has a fixed yes/no criterion whose satisfaction is a fact about the world, known only fallibly, and the criterion is itself a conjecture. **It imposes no fixed infallible metric anywhere.**

One caution on "match". If "match reality" meant only "give the world's answers", the theory asks for more: "It does not say prediction is explanation." (L23) (F1) asks that each commitment behave as its anchor does. On file 92's use of the word "matching", which is (F1) and (F2), the owner's clause and the theory agree.

### 3.3 One fixed thing the theory does not call fallible in words: the adopted physics

The theory holds candidates to the target itself (L67). It takes which changes are possible, and with them what a contract must state and where rivals can conflict, from "the adopted physics" (L75, L315). It also says "Objectivity lives in the physics and the fidelity facts" (L43). It calls physical *claims* "fallible but checkable in principle" (L53) and says "It is fallible and checkable as any physical claim is." (L481, of a selection claim). The module "adopts a task-based formulation of physics for this purpose" (L461). But it never says in words that the adopted physics may itself be wrong about what reality admits, or what becomes of a verdict when it is. Two readings of L43's "the physics":

- **Reality's laws.** Then the theory already says the owner's "The case for science is reality itself."
- **The physical theory adopted.** Then the owner's clause adds that this physics, too, answers to reality.

See finding B8.

### 3.4 "Creativity is a process that may or may not lead to a metric"

"Metric" has two readings here.

- **A measure or test procedure** (a scale, a measurement, a question with its range). The theory can represent making one: a measurement is a signature pattern (L124), and "The content \(c\) may be an organization, a transport, or a contract." (L425) Finding a question is creative (Derivation 5). Nothing requires creativity to produce any particular kind of content.
- **A standard of worth.** The theory takes one as given and "never derived" (L518). Whether a creative process produced it is outside what the theory claims.

## 4. "Matches the problem is always tentative and may be wrong"

The owner wrote: "The question of “does it need to match the problem”: yes, if resolution is the goal. But “matches the problem” is always tentative and may be wrong." (The quoted question does not appear in the project's papers. It is read here as asking whether a candidate must fit the question it is offered for.)

- **Matching the question.** Question fidelity, (A): "The query \(\mathcal Q\) is held fixed; an account of a different query is not an account of this one." (L253) Also: "Two questions with the same \(D\) and different \((C,\mathcal Q)\) are different questions" (L151).
- **"Fits" is tentative.** It is relative to an assessor and to what that assessor holds (L315). It changes as results are established or withdrawn (K2, L393; L369). A test's verdict carries its background and instruments (K3, L395).
- **A question's range is tentative.** "What makes a restriction appropriate to the question asked is a substantive, criticizable part of the claim; the semantics records the restriction and supplies no rule that certifies it." (L159) "A question may fail to pick out its alleged target, assume an incompatible baseline, or combine incompatible requirements." (L161) Contracts change across episodes; "Goalpost-moving is the act of changing the index without recording the change" (L608). The scope of a contract is a declared input (L522).
- **What is not tentative.** At a fixed question, whether a candidate is an account is a fact (L317), and it keeps its index: "A proposition indexed to a contract remains that proposition when a later theory changes the current contract. A new index is a new claim." (L367)

So the owner's "always tentative and may be wrong" has two readings. If it means *any judgement that a candidate matches is tentative*, the theory says the same. If it means *the matching itself can change at a fixed question*, the theory denies it. The owner's next sentence ("It must match reality") points to the first reading.

On "yes, if resolution is the goal": the definition of an account is not conditional on anyone's goal. Whether an account is *needed* is a goal someone states: "An epistemic obligation requires a correct account, or the correction of a use through one, to be deployable." (L443) And "Their declaration makes no claim that the aims are worth pursuing, and (P) does not rank alternatives." (L441)
