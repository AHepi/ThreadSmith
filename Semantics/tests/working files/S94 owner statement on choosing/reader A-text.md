## 0. Short answer

**Does the statement add anything?** Mostly it agrees with draft 5, and in several places it says in ordinary words what the theory already says in its own. It adds one thing the theory does not yet say outright, and it bears on two of file 93's three choices without settling them in the theory's terms.

- **Already said.** Candidates are attempts at a question; two can both "appear to solve it" (the theory's *fits*, which is relative to an assessor); if both do, a problem stands; further investigation (a *test*, or a *finer question*) may solve it; questions can be faulty; agents are fallible; acting on an appraisal needs no certificate; closing an inquiry "is a decision, not a proof"; the theory supplies no merit function, grade or ranking; matching is judged fallibly, while whether a candidate is an account is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world.
- **Not found: any conflict.** No sentence of draft 5 says what a person or system must do with candidates, rivals or problems. None treats an unsolved problem as a failure, and none assumes an infallible chooser. Every "must" in the text is a condition on what a claim, a criticism or an attribution must contain or supply, or a logical necessity (section 3).
- **Added (A3).** The theory never says outright that what happens to candidates is the chooser's choice, or that choosing one of two rivals that both fit does not solve the problem. Both follow from its definitions, and the only place it mentions choosing (the last sentence of L317) is consistent with them. One sentence would state it (proposed below).
- **Bears on choice 1 (A14).** On the most natural reading, the owner says that candidates are ruled out *by argument*, from the chooser's point of view. That leans towards counting a failure found by examining a candidate as established for the assessor who holds the argument. But the owner limits this to "non scientific theories", and the myth-and-tilt example is posed on a question about the seasons, so the statement does not settle choice 1 for that example.
- **Consistent with answering yes to choice 3 (A15); neutral on choice 2 (A16).**
- **Outside the theory, and it need not cover them:** priorities, lack of time, harvests, recharging, and whether a choice was rational. The theory can represent constraints as the resource contract and enabling conditions of a capability claim, and worth as the normative relation it takes as an input. It passes no judgment on the choices themselves.

## 3. Sweep: sentences that could conflict with "I never once claimed what must happen" and "there is no such thing as an infallible creative agent"

The whole text was searched for *must, required, requires, should, ought, need, prohibited, prefer, rank, grade, choose, decision, failure, unsettled, option, resolve, solve, settle, infallible, fallible, holds, adopt, assessor, judge*. What was found:

**Every "must".**

- L73: "can become a target, and the result must be able to change how the system proceeds." A condition on the commitment to recursive scrutiny: the result must be *able* to change practice. Scrutiny is not required to happen, which agrees with C14.
- L75: "Every attribution of an organization to a physical system must be permitted by the adopted physics." A condition on attributions.
- L223: "the world must admit changes the system's correspondence was never shaped against." A logical necessity for surprise.
- L281, L558: a rejected condition, quoted in order to be refused.
- L317 (twice): "A criticism that a candidate is easy to vary must supply such a rival (Part IX)"; a claim that one of two accounts is right "must supply" a separating change. Conditions on a criticism or a claim: what it has to contain to be that criticism or claim. Neither says what anyone must do.
- L409: "Not every representation must be simultaneously explicit." A denial.
- L441: "Losses outside \(P\) must be exposed." The nearest thing to a directive in the text. It binds how a repair claim is stated (scope honesty, like L257's "not silently"), not what the agent does. No conflict. Optionally it could be reworded as a claim ("A repair claim exposes its losses outside \(P\)"), but no reader has read it as a duty, so no change is proposed.
- L568, L572: conditions on a claim, and on membership of a population.

**Prohibitions and the record.** L161, "What is prohibited is changing \(C\) or \(\mathcal Q\) during an assessment without recording that the claim has changed", and L608, "Goalpost-moving … is a failure of the record, not a licensed operation of the semantics." Both concern how a claim is indexed, not what happens to candidates. Neither is a record of rescues (S26). No conflict.

**Grading, ranking, preferring.** Every hit is a denial: L25; L277, "It does not prefer an elegant account to a less elegant one with the same fidelity."; L317, "Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates."; L441, "(P) does not rank alternatives"; L522, "The semantics supplies no probability of truth, no merit function and no ranking of thinkers, and a claim that needs one is unsettled." The one "grade" that is not a denial is L479: the accuracy grades of the physical module, a preorder of task performance "increasingly demanding and short of perfection". It grades tasks in physics, not explanations or agents, and it is explicitly short of perfection. No conflict.

**Non-resolution treated as failure.** None. Every "failure" in the text is a failed answer, a failed question (L159, L369), the failure of an obligation the system itself claims (L429), a fidelity failure (L223, L626), or a "failure of the record" (L608). An unsolved problem "stands" (L317). That is a status for the assessor, not a fault.

**An infallible chooser assumed.** None. "Established" is always fallible. It rests on a usable receipt (L315), which lapses when a premise stops being live (L369, K2), and a test tells against a candidate "only together with the background and instruments of the test that yields it" (L315, K3). The two places that could be read as final are:

- L317, "a **test** that solves the problem whatever it shows". This is true by the definition of a test, and the solution is for that assessor, through the (K3) bundle.
- L317, "an answer it refutes stays refuted on \(p\) (Part VIII)". This is qualified at L369. See A10.

Universality (L497–L509) is a claim about capability under enabling conditions, not about infallibility: "a finite performance record does not establish it".

**Choosing.** The theory mentions choosing one of two rivals only at L317, "as when choosing one of two rivals meets a claimed obligation only by dropping the other, which fits as well, where keeping every rival that fits is among the protected obligations (Part XI)". The obligations there are the system's own declared inputs (L441), so the sentence imposes nothing on the chooser, and it agrees with the owner: choosing when both fit is not a solution, and it becomes a recognized difficulty only if the chooser has chosen to protect keeping them. Its other uses of "choose" are the theory refusing to choose for anyone: L473, "Both are declared before the attribution, not chosen after it."; L522, "rather than choosing the input from the verdict wanted".

**Assessor, holds, adopts.** "Assessor" is used at L315, L317 and L369 and is never defined. File 92 glosses "judge" as "whoever is judging a candidate: a person, a system, the project". "An assessment is an event with a frozen contract" (L55). "What the system holds" (L429) is undefined as a relation to candidates; L403 uses "holds a representation". Adopting a candidate is mentioned only at L277. Part IX then assesses whether an argument is *usable* (K2, L389–L391), not whether an adoption was right. So the theory has no relation meaning "chose" or "adopted", and no sentence tells a chooser what to adopt.

**Conclusion of the sweep.** No CONFLICTS verdict is warranted on this lens.

L157–L161 (a question can miss its target, assume an incompatible baseline, or combine incompatible requirements; exposing the defect is another question) and L497 ("coherently posed" challenges). ## 6. Findings

### A1. C1, C3: ALREADY_SAID

**Theory places.** L231; L69 "a false theory offered as an answer is an explanatory candidate"; L419 "the relation that \(c\) is actually used to address \(p\)"; L315 "A candidate **fits** what is established for an assessor when no result established for that assessor shows it failing a condition of (E)."; L317 "Two rivals that both fit what is established for an assessor pose, for that assessor, a **problem for \(p\)**: a conflict between ideas that what the assessor has established has not settled." and "the problem for \(p\) stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting."

**Explanation.** "candidate explanations attempt to solve a problem" is the theory's candidate offered for a question \(p\). Even the word matches: (G)'s \(\operatorname{Attempt}\), "actually used to address \(p\)". "Both may appear to solve it" is *both fit*. It is relative to what the assessor has established, which is what "appear" conveys. "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached" is the theory's problem for \(p\), which "stands" until what is established leaves at most one fitting. This is also a restatement of decision S20's "If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem." One caution (section 1): the owner's "problem" in C1 is the theory's *question*, not its *problem for \(p\)*.

**Proposed change.** None.

### A2. C2: ALREADY_SAID (the "sees"/"has" structure); choosing itself is not in the theory

**Theory places.** L315 (established, fits, for an assessor); L317 "Whatever the target does there, at most one of them is an account of \(p\), whether or not anyone can establish what it does" and "whether a candidate is an account of a question is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world"; L67 "Whether a transport is faithful on a contract is independent of whether anyone accepts it."; L71 "A thinker may act on an appraisal without certifying it."; L277 "the reasons for adopting it are assessed elsewhere (Part IX)"; (K2) L389–L391; L201 "a selected transport has no represented target and no criticism in its history; a constructed one has both."

**Explanation.**

- **The distinction the owner stresses.** "sees no option" versus "has no option" has an exact counterpart in the theory, though not for choosing. What an assessor *sees* is what fits what is established for that assessor. What *is* the case, whether a candidate is an account, is fixed by the world "whether or not anyone can establish" it. The theory never says a chooser *has* no option: nothing in it compels an adoption.
- **Three readings of "its best argument".** (i) The strongest argument against the candidate. (ii) The candidate's own best case, which may fail and so rule it out. (iii) The chooser's best argument: the owner uses "its" for the person in C10 ("told by its parents"), which supports (iii). On every reading, the ruling-out is done *by argument*, and it is done for the chooser. The theory's nearest term is an argument the assessor can use (K2), whose licence and premises are the assessor's own ("\(\operatorname{Lic}_j\)", "\(\operatorname{Live}_j\)").
- **"Best" is not a grade.** The theory supplies no ranking of arguments (L25, L522), and under S20 and S26 it must not acquire one. It does not need one. A candidate stops fitting as soon as *any* established result shows it failing (L315), so "not ruled out by its best argument" is rendered without "best": not shown failing by any argument or result the assessor can use. If "best" enters any future wording, it has to stay the chooser's own appraisal, never a verdict the theory hands down.
- **Choosing is on the side of criticism.** Choosing by argument involves a represented target and criticism, so it belongs with construction, not selection (L201; the change list's W37 notes that "a chooser who represents what she wants is not a selection history in this sense").

**Proposed change.** None on this point (see A3 for the one addition).

### A3. C4 ("required"), C6, C7, C8, C11: ADDS

**Theory places.** L317 (the last two sentences: "Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates." and "as when choosing one of two rivals meets a claimed obligation only by dropping the other…"); L429 "Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof."; L441 "The obligations are declared inputs"; L522 "some claims take stated inputs that the semantics records and does not supply"; L277.

**Explanation.** The theory never says what must happen to candidates, rivals or problems (section 3). But it also never says that it does not. Its only mention of choosing (L317) is an example of a recognized difficulty. The owner's central point is: "Notice I never once claimed what must happen"; "whatever happens to the candidates is up to the person doing the choosing"; and, from C3, choosing is not resolving. That point is compatible with every definition in draft 5. It follows from them: a problem stands until what is established leaves at most one fitting (L317), and a choice establishes nothing. But no sentence states it. The statement adds that sentence. It would also guard against a reader who takes L317's "stands until…" or "must supply" as directives.

**Two readings to keep apart.**

- *"Resolution is up to the person"* (C8). (a) Whether and how to pursue or close the inquiry is the person's: this agrees with L429. (b) Whether the problem *is* solved is the person's to choose: this would disagree with L317, where a problem is solved only when what is established leaves at most one fitting. The owner's own C15 ("yes, if resolution is the goal") shows that resolution requires matching, which fits (a). Reading (a) is the one adopted here.
- *"Whatever happens is always the choice of the person"* (C11). (a) Whatever happens to the candidates in the person's inquiry: agrees, by absence. (b) Everything, including whether a candidate is an account: would conflict with L67 and L317. It is excluded by the owner's own C17, "It must match reality".

"required" in C4 is read as conditional on the goal (C15). Read as a demand, it would contradict C7.

**Proposed change.** At L317, after "Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates.", insert:

> "Nor does anything here say what an assessor must do with rivals or with a problem: that is the assessor's choice, and choosing one of two rivals that both fit does not by itself solve the problem."

**Checks.** The sentence names no list, count, grade or record (S20, S26). It is true under the present definitions: a problem is solved only by what is established (L317), and a choice is not an establishing. It changes no case verdict that I can find: no case verdict says a chooser must act. It is a new explicit claim, so under the project's rules it is a change of claim (declared), not a rewording. If wanted, Part 0 could then add a pointer after L25, since L35 says "the front matter states nothing the body does not state more exactly": "It does not say what anyone must do with the candidates, rivals and problems it describes (Part VI)." Only after the body sentence exists.

### A5. C12, C13: ALREADY_SAID (fallible and constrained agents); the owner's reasons are outside the theory

**Theory places.** L67 "Systems can be wrong about their transports, their observations, their criticisms and their own capacities."; L71 "A criticism has a target and is itself conjectural. A thinker may act on an appraisal without certifying it."; L211 "A system can represent a false theory"; L383 "A criticism occurrence can exist when (K1) fails."; L385 "Using an invalid objection does not make it valid."; L393 "Withdrawing a premise removes a license; it does not make the conclusion false."; L403; L461 "Possibility is the absence of a law-imposed limit short of perfection on performance and retention; it is not one successful trajectory."; L479; L509 "Recursion does not entail universality; a historical extension does not certify it; a finite performance record does not establish it."; L528 "A single originative act does not place its author in the universal class."; L27 (humans, machines, institutions, lineages); L427, L473–L475 (resource contract); L495 (enabling conditions).

**Explanation.** That no creative agent is infallible is a commitment of the theory (Part I, L67 and L71), and nothing in the text assumes an infallible chooser (section 3). That agents are constrained has a counterpart: every capability claim is relative to a declared boundary, continuity and resource contract, and to enabling conditions. So "not enough time" narrows what the agent can do under that contract. It does not show a permanent inability (L403), and a finite list of failures is no barrier proof (L495). The "electronic brain" is within scope: the theory's agents include machines (L27). What the theory does not contain is the owner's inference, "Choice is always important because…", or the verdict "They're all valid choices". The theory passes no judgment of validity on choices. That is consistent with the owner, and the theory need not add it.

**Proposed change.** None.

### A6. C9, C10, C13 (abandoning, priority, fading away, constraints as reasons): OUT_OF_SCOPE

**Theory places.** L429 "A complete critical episode contains a recognized difficulty, a target available before its criticism, a conjectural objection, and a content-sensitive response." and "Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof."; L441 "Their declaration makes no claim that the aims are worth pursuing, and (P) does not rank alternatives."; L455 "Repairing an obligation establishes that it was repaired; it establishes nothing about whether the obligation, or the question that led to it, was worth having."; L25; L522; L317 (an unsolved problem "stands").

**Explanation.** The theory defines what an explanation is, and when attributions such as origin, repair and created knowledge hold. Deciding a problem is "a low priority" is a claim of worth, and worth is an input the theory takes and does not supply (L455, L518). Deciding to stop is a decision (L429). An episode that "may never resolve, and fade into recesses of that person's history" is simply an incomplete episode. The theory defines only complete ones, and nothing in it counts an incomplete one as a failure. The attributions just do not hold, and a problem left unsolved "stands" (L317). So the theory need not say more. The one respect in which it could help the owner is by saying outright that nothing requires an episode to be completed.

**Proposed change.** None needed. If the owner wants it said, add after L429's "Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof.":

> "Nothing here requires an episode to be completed or closed: a system may leave one for any reason, such as a lack of time, and a problem for \(p\) that it leaves unsolved stands (Part VI)."

This sentence is true under the present definitions, and it names no list, count, grade or record.

### A8. C5: ALREADY_SAID for questions, with a gap that is choice 1 again

**Theory places.** L157 "## Scope, and a question that can be wrong"; L161 "A question may fail to pick out its alleged target, assume an incompatible baseline, or combine incompatible requirements. Its formulation is still an event. Exposing the defect is another question with its own contract."; L257 "\(\operatorname{Sol}_D(1,b_0)\neq\varnothing\)."; L497 "the coherently posed advanceable challenges"; L425 "When it is a contract, the originative act is the finding of a question."; L592 "Finding the right question is a creative act on the same footing as answering one."; L606.

**Explanation.** A question that is ill posed is in the theory, with three named ways to be faulty, and so is replacing it with a found question. What is missing is the link to candidates. ## 9. For the owner, in plain words

Yes, your statement adds something, but mostly it confirms what the theory already does. The theory already says:

- two candidates can both look right to someone, and then there is an open problem;
- a test, or a sharper question, may settle it;
- questions can be badly posed;
- nobody is infallible;
- ending an inquiry is a decision, not a proof;
- the theory grades no one and ranks no explanation.

Nowhere does it say what anyone must do with the candidates. What it does not yet say in so many words is your point itself: what happens to the candidates is the chooser's choice, and choosing one of two that both still look right does not settle the problem. One sentence would add that.

Your statement also bears on the first question put to you. You say a candidate is ruled out by an argument, as the chooser sees it. That suggests a failure found by examining a candidate, like the myth that assumes its answer, should count. But you said this of non-scientific theories, and the example is about the seasons. So it would help to know whether you mean it for questions about the world too.
