## 5. Rivals, problems and tests: what 'easy to vary' means here

### Example: the unused joint setting

Two wiring arrangements are offered for a panel of switches. They agree at every tested setting and almost every other, differing only at one setting of two switches together, never used, though anyone could make it by hand.

The **fixed verdict**, written in advance: "They are two arrangements, and the record has not chosen between them. One reachable setting is enough." The **ruling** on file 11, the text in force: the theory **agrees**.

### The idea in plain words

Explanations compete when they say different things somewhere the world could be changed; until something found out decides, that is a problem. Look where they differ, or, if the question does not cover that, ask a different question.

### What the theory says

#### Rivals

Each arrangement is a **candidate**, an explanation offered for a question, with a **link** tying the panel, its **target**, to it. They are **rivals**: one was **offered** (put forward as an answer) in place of the other, and they **conflict** at some **setting** (one allowed change, in given circumstances) that both links carry over, inside the question's **range** (the settings it covers) or outside it.

Two candidates conflict at a setting when their **answers** there differ, as here; or when each could meet **matching** and **answering the question asked** there under some behaviour of the target that **the physics** (the adopted theory of physics) allows, but none lets both, as when two **commitments** (pieces offered as doing the work) tied to one **anchor** behave differently.

An offered candidate claims matching and the target's answer at every setting of the range, tested or not, and the rest of section 2's four requirements; outside the range it claims nothing on the question, though what it gives there can conflict with another's.

#### What is not a rival

Redraw one arrangement with other letters for the wires: this **rewriting** (a one-to-one mapping keeping every connection, taking the link along, leaving the answers alone) conflicts nowhere. Nor are two candidates rivals, however differently they split the target, if some behaviour the physics allows lets both match and answer at every allowed setting both carry over.

In the **proposed case** "What holds the universe up" (used in no **round** of the cases), a dog holds up the universe in one story, a turtle in another, and nothing inside can touch or see either. On draft 4's view no allowed change separates them, so they are not rivals: "the difference between them is idle", says the fixed verdict. Nor do an added **idle commitment** (as Tomas's sun god may be, section 4), a repetition, or saying less without contradicting make rivals.

#### No list of all rivals

Nobody need list every wiring of the panel. A candidate's rivals are among those someone has offered; one nobody has offered is no one's rival. This is your decision of 24–25 September on hard to vary: nobody can list all rivals, even in principle. Nor are rivals the **pool** selection works on (section 7).

#### What is established, and what fits

Each tested setting's note is an **evidence item**, a pointer to an event with what it is taken to show; a **receipt** is a chain of reasoning over such items. A receipt for a claim is one against its denial; missing evidence stays missing; a record rebuilt from the claim it is meant to count for is no receipt for it.

A receipt is **usable** by a **judge** (whoever is judging) entitled to it, where it applies and every **assumption** it needs is **still held**. Withdrawing an assumption removes the entitlement but does not make the conclusion false.

A faulty meter can spoil a prediction: **blame is shared** among the claim tested, the test's other assumptions and its instruments, never anything narrower.

A **result**, what a test shows about the target, is **established** for a judge holding a usable receipt for it. A candidate **fits** when no established result shows it failing a requirement. Both arrangements fit.

#### Problems, and the test that solves one

Two rivals that both fit what a judge has established pose, for that judge, a **problem** for the question: they conflict, and what is established has not decided between them.

In a **problem inside the range**, they conflict at a setting the range covers. Whatever the target does there, at most one is an **account** (a candidate meeting all four requirements), whether or not anyone can find out. Finding out what the target does at a setting, by looking or experiment, is a **test**; there it solves the problem whatever it shows. Where the rivals' answers there agree, it must find out how the target's pieces behave. An answer it refutes stays refuted on that question (section 6).

On draft 4's view (untried in a round), setting the two switches together is such a test, whichever way it comes out.

#### Example: a farmer's rule carried south

A proposed case. A Mediterranean village rule says: "Sow the wheat after the first autumn rains, in October, and it ripens in early summer, in June." Nobody knows why it works. A family moves far south of the equator, where the cool rains come in April and May.

The fixed verdict: taken by its months the village rule misleads; taken by its seasons it points to April or May; it cannot say which to trust. The father needs to know why it worked, or to try it.

On draft 4's view (no ruling), "sow in October" and "sow after the first autumn rains" are rivals once either is offered in the other's place. At home both fit generations of harvests and give the same answer at every setting of the home question; they conflict only in the south.

#### Problems outside the range, and easy to vary

That is a **problem outside the range**: the rivals conflict only at allowed settings the range leaves out. Their answers agree throughout it, so no established answer counts against one without the other; a test inside the range can refute one only for a failure of that one alone, such as a piece not behaving like its anchor. Where both meet the requirements, both are accounts.

A candidate is **easy to vary** when it and a rival pose such a problem. The rival is then easy to vary too, and the words take no side: at home each sowing instruction is easy to vary relative to the other. A criticism calling a candidate easy to vary must supply the rival: the criticism is the rival. The theory defines only "easy to vary", not "hard to vary".

#### The cure: a finer range

If both rivals are accounts, a claim that one is right and the other wrong must name an allowed change outside the range that separates them. A **finer range** holding it makes a **different question**, on which the two, while both fit, pose a problem inside the range. For the farmer: when to sow wherever he farms, the south included; a season's sowing there is a test that solves it.

Whether a candidate is an account of a question is fixed by candidate, question and world, not by when anyone first asks or checks.

#### Limits, and no grading

If someone says the panel's question never covered that joint setting, that **limit** (a statement of which possible changes a range leaves out) makes a different question. Leaving out where rivals conflict solves nothing on the first: the problem stands until what is established leaves at most one fitting.

Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates. A problem the system **pictures** (section 7) can be a **noticed difficulty** (section 9): say printing one wiring in a manual is a **demand**, and keeping every rival that fits a **safeguard**: choosing meets the demand only by dropping a rival that fits as well.

#### Same anchors, one account

Two descriptions of the panel word things differently but tie each piece to the same wires and terminals. The theory proves that two candidates matching and answering on a range give the same answers there; and that if their commitments pair off, each pair tied to one anchor (the same **cluster**, or connected bunch, of the target's pieces) on the same slots, each pair is one **kind** (section 3), and as far as matching and answering go the two are one account. The proof is technical.

Without the same anchors nothing more follows: candidates with different anchors, or that split the target differently, are different candidates with the same answers. A claim that one way of tying pieces to anchors is "really" right must name a change separating them, which makes a finer range.

Files 10 and 11 said any two such candidates are one account ("Indistinguishable is identical"). A review in September found that false as written, as both **outside readers** (the two AI systems cross-examining the project) agreed.

#### Departures from the source books

- Deutsch judges ease of variation, and rejects a bad explanation, before any rival or experiment; here it shows only when a rival is offered.
- For him an explanation that could easily explain anything explains nothing; here such a candidate is still an account if it meets the requirements; a test decides on a finer question.
- Here being easy to vary is relative to the question and applies to both rivals: on the seasons the Greeks knew, the Earth's tilt, which he calls hard to vary, is easy to vary relative to the myth of Demeter once either is offered in the other's place.
- The Persephone and Freyr myths, incompatible for him, conflict at no allowed change: here they are not rivals.
- His idea of how far an explanation carries beyond its making is undefined here.

### What changed in this revision

The paragraphs "Rivals" and "Problems" replace file 11's count of changed candidates still passing, its remark that the more an explanation must do the less it can be varied, and its caution that counting is no warrant. Derivation 2, a short proof, is narrowed to "same anchors, one account".

### What is open

- Unseen by the outside readers, used in no round, tried only on small made-up worlds, and not yet placed in the theory's **building order** (which definition rests on which).
- Making "easy to vary" point at one rival would need a further requirement, which risks ranking them.
- Your example, "Demeter grieves" against "Persephone is underground", makes rivals only where some allowed change, such as a veil of dust dimming the sun, sets the in-between state alone.
- What the physics allows at a setting is not among the **stated inputs**, those a claim must state.
- Whether a failure worked out from the candidate alone, such as an assumed answer, counts as established is unsaid; whether a myth tied to nothing and the tilt pose a problem turns on it.
