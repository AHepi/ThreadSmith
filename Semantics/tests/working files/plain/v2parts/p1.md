## The theory in one page

The theory is called "Claude Fable Semantics". Its subtitle, "a structural class of explanatory creativity, with selected and constructed correspondence", says two things in plain words. The theory describes creativity at explaining by its structure alone, by what depends on what. And the links between a system's ideas and the world they are about come about in two ways: shaped by selection, or built by the system.

The theory tries to say exactly what makes something an explanation, and what makes a system creative at making explanations, or questions, that did not exist before. It does so in claims that can be checked against **cases**, short everyday stories with an agreed verdict (section 1), and that could be shown wrong.

Its first example: "The shadow is ten metres long because the pole is five metres tall and the sun is low." Raise the pole and the shadow lengthens. Fix the shadow's length directly, say with a board laid so the shade ends where you choose, and the pole stays as it was. The changes, not the words "pole" and "shadow", show that the dependence runs from pole to shadow. The theory judges every explanation this way: only by what happens when things are changed.

In outline:

- The theory describes the world, and every explanation of it, as a **set-up**: **slots** that hold values (the pole's height, the shadow's length), **pieces** that tie slots together (the sunlight), and **changes** that can be made (raising the pole). A **question** says which changes it covers and what it asks (section 2).
- An explanation offered for a question is a **candidate**. It counts, and is called an **account**, when it meets four **requirements**, all about how it behaves under the question's changes (section 3).
- Some hold that an explanation must pair each of its pieces with a piece of the world "of the same kind". The theory says there is nothing more to a kind than how a piece responds to changes, so no such rule is needed (section 4). It reports which pieces of an explanation do the work, without demanding that all of them do (section 5).
- Two candidates, one offered in place of the other, that disagree somewhere are **rivals**. When nothing yet found out decides between them, they pose a **problem**. A candidate is **easy to vary** when it has a rival that fits what is known as well as it does and disagrees with it only outside what the question covers. A village rule, "sow in October, after the first rains", is an example: at home "in October" and "after the first rains" say the same, and they part only in a land far to the south, which the village's question never covered. Nobody needs a list of every rival there could be; the rivals someone has offered are enough (section 6).
- A question narrowed after a failure is a different question. On the question it failed, a wrong answer stays wrong, whoever offers it again. What can lapse is only the finding that showed it wrong, if an assumption behind that finding is withdrawn (section 7).
- A **system** is whatever the theory is applied to as a possible thinker: a person, an animal, a machine. The **link** between a system's pictures and what they picture has a **history**. It was shaped by selection, with nothing in that history picturing what the link is about; or built through guessing and criticism; or merely written into a model. Creativity lives only in the building (section 8). A system **originates** something when it builds it, the thing is new to the system, and the system uses it on a question. Finding a question counts too (section 9).
- Whether a fault was mended (a **repair**), who gets the **credit**, and whether knowledge was created are read from what actually happened. In "Two plumbers", the one who explained the leak correctly did not stop it, and the one who stopped it had no explanation (section 10). Whether a system has an ability is judged inside a stated **boundary**, the line that says what belongs to the system, and the physics must allow the ability (section 11).

The theory's **central guess** is that creativity at explaining can be fully described with four things. They are not the four requirements above. They are: set-ups and the changes they allow; links between set-ups, judged only by how they behave under change; the history of those links and of the questions they serve; and how all of this is embodied in physical things. An achievement these four cannot describe would count against the guess. So would something that has all four and plainly explains nothing.

The theory does not claim that matter alone fixes what a thing stands for (a history is needed too), or that predicting is explaining. It does not say what is beautiful or worthwhile, how probable a theory is, or which thinker is better, and it does not share out credit beyond what the course of events shows. It defines **classes** of systems, such as the universal class of section 11, and does not claim that any person or machine belongs to one.

The theory has two foundations: the physics and, where a question is about worth, a standard of worth taken from outside. Everything else is built from those two, from each claim's **frame** (what the claim is relative to, such as its range of changes), and from the **stated inputs** that a claim takes as given and states (section 12). The theory names five **main claims**, each with what would defeat it, and counts any mathematical error in its proofs as a further way to be shown wrong (section 12).

This document is also a progress report. Draft 4 has not yet been tried on the cases. Several verdicts are open (section 14). On one example, "The seasons and the sun god", one view of the text would go against the careful person's verdict (section 5). And one of the theory's listed ways of being shown wrong was triggered by the two older texts of the theory, not by draft 4 (sections 6 and 12).

The project keeps two case books. One holds 52 cases, on which file 11 was tried. The other holds 25 **proposed cases**, most drawn from the two source books (below) and five made for the set. A **round** is one complete trial of a text of the theory on the cases. In a round, several readers each read a case against the text and give a **mark**:

- **agrees**: the text gives the fixed verdict;

- **split**: the text can be taken two ways;

It can stand against most of the marks when their reasons do not hold up, as in "Greta's dough" (section 7). In the round on file 11, the rulings were: agrees on 46 of the 52 cases, silent on 6, and disagrees on none.

Two computer programs that write and reason in language, from outside the project, are the **outside readers**. They cross-examine the project's rulings and its drafts.

A **verdict**, in general, is a judgement on a claim. The fixed verdict is the careful person's. The theory can also give a verdict, or leave one **unsettled** when something it needs has not been stated (section 12).

**How each example is marked.** Each example is a case, unless it is marked as one of the theory's own examples. After a case's fixed verdict comes one of two marks.

- *Ruled on file 11: agrees* (or *silent*). The round on file 11 ruled it so, and draft 4 does not change the answer.

## 2. The world as a set-up, and questions about it

### The fever and the thermometer

Hana says the child has a fever, since the thermometer reads thirty-nine. Later she says the reading is what makes the child shiver. Warm the thermometer in her hand: the reading climbs, and the child shivers as before. Cool the child in a bath: the shivering eases. *Fixed verdict:* her first statement is a good reason to believe there is a fever; her second mistakes the instrument for the illness.

### A set-up

Take the pole in the sun. The pole's height, the sun's height and the shadow's length are **slots**, places that each hold a **value**. The sunlight is a **piece**, a working element that ties slots together: it fixes the shadow's length from the other two. The flat ground belongs to the **circumstances**, held fixed while changes are made. Raising the pole is a **change**.

A **set-up**, then, has four things: slots, with the values they can take; pieces; circumstances; and **allowed changes**, the changes the set-up counts as ones that can be made. Changes can be chained one after another, and "no change" counts as one. Under each change and circumstances, each piece has a **behaviour**: the combinations of values it allows among its slots. A **state** gives every slot a value. The **consistent states** are those that every piece allows at once.

A few rules keep this description exact.

- A **removed piece** no longer restricts its slots at all. Take the sunlight away, and nothing fixes the shadow's length.
- A change that **sets** a slot fixes its value directly. It replaces the piece that used to fix that slot, rather than leaving the old piece in place beside a clashing new one. The board that fixes the shadow's length is such a change: it takes over from the sunlight's hold on the shadow.
- A changed status rule (below, and section 4) is a changed piece.
- Values need not be numbers: they may be paths, proofs or histories. Pieces may depend on one another in a loop. Where several states are consistent, the theory keeps them all rather than picking one.

Changes need not be physical. The seventh complaint says "mathematics has no interventions": nothing in mathematics can be reached into and altered, as a pole can be raised. The theory answers that removing a starting assumption, dropping a constraint, or changing a dimension (such as the number of rows of a table) are changes too. Section 3 works one through.

### Roles and direction

Roles are not labelled in advance; they come from the allowed changes.

- An **input slot** is a slot that some allowed change sets directly, such as the pole's height.
- An **output slot** is a slot that a piece fixes from its other slots, such as the shadow's length.
- A **reading** is a slot that reports another, where some allowed change alters the report without altering what is reported, as warming the thermometer does.

**Direction** follows too. Raise the pole and the shadow lengthens; set the shadow directly and the pole stays put. So dependence runs from pole to shadow. That holds whichever way round the calculation linking them is written: "shadow from height" and "height from shadow" use the same numbers, but only one of them behaves like the world when things are changed.

### A question

Take Hana's second question, "what makes the child shiver?" A **question** has five things.

- A **target**: the set-up in the world that the question is about. Here, the child, her temperature, the thermometer and its reading.
- A **range**: the **settings** it covers, a setting being one allowed change made in given circumstances. Here, cooling the child in a bath, or warming the thermometer in a hand. The **untouched setting**, with no change made and ordinary circumstances, is always in the range.
- **What is asked**: the exact thing read off the target at each setting. Here, whether she shivers.
- The demands and safeguards in play, which matter only when a question is about mending something (section 10).
- The history of its range: how the range came about (below).

The **answer** is what the target gives, for what is asked, at each setting. Here, she shivers at the untouched setting and when the thermometer is warmed, and shivers less after the bath. A candidate (section 3) gives its own answer at each setting, and it is right there when its answer is the target's.

A range holds the settings the question states, and no more. In **The table with empty columns**, Lea's question about turning two freezer knobs together is rewritten as a table with one column for each knob. Every row moves both knobs, and no row moves one alone. *Fixed verdict:* "The table is faithful. Its columns could have carried single-knob rows, and none was added. A form that could ask more has not asked more." *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* Nobody makes a mistake here. The point is that a range holds only the settings actually stated, however much more its form could hold.

The sort of question is fixed by what it reads and what its range holds, never by a label.

- A **producing question** asks what produces an outcome. Its range holds changes to what comes before the outcome. ("What makes her shiver?")
- A **pinning-down question** asks what a reading settles about a feature. Its range runs over the different readings that could come up. ("The thermometer reads thirty-nine: has she a fever?", asked for each reading it might show.)
- A **blocking question** asks whether one state can be reached from another at all. ("Why can no machine run forever?", below.)
- A **status question** asks what status something has under a **status rule**, a rule that gives things a status, as the rules of chess make a move legal. (No case in the books is about one.)
- A **purpose question** asks whether a purpose is achieved.

A different range, or a different thing asked, makes a **different question**, and an answer to one is not an answer to another. A reading that pins down an outcome, with a reliable forecast from it, answers the pinning-down question. Whether the thing read also produces the outcome is the producing question, and the first answer is not the second.

**Back to Hana.** *Ruled on file 11: agrees; draft 4 does not change it.* The thermometer's number is a reading: change only the reading and the child is unchanged; change the child and the reading follows. It answers "has she a fever?", a pinning-down question. "What makes her shiver?" is a producing question. Its range holds changes to her temperature, and there the reading does nothing.

### Pinning down and blocking: two of the theory's examples

**Two balances** (the theory's example). An object is weighed on two balances. Each shows the object's mass plus that balance's unknown error. The readings settle the difference between the two errors, but not the mass: put a gram more mass and a gram less error on each balance, and both readings stay the same. So the example explains a limit on what the readings can settle.

In general, the **look-alike states** of a reading are all the states that give that reading. A reading **pins down** a feature when some state gives the reading and all its look-alike states agree on the feature. The theory's **proof on pinning down** shows that a feature is pinned down by every reading that can occur exactly when the feature can be worked out from the reading alone.

Two things follow. Repeating the same weighing adds nothing, since it has the same look-alike states. A **calibration**, a reading taken against something already known, can add something. And "take balance B's error to be zero, because that gives the mass I favour" is not drawn from the readings: its reasoning goes in a circle, even if the mass it gives is right. (Section 17 gives the exact rule for balances.)

**Twenty-three tokens** (the theory's example). Twenty-three whole tokens cannot be shared equally among three people. Handing a token from one person to another never changes the total, and three equal whole shares always make a total that three divides, which twenty-three is not.

An **unchanging quantity** is one that no allowed change alters, like the total here. The theory's **proof on blocking** shows that no chain of allowed changes joins two states where an unchanging quantity differs. The reverse does not hold. Two states with the same total need not be **reachable** one from the other, where reachable means that some chain of allowed changes leads from one to the other. Allowing tokens to be cut makes different states, and a different question; it does not refute a claim about whole tokens.

### A case about blocking

**The machine that runs forever.** At a fair, visitors ask why no machine drives a water pump forever, with no fuel, no wind, no flowing water and nobody pushing it. Pia says hundreds of designs have been tried and every one has stopped, and perhaps someone will find one that works. Quentin says the energy anything gives out must be matched by energy put in or used up, and that holds for any design, tried or not. *Fixed verdict:* only Quentin's answer explains; Pia gives evidence and a hope, but no reason.

*Not yet ruled.* The list of changes expects the verdict to hold. It notes one risk: a reader might call Quentin's energy law the answer restated, which the third requirement forbids (section 3). *This document's view* of the reasoning: it is a blocking question. Quentin gives an unchanging quantity, energy, so no chain of changes leads to a pump that works with nothing used up, whatever the design. Pia gives a list of failures. It has no piece that responds to any change, so it is a table of observed answers (section 3), and it fails the first requirement.

### Where a range comes from, and faulty questions

A range has one of three histories.

- **Declared**: written in by the **modeller**, whoever writes the model down. ("The range is: raise or lower the pole.")
- **Selected**: the survivor of a pool of ranges that were varied and weeded out, with nothing in that history picturing the range (section 8 describes selection). No case in the books shows a selected range.
- **Constructed**: made by a system in an episode, roughly a stretch of inquiry from a noticed difficulty to a response (section 10 says what a complete one holds), with evidence of how it was made (section 9). Noor's question in "The rota" (section 9) is the example.

Judging whether a candidate is an account works the same way whichever history its range has. Only a claim that someone *found* a question needs a constructed range, with the evidence.

**Where the second compartment used to be.** A change removes a freezer's second compartment. Lea had asked for that compartment's temperature. After the change, a colleague answers with the temperature of the space where it used to be. *Fixed verdict:* "A meaning has been supplied, and the question now asks something. It is not what Lea asked." *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

A **faulty question** is one of three sorts.

- It misses its target. On *this document's view*, once the compartment is gone, Lea's question no longer picks out anything at that setting.
- It assumes an untouched setting that cannot be: its starting point has no consistent state, as a question about a freezer both running and unplugged would.
- It asks for things that clash, such as one temperature that is at once the compartment's and the empty space's.

A faulty question was still asked, and its asking is an event like any other. Showing that it is faulty is itself a different question, with its own range. What is forbidden is changing the range, or what is asked, during an **assessment**, one act of judging a candidate with the range held fixed, without recording that the claim has changed. Supplying a meaning for a replacement question makes a different question; it does not answer the first one.

The theory gives no rule for choosing the level of detail at which a question should be asked (section 3 explains level of detail). Draft 4's one change to the theory's text on questions concerns limits, and is in section 7.

## 3. When an explanation counts: the four requirements

### The tide table with a real bell attached

Carla explains why the harbour water is high at noon today with two things. One is the printed almanac of high and low water for every day. The other is a real float that, rising past a mark, tips a lever and rings the harbour bell. *Fixed verdict:* "Carla has explained the bell. She has explained nothing about the tide. The almanac is the answer written down."

### A candidate

Take the explanation of the shadow as a candidate for the question "what fixes the shadow's length?", whose range holds raising the pole and moving the sun.

- The candidate has its own set-up: slots for the height, the sun's angle and the shadow, and a piece, "light travels in straight lines", which fixes the shadow's length from the other two.
- Its **link** says which of the target's states, changes and circumstances go with which of the candidate's. Raising the real pole goes with raising the pole in the candidate. The link **carries over** each setting of the target to the candidate's matching setting.
- The link also ties each piece of the candidate to an **anchor**: a **cluster** (a connected bunch) of the target's pieces that the candidate's piece must behave like. The candidate's light piece is tied to the real sunlight.
- A **slot pairing** says which slot of the anchor goes with which slot of the piece: the candidate's height slot with the real pole's height, and so on.
- The **commitments** are the pieces the candidate offers as doing the work, whether or not anyone has described what work they do (section 5 has a case of a piece nobody described). Here, the light piece is the commitment.
- Everything else the candidate holds fixed is **background**: the values of its circumstances, and its other pieces, including any piece that gives an input slot its value. Here the pole's height and the sun's angle are input slots, and whatever gives them their values (the pole as built, the time of day) is background.

In general, anything offered as an explanation for a question, true or false, is a **candidate**. An **account** is a candidate that meets the four requirements below.

### The four requirements

A candidate is an **account** of a question exactly when it meets four requirements, each about how it behaves under the changes the range holds. None looks at a label.

**1. Matching.** This has two halves, and both are needed.

- *Piece by piece:* at every setting of the range, each commitment allows exactly what its anchor allows, once the anchor's **hidden slots** are set aside (slots inside the anchor that the commitment does not mention). The candidate's light piece gives the same shadow for each height and sun as the real sunlight does.
- *As a whole:* at every setting, the target's consistent states, carried over, are exactly the candidate's consistent states; and a chain of changes in the target carries over as the same chain in the candidate.

Why both? A candidate can get the whole right while splitting it into the wrong pieces. For instance, an explanation of a door hung on two hinges might say that the top hinge carries all the weight: the door still hangs straight, but that piece does not behave like the real top hinge. And pieces that each behave right can lose a tie between them. For instance, two pieces might each describe one of two meshed gears correctly, in an explanation that forgets the gears are meshed; then the whole allows one gear to turn while the other stands still, which the world does not allow.

**2. Answering the question asked.** At every setting of the range, the candidate gives the target's answer, with what is asked held fixed. An account of a different thing asked is not an account of this one.

**3. No assumed answer.** The answer must follow by working out the candidate's own set-up in its circumstances. This has two halves.

- *The answer is not written in.* The target's answer may not appear as an unexplained input or piece, even one labelled "law". This is judged at the stated **level of detail**, how finely things are described. Whether a statement is the answer written in is judged by its form at that level, not by treating every mathematically equivalent truth as the same statement. In mathematics every true statement is equivalent to every other, so on that view every proof would "assume its answer". A long working-out, step by step, is not the bare answer "this table cannot be undone", though the two are equivalent (the tables example, below).
- *Some commitments make the difference.* At some setting of the range, the answer must differ from the answer at the untouched setting (or not be fixed there in the way claimed). And some **group** of commitments must carry that difference: delete the group from the candidate, and the candidate's answers at the two settings come out fixed and equal, or one of them is no longer fixed. In the salt case below, salt spread and salt swept off give two different answers (melted, not melted); delete the salt's action from the explanation and nothing tells the two settings apart.

Both halves are about one thing: the answer must come from the commitments, not from what the candidate takes as given.

**4. No dodging.** The target has a consistent state at the untouched setting. And the range is a stated selection from the **possible** changes, those the laws of physics allow: any possible change the range leaves out is left out by a stated **limit**.

One consequence ties the fourth requirement to the third. Suppose a range holds only **renaming changes**, changes that rename things and alter nothing else, or leaves out every change at which the commitments could matter. Then the commitments have no difference to make, so the range fails the third requirement, and no account can be claimed on it.

**Back to Carla.** *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* Her only piece about the tide is the almanac, which is the answer written in, so she fails the third requirement. A real mechanism that answers another question beside it (why the bell rings) does not change this. Draft 4 also says which of her pieces are commitments, and no group of them makes an account of the tide.

**The domino that never falls.** A network of dominoes checks whether a number is prime: one particular domino falls only if a divisor is found. Fed 641, it never falls. Answer A: nothing ever knocks it over. Answer B: 641 is prime. *Fixed verdict:* B explains; A, though true, does not really.

*Not yet ruled.* "The domino never falls" and "nothing ever knocks it over" say the same thing, the second at the level of single dominoes. So A's only working piece is the answer restated. B has a piece that responds to a change: feed in 640, which has divisors, and the domino falls.

### What the requirements exclude, and what they do not

- A **table of observed answers** lists answers seen, with no piece that responds when the world is changed. It fails matching on any range that holds such a change. A **full response table** records how the set-up responds to every allowed change. It passes matching, and it is judged on the other three requirements like any other candidate. The word "table" settles nothing. (Carla's almanac lists answers, and it fails the third requirement.)
- A **calculation run backwards** works an earlier quantity out from a later one and offers that as what produces it. On the producing question it fails whole matching. Raise the pole, and the world's shadow lengthens; but in the calculation, where the shadow comes first, the shadow stays as it was. It may match on the pinning-down question, which is a different question.
- "It is so because it is so" fails the third requirement. So does a candidate whose only real piece restates the answer, whatever mechanism for another question sits beside it.
- A candidate fails the fourth requirement when the only real difference it makes is one that no possible change could bring about: its range was narrowed without saying so. For instance, an explanation of a pendulum whose only working difference lies between gravity as it is and gravity switched off, when nothing possible switches gravity off.

**The planets tonight and a thousand years ago.** Priya calculates where the planets were a thousand years ago from where they are tonight, and says they were there *because* they are here tonight. *Fixed verdict:* the earlier arrangement, with the laws of motion, explains tonight's; not the reverse.

*Not yet ruled.* In the forward explanation, the laws of motion are the commitments, and the old positions are background, giving the input slots their values. Removing the laws loses the difference between settings, so the forward explanation meets the third requirement. Priya's is a calculation run backwards. Under changes to the earlier positions, it fails whole matching. Under changes to tonight's positions, which leave the past as it was, there is no difference for it to carry. *This document's view* adds that her "because" answers "where were they?", a pinning-down question, following the calculation run backwards above.

**Salt on the icy step.** Ines says the ice melted because she spread salt: spread it, and the ice melts within the hour; sweep it off at once, and the ice stays. She knows nothing finer. *Fixed verdict:* a genuine explanation, a coarse one. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* A coarse account answers the coarse question, and its strength is fixed by its range, not by what a finer range would add.

Draft 4 states the reason more directly (*not yet ruled*). Her explanation has two things in it. Spreading the salt sets whether salt is on the step, an input slot, so the spreading is background. What the salt does to the ice is the commitment. The difference between spread and swept lies in the input slot; what carries that difference to the ice is the salt's action, and removing it loses the difference.

Nor do the requirements exclude a true mechanism guessed for bad reasons, or prefer an elegant account to a clumsier one that matches as well. A proof meets them for the question "why does this follow from these assumptions?" without answering "what caused this?". How deep an explanation goes, how far into finer workings, depends on the question, and the requirements do not measure it.

### Odd-sized tables: a mathematical example

This is the theory's own example, and it answers the seventh complaint, that mathematics has no changes. Take square tables of numbers with an odd number of rows, where each number is the negative of its mirror image across the diagonal (so the diagonal is all noughts). No such table can be undone: no other table reverses it.

Why? The question's range drops the mirroring, or the oddness, or both, holding ordinary arithmetic fixed. The long working-out of the number that decides whether a table can be undone, with the mirroring put in, meets all four requirements, so it is an account. Drop the mirroring, or drop the oddness, and a table that can be undone appears, so the difference is carried by those two commitments. A three-line proof is shorter, but shortness is not a fifth requirement. Here the changes are dropped assumptions, which is the answer to the complaint. Section 17 gives the working.

### Explanations of absence

**Denying the inner experience.** Wren says there are no inner experiences, and people are mistaken in thinking they have them. Yuri says the same, and adds that people looking inwards consult memories of what they have just perceived, through machinery that evolved to report experiences that never took place. *Fixed verdict:* Wren gives no reason why people believe they have inner experiences; Yuri gives one, though an incomplete one.

A question "why is there no such-and-such?" works like this.

- Its target lacks the pieces that the opposing view needs: here, the inner experiences themselves.
- Its range holds the changes that would add those pieces.
- An **explanation of absence** passes when adding those pieces would change its answer as it would change the target's, and leaving them out changes neither.
- The opposing view's imagined pieces are tied to a removed cluster, and a removed piece restricts nothing (section 2), so they cannot match anything.

Why the absent thing *seems* present is a different question. An account of the absence neither answers it nor needs to. A **bare denial** offers no piece that responds to a change in what produces the appearance, so it is no account of that second question.

*Not yet ruled.* Wren's is a bare denial. Yuri offers such a piece: machinery that reads memories and produces reports. Whether it is a good one, the question does not ask.

*Departure:* Deutsch asks an explanation that denies something to explain why it seems to exist; here that is a different question.

### Candidates and accounts

**A myth about winter.** A Greek farmer asks why winter comes every year, and is told the myth: Persephone must return to Hades for part of every year, and while she is away her mother Demeter grieves and makes the world cold. Taken as it stood for the Greeks, the myth agrees with all they knew. *Fixed verdict:* it does not explain why winter comes every year; it is an explanation only in form, an attempt, and a false one.

*Not yet ruled.* The yearly return is written into Persephone's bargain, so the answer is assumed, and the myth is no account. A false theory offered for a question is a candidate, which ordinary speech may call an explanation. One outside reader (of two) named this reasoning as a move toward the fixed verdict on an earlier draft, and the project kept the text.

Two **principles** of the theory stand behind the requirements.

- Whether a link matches is a fact, whether or not anyone accepts it. A system can be wrong about its links, its readings, its criticisms and its abilities.
- An explanation may hold a correct dependence alongside errors elsewhere. But an error in the very dependence said to do the work cannot count. In this principle, "explanation" means an account.

In this document, "explanation" is an everyday word for anything offered as the reason something is so; "candidate" and "account" are the theory's terms. Whether an account is easy to vary is a separate matter (section 6).

*Departure:* Deutsch counts a false myth as an explanation; here it is a candidate.
