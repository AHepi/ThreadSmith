## 5. Kinds are how pieces respond

### 5.1 The float that does two jobs

A float in a fuel tank turns a dial on the dashboard and, when it drops low enough, opens a valve to the reserve tank. Milan says the falling float opens the valve; Lena says the dial reaching "low" does. Bend the needle to show "full": the valve still opens. Hold the float up with a wire: the dial shows "full" and the valve stays shut. *Fixed verdict:* Milan is right; "The float is read by the dial and also does the work."

Two changes, not the names "float" and "dial", showed which piece does the work. That is the theory's whole view of kinds.

### 5.2 Response patterns and kinds

Across the two changes above, the float and the dial respond differently. Bending the needle changes what the dial shows and leaves the valve as it was. Holding the float up changes the dial and keeps the valve shut. A piece's **response pattern** is its behaviour at every setting of a range, taken together.

Two pieces are one **kind** on a range when their slots can be paired one to one so that their response patterns agree. So kinds depend on the range, that is, on which changes are allowed; a level of detail matters to kinds only through the changes it allows. Pieces that no allowed change tells apart are one kind, whatever they are called. They may come apart on a **finer range**, one with more settings. A response pattern is built from behaviour, not from the values the slots happen to hold, so pieces that differ only in their values are one kind.

The same comparison is made between a piece of one candidate and a piece of another, and between a commitment and its anchor.

**Two thermostats.** Two thermostats of one make are set to eighteen and twenty-two degrees. Rosa says they are one kind of thing in two states; Emil says they are two kinds, one keeping a warm room and one a cool one. *Fixed verdict:* Rosa is right about what the devices are; Emil's difference matters only when the question is why this room is warm.

*Ruled on the current text: agrees*, though several readers in the round marked the theory silent; the ruling weighed their reasons (section 2.4). Two points settle it, and draft 4 now states both outright. A change that sets a slot, such as turning a dial from eighteen to twenty-two, replaces only what fixes that slot's value; it does not change how the pieces that read the slot behave. And a change under which two pieces behave alike does not tell them apart. Eighteen and twenty-two are values of one slot, and both devices behave alike whatever it holds. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 5.3 Cause, measurement and status rule

Take the pole again. Set the shadow's length directly, with the floodlight, and the sunlight's hold on the shadow is replaced. Misread the shadow with a badly held ruler, and the sunlight is unaffected. Now the thermometer. Set the child's temperature directly, with a bath: the thermometer measures just as before, and the number it shows follows the child. Warm the thermometer in a hand: now its number no longer comes from the child at all, so the measuring itself has been altered.

What ordinary speech calls a cause, a measurement or a rule is a family of response patterns, not an extra fact.

- A **cause** is a piece whose behaviour changes when its output slot is set directly, or when the piece itself is replaced, and is unaffected when only a report of its output is altered. The sunlight is a cause of the shadow's length: the floodlight replaces its hold, and the misread ruler leaves it alone.
- A **measurement** is a piece whose behaviour is unaffected when the thing measured is set directly, and changes when the measuring itself is altered. The thermometer's measuring is one: the bath leaves it as it was, and warming the thermometer in a hand alters it.
- A **status rule** is a piece unaffected by changes to the world, which changes when the rule itself is edited. The rules of chess do not change when a move is made on the board; they change when the rule book is rewritten. A status rule answers a status question. Whether it governs a practice, or should, are different questions. A changed status rule counts as a changed piece, like any other.

So the theory never asks whether a piece "is" a cause. Anything that reports another thing, as the thermometer reports the child's temperature, has a measurement's pattern: change only the report, and what it reports stays as it was; change what it reports, and the report follows. Which of the two an explanation offers as producing an outcome is settled by the pattern, not by the explanation's wording.

**Back to the float.** *Ruled on the current text: agrees; draft 4 does not change it.* The dial has a measurement's pattern, and the float has a cause's pattern for the valve. So the theory gives the fixed verdict: Milan is right.

### 5.4 No requirement about kinds

Should each piece of an account have to be tied to a piece of the world "of the same kind"? Such an **anchoring requirement** would be redundant or empty. If the range has a setting that tells the two kinds apart, a piece tied to the wrong one already fails piece-by-piece matching, as holding the float up with a wire told the float from the dial. If the range has no such setting, the two are one kind on that range. There is no third case.

The theory's **proof that kinds add nothing** shows that piece-by-piece matching gives each commitment its anchor's response pattern, setting by setting. So "kind" can be dropped from the definition of an account without losing any case. That is the third of the five main claims (section 13.5). It would be defeated by a case in which a label told apart two accounts that no allowed change tells apart, and the difference did real explaining. The theory holds that no such case exists; to show it wrong, someone would have to find real explaining that the changes miss. Nobody has yet (section 14).

### 5.5 Three complaints

*"Without kinds you cannot tell a cause from a correlation."* You can, and only this way. A correlation has no piece that responds when its supposed input slot is changed; a cause has one. A label adds nothing. If some allowed change tells the two apart, matching already finds it. If no allowed change tells them apart, the label claims a difference that the level of detail does not have.

*"So a thing is whatever you can do to it."* No. A kind is only what the allowed changes at a level of detail can tell apart. The explanation itself keeps every one of its pieces, including pieces that no change at that level tells apart. Those are one kind at that level, and that is correct, not a loss. On a finer range, with more changes, they may come apart. So the claim is only that every claim about kinds is tied to a level of detail and its changes.

*"Kinds obviously exist. A rule is not a cause."* Agreed that a rule is not a cause, and the difference is one of response pattern, as section 5.3 shows. What the theory denies is that the difference exists before, or apart from, the allowed changes.

### 5.6 Matching over many steps

**Keeping count with string.** Two groups of goatherds keep count of their flocks with string. Nobody can measure string exactly: every length they measure out is off by up to a tenth of an inch. In the first group, as each goat leaves the pen in the morning, a herder reels out a length of string as long as that goat's body; in the evening, as each goat returns, its length is reeled back in, and when all the string is back, all the goats are home. In the second group, each goat stands for exactly one inch of string, and after every goat the herder trims or lengthens the string to the nearest whole inch. Both groups also add flocks together by joining strings, and split flocks by cutting a string in two. *Fixed verdict:* only the rounding method keeps count of a flock of any size. In the first, the error piles up with every goat, join and cut, until it is more than a goat's length, and then the string can no longer show whether a goat is missing.

Two proofs of the theory concern chains of changes like these.

- **Matching step by step.** If an explanation matches the world for each single change, it matches for every chain of those changes, so long as each step stays within the settings where its link applies. For instance (this document's illustration), a tally that matches the flock for each goat leaving and each goat returning matches it for any day's comings and goings. Nobody needs to check every chain. (Section 18 adds a related result, on matching in one direction or in both.)
- **Error build-up.** This is for candidates that match only approximately. It says how small errors add up over many steps.

The **proof on error build-up** works like this, in the goatherds' terms. Suppose each step of the candidate lands at most a small **step error** away from where the target's step lands: here, a tenth of an inch per goat, join or cut. And suppose there is a fixed **widening factor**: a number such that one step of the candidate never makes an existing gap more than that many times larger. Then, from a shared start, the gap after many steps is at most the step errors added up, each enlarged by the widening factor once for every later step. If there is no fixed widening factor, because a single step can blow a small gap up without limit, no ceiling on the gap follows at all. And coming close is not matching: a question that asks for matching is not quietly answered by a candidate that only comes close. Section 18 gives the proof exactly.

*Not yet ruled.* For the first group the widening factor is one: measuring, joining and cutting never make an existing gap larger. So the ceiling grows by a tenth of an inch for every goat, join or cut, without limit. That fits the first half of the verdict, but only loosely: the proof gives a ceiling, and does not show that the error must actually grow. The rounding method lies outside the proof altogether, since rounding removes the error at every step, which the proof does not describe. So the theory does not by itself reach this verdict. Its proof is consistent with the first half and silent on the second, which rests on the case's own reasoning.

## 6. Which pieces do the work

### 6.1 The seasons and the sun god

Tomas explains the seasons. The Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space as the Earth goes round the sun, as a spinning top keeps its axis steady. For half the year each half of the Earth leans towards the sun, and for the other half it leans away. Each step can be checked on its own. Then he adds: "And the sun god, who approves of this arrangement, keeps the tilt steady." *Fixed verdict:* "Yes, Tomas explains the seasons. No, the sun-god sentence is not part of what explains them."

The theory's verdict here is not settled. Taken one way, the text gives the fixed verdict; taken other ways, it may not. Section 6.5 explains.

### 6.2 Supports and load-bearing groups

Tomas's explanation has several commitments: the tilt, the axis keeping one direction, the point about spinning bodies, and the sun god. Keep only some of them, with the background held fixed, and you have a **cut-down candidate**. Keep the first three, say. The way of cutting candidates down is stated in advance; the theory does not say by whom, or anything more about it (section 19 notes that it is not among the stated inputs).

A **support** is a group of commitments that by itself, with the background, makes an account. If Tomas's first three commitments make an account, they are a support. A **smallest support** is one from which nothing can be taken without losing it. The theory does not assume that adding a commitment to a support always leaves a support (section 6.4 shows it can fail). Nor does it assume that every support contains a smallest support.

A group is **load-bearing** in a support when the support stops being one without it. A group can be load-bearing though none of its members is alone. With two springs, each enough by itself to swing a door back (section 6.3), taking both away breaks the support, while taking either one away leaves the other doing the job.

Load-bearing is relative to the support being judged. And the supports judged are groups drawn from the commitments the candidate actually has, whether or not anyone has pointed to a particular group as a support. They are never groups that need commitments the candidate does not carry.

**A premise both routes use.** Two lines of proof lead to the answer (the case calls them routes; in this document's words they are supports), and both use premise P. The second line could be rewritten without P, but nobody has written that version. *Fixed verdict:* P is load-bearing in each line as written; that another line could be written without it is "a fact about a candidate nobody has written". *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict. Draft 4 adds a reason: a candidate nobody has offered is no one's rival (section 7.3).

Suppose three things: the candidate has a limited number of commitments, not endlessly many; adding a commitment never breaks a support; and the whole candidate is a support. Then the theory's **proof on smallest supports** gives two simple rules. A single commitment is load-bearing in some support exactly when it belongs to some smallest support. And it is **needed in every support** exactly when it belongs to every smallest support. So, under those assumptions, finding the smallest supports tells you which commitments do the work. Where the assumptions fail, as with interference (section 6.4), the rules do not apply. Section 18 states the proof exactly.

### 6.3 Two springs, and supports that run

**The spring nobody mentioned.** An explanation of why a door swings back holds two connected springs, each enough alone. The second is never described as doing anything, until the first is deleted from the explanation; then whoever gave the explanation says the second was always there. *Fixed verdict:* "It was already a route. That nobody said it did work does not make it a later addition." ("Route" is again the case's word for a support.)

*Ruled on the current text: agrees.* The two springs give **redundant supports**: each spring alone is a support, and neither is needed. The second spring was a support from the start, whether or not anything ever set it working. Draft 4 rests this on firmer text: commitments are the pieces offered as doing the work "whether or not anyone has described their work". So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

**The cable that used to be a spring.** After the first spring is deleted, whoever gave the explanation takes the piece that stood for the surviving spring, says it now stands for a cable, shows that the door still returns, and reports that the spring explanation survived. *Fixed verdict:* what survived is a replacement, and "the replacement's success is its own". *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* A piece tied to a different anchor makes a different candidate, judged on its own. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

A support is a group of commitments in an explanation. It **runs** in a real system when actual events carry it out. Open the real door, and the second spring stretches and then pulls the door shut: those pulls are events, and they carry out the support "the second spring swings the door back". Such events form a **route that ran**. Section 11.2 says why credit follows routes that ran, not the supports a candidate contains.

Two machines can give the same outputs, whatever goes in, and still differ in which supports run. The theory names parallel and priority wiring as its example, without details. In plain terms: in parallel wiring two circuits work at once, and in priority wiring one works while the other waits, and the two can give the same outputs. The theory's **proof that outputs do not settle accounts** shows what follows. When two systems give the same outputs, whatever goes in, but differ on a claim about an account, no judgement made from outputs alone can be right about both. The theory adds two everyday instances. Who wrote a text cannot always be judged from the text alone, since two writers can reach the same words by different routes. And whether something delivered was actually used cannot be judged from delivery logs alone.

That is why predicting is not explaining. A prediction looks only at outputs. An account is judged by its pieces and the supports that run, and two systems with the same outputs can differ there.

### 6.4 Interference and idle commitments

**One more commitment.** Every earlier addition to a finite collection of commitments kept its support. The newest, that the room was locked, conflicts with a premise every line of it uses. (The case gives no more detail; picture, say, an explanation of how someone got into a room, every line of which uses the premise that they came through the door.) *Fixed verdict:* the conclusion no longer follows; "additions could not always be made without loss". *Ruled on the current text: agrees.*

That is **interference**: adding a commitment breaks a support. The whole fails although a smaller group succeeds. Nor can whoever gave the explanation get round the failure by declaring, after the fact, that the troublesome commitment was never really a commitment. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

With endlessly many commitments, stranger things can happen: a whole endless group can do work that no single commitment and no smallest group does. The theory has a mathematical example of this; no case in the books is about it, and section 18 gives it.

None of the four requirements asks that each commitment do some work. Take a candidate that has at least one support. An **idle commitment** is one that makes no difference to any support: adding it to any support leaves a support, and removing it from any support leaves a support. (For a support that does not contain it, removing it changes nothing.) The candidate is then an account with it exactly when it is one without it, and it is load-bearing in no support.

Both halves are needed.

- The removal half keeps out a redundant spring. Take the support whose only spring is the second one. Remove the second spring from it, and no support is left. So the second spring is not idle.
- The adding half keeps out interference. Add "the room was locked" to a support, and the support breaks. So that commitment is not idle either.
- The definition applies only to a candidate that has a support. In a candidate with no support at all, even a wrong commitment would pass both halves, since there would be no support to break.

*Departure:* Deutsch counts superfluous details as a defect of an explanation; here nothing counts against a candidate for carrying one.

### 6.5 Back to Tomas

*Not yet ruled.* If the god sentence restricts nothing, because the spinning-top point already explains why the tilt stays steady, then it is idle and load-bearing in no support, and the fixed verdict follows: Tomas explains, and the sentence is not part of what explains. But the text can be taken two other ways, if the god is offered as a second piece that keeps the tilt steady.

- If that piece matches its anchor, it is a second, redundant support, like the second spring. Then it does some explaining, which goes against the verdict's second half.
- If it does not match, it is interference. The whole of what Tomas said then fails piece-by-piece matching, and so is not an account, though the part without the sun god still is one. Whether that still counts as "Tomas explains" is open.

Which way applies turns on what Tomas offers the sentence as doing, and the case does not say. So, taken the first way, the theory gives the fixed verdict; taken either of the other two, it may not.

## 7. Rivals, problems and tests: what "easy to vary" means here

### 7.1 The gardener's two rescues

This illustration comes from the project's note on rivals of 25 September; it is not a case.

A gardener says her south bed ripens first because the south wall gets the most sun. One summer the north bed ripens first. Two rescues are offered. One says the neighbour planted an early variety along the north side. The other says that in a wet spring the north bed ripens first. Both fit every summer she has seen. But they give different answers for summers she has not seen. In a dry spring with the early variety planted, the first rescue says the north bed ripens first, and the second says it does not.

This one story shows most of this section: two ideas offered against each other, both fitting everything seen, a summer that would decide between them, and no need for a list of every rescue anyone could invent.

### 7.2 Your decision

On 24 September you spoke to two points. On the set of all the versions of an explanation that would still fit, you said: "That assumes that the set even matters or that the creative agent can even list them. As far as I'm aware, that's not possible, even in principle. A variation is a competitor. Whether anyone can list all variations that still fit is beside the point. If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem."

On whether the theory should grade explanations, you said that it "depends entirely on how error correction is handled", and then: "A record is redundant. Once the explanation is rescued, the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in." A grading scheme would have kept a record of past failures, and of the changes made to rescue the explanation after each; you judged such a record unnecessary. On 25 September you asked that this be logged, so that you would not have to correct it again.

Draft 4 writes your position into three paragraphs: "Rivals" and "Problems", in this section, and "A failed answer stays failed", in section 8. Your "variation", or "competitor", is a rival here.

### 7.3 Rivals

In general, two candidates for one question are **rivals** when two things hold. One of them has been **offered** for the question in place of the other, as each rescue is offered as the reason the north bed ripened first. And they **conflict** at some allowed setting of the target that both links carry over, inside the range or outside it. "Both links carry over" means that each candidate's link gives a setting of its own that goes with it, so that both candidates say something about it, as both rescues say something about a dry spring with the early variety.

Two candidates conflict at a setting in either of two ways.

- Their answers there differ. In a dry spring with the early variety, one rescue says the north bed ripens first and the other says it does not.
- Or their answers agree, but they differ about how the target's pieces behave there: each could match, and give the answer, under some possible behaviour of the target, but no such behaviour lets both. For instance, two explanations of a lamp might both say "lit" at some setting, while one says a certain wire carries current there and the other says it does not. Whatever the wire really does, at most one of them matches. (The theory's wording: two of their commitments tied to one anchor behave differently there.)

A candidate offered for a question claims all four requirements on the whole range, at every setting, tried or not. Outside the range it claims nothing on that question, though what it gives there can still conflict with what another candidate gives.

Not every pair of candidates is a pair of rivals.

- A candidate and a **rewriting** of it, renamed piece for piece with every connection kept and its answers left as they are, conflict nowhere. "A rainy spring" in place of "a wet spring" is no rival.
- Nor do two candidates, however differently they split the target into pieces, when some possible behaviour of the target would let both match, and give the answer, at every allowed setting both links carry over. Examples are a fuller and a leaner account of the same thing; a coarse account beside a finer one, such as Ines's salt beside a chemist's account of how salt melts ice (this document's illustration); a duplicate, the same piece stated twice; an idle addition, such as the sun god if it is idle (section 6.5); and a **blurred copy**, a less detailed version of the same account.

**What holds the universe up.** One story says an eternal dog outside the universe holds it up; another says a turtle; a third, turtles all the way down. Nothing inside the universe can touch or see the holder. *Fixed verdict:* none explains; and though the dog and the turtle differ in words, nothing anyone could see or do depends on which is true: "the difference between them is idle".

*Not yet ruled.* Dog and turtle conflict at no allowed setting, so they are not rivals. Since nothing inside the universe can touch or see the holder, no allowed change gets a different answer from the one story than from the other, and no possible behaviour of the holder tells them apart. They look like rival stories, but their difference is in words only, which is the verdict's "idle". And since no allowed change alters the answer, no group of commitments has a difference to carry, so none of the stories meets the third requirement. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

A candidate's rivals are among those someone has offered, and a candidate nobody has offered is no one's rival. No list of all rivals is supposed. As you said, as far as you know nobody can make one, even in principle.

### 7.4 What is established, and what fits

**The worn key and the diary.** Sam makes keys. His present key diagram, a drawing of how to cut a key for a lock, agrees with a worn key from an early attempt, found in his drawer. The claim at stake is when that early key was made, and so how the diagram grew. To date it, Sam wrote a diary last week, working the dates out from the stages visible in the diagram. *Fixed verdict:* "The worn key is evidence on its own. The diary is the diagram talking about itself." *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* The reasons are below; the theory gives the fixed verdict.

The theory's words for evidence, step by step, with the worn key:

- An **evidence item** is a pointer to an event, with what it is taken to show. The worn key points to an early key once cut to that shape.
- A **receipt**, in the theory's word, is a chain of reasoning built on evidence items: something a judge can hold and show, as one shows a receipt for a payment. Evidence for a claim is, by the same token, evidence against its denial. Where there is no receipt either way, the claim simply has none: missing evidence stays missing, and nothing fills the gap.
- Something written up from the claim it is meant to support is no receipt for that claim. Sam's diary was worked out from the diagram, so it is no receipt for the diagram's history. The worn key is.
- An argument is **usable** by someone when three things hold: they are entitled to use it (the theory says no more about what entitles them); it applies to the case in hand; and every **assumption** it needs is **still held**. Withdrawing an assumption takes away the entitlement, without making the conclusion false.
- **Blame is shared.** A failed prediction counts against the claim being tried together with the other assumptions behind the prediction and the instruments used, as a bundle. It does not by itself say which of them is at fault.
- A **judge** is whoever is judging a candidate: a person, a system, the project. A **result** is what looking or experiment shows about the target. It is **established** for a judge who holds a usable receipt for it.
- A candidate **fits** what is established for a judge when no result established for that judge shows it failing a requirement.

Both of the gardener's rescues fit: nothing she has established shows either failing.

### 7.5 Problems

Two rivals that both fit what a judge has established pose, for that judge, a **problem** for the question: a conflict between ideas that what is established has not settled. There are two sorts.

In a **problem inside the range**, the rivals conflict at a setting the range covers. Suppose the gardener's question covers every kind of spring and planting. Then her two rescues conflict inside its range, at a dry spring with the early variety. Whatever the target does there, at most one of them is an account, whether or not anyone can find out. Establishing what the target does there, by looking or by experiment, is a **test**: plant the early variety and wait for a dry spring. A test establishes the target's answer at that setting, and also, where the rivals' answers there agree, how the target's pieces behave. It solves the problem whatever it shows, since afterwards at most one rival fits. A rival's answer that the test refutes stays refuted on that question (section 8.3).

In a **problem outside the range**, the rivals conflict only at allowed settings outside it. Suppose instead that the gardener's question covers only the summers she has seen. Then her two rescues agree throughout the range, and conflict only at summers the question does not cover. No established answer inside the range tells against one without telling against the other. A test inside the range can refute one without the other only for a failure peculiar to that one. And where both meet the requirements, both are accounts of the question.

**The unused joint setting.** Two wiring arrangements agree on every setting that has been tried, and on almost every untried one. They differ at one setting of two switches together, which nobody has used and anyone could make by hand. *Fixed verdict:* they are two arrangements, and nothing tried so far has chosen between them; one reachable setting is enough.

*Ruled on the current text: agrees*, resting on a remark in the current text that a claim that two candidates really differ must name a change that separates them. Draft 4 narrows that remark to candidates that differ only in which piece is tied to which anchor (section 7.8). So on draft 4 the verdict rests on two other grounds (*not yet ruled*).

- The change list treats the tried settings like the trials of selection (section 9.6). Both arrangements come through every tried setting, and they differ at an untried one, so the tried settings leave that setting open: nothing tried so far has chosen between them.
- The joint setting can be reached by hand, so it is possible, so it is in the range unless a stated limit leaves it out. The case does not say that anyone offered one arrangement in place of the other. If someone did (two electricians, say, each claiming to know how the box is wired), the arrangements would be rivals that conflict at the joint setting, and both fit: a problem inside the range. Setting the switches together is a test that solves it either way: "one reachable setting is enough".

So on this view draft 4 gives the fixed verdict.

### 7.6 Easy to vary

A candidate is **easy to vary** when it and a rival pose a problem outside the range. On the question about the summers she has seen, each of the gardener's rescues is easy to vary: "a windy spring" would fit as well as "a wet spring", and nothing the question covers could tell them apart. *This document's view* of why the name fits: the candidate can be swapped for its rival, varied, and nothing on the question notices, since every answer in the range stays the same.

Is being easy to vary a fault? Not in itself, in this theory. The rival is then easy to vary too, and the words say nothing about which of them is right. Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates. What the theory does with the idea is this. A criticism that a candidate is easy to vary must supply such a rival: offering the rival is the criticism. And the cure is a finer question, on which a test can decide (section 7.7).

The theory defines only "easy to vary". It does not define "hard to vary", and it counts nothing. It does not say why it leaves "hard to vary" undefined. *This document's view* of one reason: "not easy to vary" would be true of any candidate nobody has challenged with a rival, however loose it is, so it would not pick out a merit (section 14).

**A farmer's rule carried south.** A village rule on the northern shore of the Mediterranean says: "Sow the wheat after the first autumn rains, in October, and it ripens in early summer, in June." Nobody knows why it works. A family moves to a land far south of the equator, where the cool rains come in April and May. *Fixed verdict:* the rule tells the father little he can rely on. Taken by its months it misleads; taken by its seasons it points to April or May; and it cannot say which to trust. He needs to know why it worked, "or else trial" (the case's word, in its everyday sense: trying it and seeing, which in this document's words is a test).

*Not yet ruled.* "Sow in October" and "sow after the first rains" are two ways of taking the rule. Once either is offered in place of the other, as the father must do when he chooses, they are rivals. At home they agree at every setting, and both fit generations of harvests; they conflict only in the south. So they pose a problem outside the home range, and each is easy to vary relative to the other. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict: the rule alone cannot tell him which way of taking it to trust.

**Your example.** You set "winter comes because Demeter grieves while Persephone is underground" against "winter comes because Persephone is underground", with no grief in it. On every season the Greeks knew, the two say the same. Are they rivals? Only if the world allows a change at which they come apart.

- In the grief story, the cold comes through Demeter's grief, and her grief stands for one thing in the world: how much warmth the Earth receives from the sun. In the other story, the cold comes straight from Persephone's absence.
- Now picture a change that cuts the warmth received while Persephone is above ground: a veil of dust dimming the summer sun. The grief story, whose grief is the warmth received, says it turns cold. The other story says it stays warm, since Persephone is above ground. So at that change the two conflict.
- The world allows such a change, so they are rivals. The Greeks never met it, so they conflict only outside the range of seasons the Greeks knew, and each is easy to vary relative to the other.

In a model of the world that allowed no such change, they would conflict nowhere, and would not be rivals.

Two partial explanations, each exactly right about a different step of a mechanism and both true inside the range, can still be rivals outside it. Then the finer question's test can show both wrong. The project's note on rivals of 25 September gives this only in general terms, without a worked story.

### 7.7 A finer question

If two rivals are both accounts on a range, they conflict only outside it. The reason is short. Both give the target's answer at every setting of the range, so their answers agree there. And both match the target there, so the target's own behaviour is one that lets both match. A claim that one is right and the other wrong is then a claim that some allowed change outside the range separates them, and it must name that change.

A finer range that holds a change at which the two conflict makes a different question. If both are offered for that question, and both still fit, they pose a problem inside its range, and a test can decide. On the original question nothing changes: both remain accounts there. The farmer's finer question covers sowing anywhere, the south included. A season's sowing in the south is a test that decides it: the verdict's "or else trial".

Whether a candidate is an account of a question is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world. It is not fixed by when anyone first asks the question, nor by whether anyone has checked the candidate against it.

**The tilt and the midnight sun.** An ancient astronomer proposes that the Earth's axis is tilted, to explain why the summer sun stands higher than the winter sun. It follows, though nobody works it out for centuries, that far north the summer sun never sets. *Fixed verdict:* the idea already accounted for the midnight sun (the case's words); no one, she included, had explained it before the connection was worked out.

*Not yet ruled.* The first half follows from the sentence just given: the idea's standing was fixed by the idea, the question and the world, so it already accounted for the midnight sun. The second half needs a distinction from section 10.5. An idea can be an account of a question whether or not anyone uses it; but explaining, as something a system does, needs the idea to have been used on the question, and nobody had used it on the midnight sun. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict on both halves.

A range narrowed to leave out the settings where two rivals conflict also makes a different question. It solves nothing on the first question, where the problem stands until what is established leaves at most one rival fitting.

### 7.8 Same anchors, one account

The theory's **proof on same anchors**, narrowed in draft 4, says two things. First, two candidates that both match, and both give the answer, on a range give the same answers there, since each gives the target's. Second, if in addition their commitments pair off, each pair tied to the same anchor through the same slots, then each pair is one kind, and, so far as matching and answering go, the two are one account. For instance (this document's illustration), two explanations of the shadow, one calling its piece "the light" and the other "the rays", both tied to the real sunlight through the same slots, are one account. Section 9.7 uses the proof on a harder example.

Without the same anchors, nothing more follows. Candidates that split the target differently are different candidates with the same answers. A claim that one way of tying pieces to anchors is "really" the right one must name a separating change, which makes a finer range, a different question. A blurred copy is not a rewriting (section 9.7).

The two earlier texts said more: that any two such candidates are one account, piece paired with piece. A review in September found that false as written, and the outside readers agreed. Draft 4 corrects it. The theory counts any mathematical error in this proof as a way it could be shown wrong, so by the theory's own rule the two earlier texts were shown wrong on this point; draft 4, with the corrected proof, is not (section 13.6).

### 7.9 Departures

- Deutsch judges ease of variation before any rival is offered, and would reject a bad explanation without experiment. Here it shows only when a rival is offered.
- He holds that an explanation which could easily explain anything explains nothing. Here a candidate with such a rival is still an account if it meets the requirements, and a finer question is where a test can decide between the two.
- Here "easy to vary" is relative to the question, and applies to both rivals alike. On the question of the seasons the Greeks knew, even the tilt of the Earth's axis, which he calls hard to vary, is easy to vary relative to the myth, when the two are offered in place of each other and the myth fits what is established. Does the myth fit? Section 4.7 found that it assumes its answer, which fails the third requirement. But that failure was found by examining the myth, not by looking at the world, and the theory does not say whether a failure found that way counts as established. If it does, the myth does not fit, and no problem arises. If not, the two pose a problem outside the range. Either way the theory does not prefer the myth: "easy to vary" ranks nothing, the myth is no account, and the tilt can be one. This point is open (section 14).
- Deutsch sets the Greek myth beside a Norse myth of the seasons about the god Freyr, and finds that they assert incompatible things, yet come down to one core explanation. Myths that differ only in their gods conflict at no allowed change, so here they are not rivals at all.
- His "reach", the power of an explanation to apply beyond the challenges it was made for, has no counterpart here: the word is not defined.
