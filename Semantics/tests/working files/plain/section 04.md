## 4. Which pieces do the work

### Example: the seasons and the sun god

Asked why there are seasons, Tomas says the Earth's tilted axis, like a spinning top's, keeps pointing one way as the Earth circles the sun. For half the year the north leans sunward, gets more direct rays and longer days, and warms; then it leans away and cools. Then he adds: "And the sun god, who approves of this arrangement, keeps the tilt steady."

The **fixed verdict**, written in advance: Tomas explains the seasons; the god sentence does none of the explaining.

No **round** (complete trial of the theory on the cases) has used this **proposed case**, so there is no **ruling** (settled view of what the theory says); below is the change list's view, accepted on an earlier draft by the **outside readers** (two AI systems that cross-examine the project).

### The idea in plain words

Strike out pieces, singly or in groups: does what is left still count? The theory reports which pieces work, without requiring all to.

### What the theory says

#### Cutting a candidate down

A **candidate** is an explanation as offered for a question. Its **commitments** are the pieces it offers as doing the work, whether or not anyone has described their work; the rest is **background**, held fixed. Tomas's include the tilt, the spinning-top point and, if it is a piece at all, the god sentence.

Keep some commitments, background fixed: that is a **cut-down candidate**, whose commitments are those kept. The way of cutting down is stated, not supplied by the theory.

#### Supports and load-bearing groups

A **support** is a group of commitments that on its own, with the background, makes an **account** (a candidate meeting section 2's four requirements): Tomas's commitments without the god sentence, for one. The theory assumes neither that **adding never breaks** a support nor that there is a **smallest support**, one from which nothing can be taken without losing it.

Take the tilt from that support and no seasons are explained: a group is **load-bearing** in a support when the support stops being one without it. A group can be load-bearing though none of its single commitments is. Load-bearing is relative to the support judged: a commitment load-bearing in one support need not be in the whole candidate. Only groups of the written commitments are judged, whether or not anyone has set them out.

#### The tipping edge

For a stated family of changes to the candidate itself (removing the god sentence, or the tilt), the **tipping edge** is the pairs of changed candidates of which one is an account and the other not; those two make one. Nothing else in the theory uses it.

#### The smallest-supports theorem

A door's explanation has a hinge and two springs, either spring enough with the hinge. Its smallest supports are the hinge with each spring: all three are load-bearing somewhere; only the hinge is **needed in every support**.

The **smallest-supports theorem** generalizes this. If the commitments are finitely many, adding never breaks, and the whole is a support, a commitment is load-bearing somewhere exactly when in some smallest support, and needed in every support exactly when in all. The theory proves this; the proof is technical. It says nothing where its assumptions fail, as under interference.

#### The spring nobody mentioned

A door's explanation holds two connected springs, each enough alone. Nobody describes the second as doing anything until the first is removed; then the author says it was always there. The fixed verdict: it was already a route; that nobody said it did work does not make it a later addition.

A **route** is a support of the candidate, whether or not anything runs it. Each spring alone is a support, as are both: **redundant routes**, neither needed. Each is load-bearing where it stands alone; in the whole, only the pair is.

The ruling on file 11, the text in force, **agrees**; on file 10, the text before, it was **split** (readable two ways): one of only two moves **toward** a fixed verdict. File 11 said a route already present is a route whether or not anyone has described its work; draft 4 grounds this more firmly (its view, untried in a round), counting commitments "whether or not anyone has described their work". The second spring is not idle: removed from the support where it is the only spring, it leaves none.

A piece tied to a different target after a removal ("The cable that used to be a spring") makes a different candidate, judged afresh, whose success is not the old one's.

#### Routes that ran, and the same outputs

A route runs in a system when actual events **embody** it (carry it out physically), forming a **route that ran**, through which the difference really passed (section 9). **Credit** for a **repair** (something that had failed now holding) goes to the **contributions** (stretches of events) whose routes that ran led to it, not to routes a candidate contains.

In one door both springs pull; in another the second hangs slack until the first breaks: same outputs, different routes run. The theory proves that if two systems give the same outputs for every input yet differ on a claim about an account, no judgement from outputs alone gets both right; likewise for judging from a text alone where it came from, or use from a delivery log. So the theory does not say predicting is explaining: matching is judged piece by piece under change, and the two doors are different set-ups.

#### Interference

In "One more commitment", every earlier addition kept the conclusion; the latest, that the room was locked, clashes with an assumption every route uses, and the conclusion fails. The ruling on file 11 agrees. This is **interference**: adding a commitment breaks a support. A smaller group succeeds, the whole fails, and calling the addition "not a commitment" is no remedy.

#### Spread-out support

Say commitments put a quantity within one of zero, a half, a third, and so on without end. Any endless selection pins it at zero; no finite one does. In this **spread-out support** no single commitment or smallest group does the work: remove any one bound from a support and a support remains. Yet the whole group is load-bearing.

#### Idle commitments

None of the four requirements asks each commitment to work. In a candidate that has a support, a commitment is an **idle commitment** when adding it to any support and removing it from any support both leave a support. The candidate is then an account with it exactly when without it, and the commitment is load-bearing in no support.

The removal half excludes a redundant route, like the second spring; the adding half, interference, like the locked room. (Without a support, even a wrong commitment would meet both halves.) With endlessly many commitments, idle ones can together be load-bearing: each bound above is idle.

**Departure** from the source books: Deutsch counts idle details as a defect. Here nothing grades a candidate for carrying one.

#### Back to Tomas

If the god sentence restricts nothing, or merely renames the spinning-top point, it is idle, load-bearing in no support, and Tomas's candidate is an account exactly when it would be without it: the fixed verdict on both questions.

As a second piece keeping the tilt steady, the god gives a fork:

- if it matches its **anchor** (the world's pieces it is tied to), it is a redundant route, not idle, and does some of the explaining, against the fixed verdict;
- if not, **piece-by-piece matching** (each commitment behaving like its anchor under every change the question covers) fails: interference, so the whole candidate is no account, and "Tomas explains" is at risk.

Which holds depends on what the sentence is taken to say and to be tied to; the fixed verdict is the same either way, the theory's answer is not. On no view is the telling without the sentence, or with another god, Tomas's **rival**, one offered in its place and conflicting with it (section 5).

### What changed in this revision

- Gone: file 11's claim that giving an explanation more "explanatory jobs" never leaves more of its changed candidates passing, and its remarks on that. Your decision of 24–25 September on hard to vary holds that no one can list all rivals, even in principle; nothing now counts, grades or ranks candidates (section 5).
- Said outright: no requirement asks each commitment to work; idle is defined by both halves, given a support.
- A cut-down candidate's commitments are those kept; supports judged are groups of the written commitments; routes are kept apart from routes that ran, which credit follows.

### What is open

- Which view of the god sentence holds; the text does not say.
- The way of cutting down, and the family of changes behind the tipping edge, are not among the **stated inputs**, what a claim must state.
- Counting idle commitments as a defect would change what the theory claims.
