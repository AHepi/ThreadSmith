## 0. What this theory is for, and how to read this

### An example

"The shadow is ten metres long because the pole is five metres tall and the sun is low." Make the pole taller and the shadow lengthens; lengthen the shadow with a board's shade and the pole does not grow. The changes, not the names, show which depends on which.

### The idea

The theory, named "Claude Fable Semantics", says what makes something an explanation, and a thinker creative at explaining: at making explanations, or questions, that did not exist before.

### What the theory claims

- An explanation is a **set-up** of **pieces** that depend on one another, relevant to a question, held to its **target** in the world by a **link** judged only by **matching**: whether the two behave alike under each change the question covers. Whether a piece is "the same kind of thing" as a piece of the world is never asked; at a level of detail, a **kind** is just how a piece responds to the allowed changes, whatever it is called (section 3).
- A **picture** (not only an image) is a thing whose set-up has a selected or constructed matching link to what it pictures. A link has a **history**: it is **selected** (by variation and survival, nothing in that history picturing the target), **constructed** (by guessing and criticism) or merely **declared** by whoever writes the model down. Creativity lives in construction, which works on what selection supplies; no claim of creativity may rest on declaring (section 7).
- Questions have a history and can be found, not only answered; a theory taking them as given cannot describe finding them, and finding is at least half of creativity (section 8).
- The **central guess**: four things fully describe creativity: set-ups and their allowed changes; links judged by matching alone; the history of links and questions; and the physical things that **embody** them (have them, at some level of detail). An achievement in explaining that these cannot describe counts against it, as does something meeting all four yet plainly explaining nothing (section 11).

### What it does not claim

- That matter alone makes a picture: a history is needed too.
- That predicting is explaining: the same outputs by different **routes** inside (ways to the answer) make different set-ups (section 4).
- That it supplies true answers about beauty, a probability of truth, a merit score, a measure of worth or a ranking of thinkers. Worth and artistic value come from a **standard of worth** taken from outside; a claim needing the others is **unsettled**: the theory gives no verdict, and says so. Nor does it share out credit beyond what the course of events shows (sections 9 and 11).
- That anyone or anything belongs to the **classes** of systems it defines; it only defines them (section 10).

### The six principles

1. Whether a link matches is a fact, accepted or not; a system can be wrong about its links, its **readings** (what it observes), its criticisms and what it can do (section 2).
2. A theory can be right about one dependence and wrong elsewhere, but an error in the very dependence said to do the work never counts as an **account**, a **candidate** (any explanation offered, even a false one) meeting section 2's four requirements; whether an account is "easy to vary" is a separate matter, and "hard to vary" is not defined (section 5).
3. Ideas may be held without proof; a criticism is itself a guess; one may act without proving first (section 9).
4. Any aspect of a system's practice can be criticized, and the criticism must be able to change how the system goes on (section 10).
5. Any **sort of matter** may embody a set-up, as far as **the physics** (the adopted theory of physics) allows, and pass what it bears to another sort only as far as the physics lets it; where two sorts cannot exchange what they bear, what only one can bear is a **barrier** for systems of the other, closed to them under any fair aid (section 10).
6. Selection and construction both make links, told apart by their histories; neither reduces to the other (section 7).

### How to read this

Each special word keeps one meaning, from the word sheet; each section runs from an example to what is open.

A **case** is a short everyday situation with a **fixed verdict**, a careful person's, written in advance. In a **round**, file 11, the text in force, was run against 52 cases: it **agreed** with 46 fixed verdicts, disagreed with none, and was **silent** on 6, each lacking something the case does not state.

**Draft 4** is file 11 with 57 changes, 51 of them **changes of claim**, from which a reader can conclude something file 11 did not.

The **source books** are the owner's two named sources, by Chiara Marletto and David Deutsch; sections name the **departures** from them, made on purpose.

### What changed in this revision

File 11 said a claim needing any of these measures must state it, which nothing else in file 11 bore out; draft 4 now says what the third point just above says.

### What is open

Draft 4 is not frozen. The **outside readers**, two AI systems outside the project, have not seen the fixes made after their cross-examination, nor the last changes, such as the paragraphs on rivals, problems and failed answers. No round has used it (section 12).
