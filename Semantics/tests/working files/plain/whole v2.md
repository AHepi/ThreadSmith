## The theory in one page

The theory is called "Claude Fable Semantics". Its subtitle, "a structural class of explanatory creativity, with selected and constructed correspondence", says two things in plain words. The theory describes creativity at explaining by its structure alone, by what depends on what. And the links between a system's ideas and the world they are about come about in two ways: shaped by selection, or built by the system.

The theory tries to say exactly what makes something an explanation, and what makes a system creative at making explanations, or questions, that did not exist before. It does so in claims that can be checked against **cases**, short everyday stories with an agreed verdict (section 1), and that could be shown wrong.

Its first example: "The shadow is ten metres long because the pole is five metres tall and the sun is low." Raise the pole and the shadow lengthens. Fix the shadow's length directly, say with a board laid so the shade ends where you choose, and the pole stays as it was. The changes, not the words "pole" and "shadow", show that the dependence runs from pole to shadow. The theory judges every explanation this way: only by what happens when things are changed.

In outline:

- The theory describes the world, and every explanation of it, as a **set-up**: **slots** that hold values (the pole's height, the shadow's length), **pieces** that tie slots together (the sunlight), and **changes** that can be made (raising the pole). A **question** says which changes it covers and what it asks (section 2).
- An explanation offered for a question is a **candidate**. It counts, and is called an **account**, when it meets four **requirements**, all about how it behaves under the question's changes (section 3).
- Some hold that an explanation must pair each of its pieces with a piece of the world "of the same kind". The theory says there is nothing more to a kind than how a piece responds to changes, so it need not be required (section 4). It reports which pieces of an explanation do the work, without demanding that all of them do (section 5).
- Two candidates, one offered in place of the other, that disagree somewhere are **rivals**. When nothing yet found out decides between them, they pose a **problem**. A candidate is **easy to vary** when it has a rival that fits what is known as well as it does and disagrees with it only outside what the question covers. A village rule, "sow in October, after the first rains", is an example: at home "in October" and "after the first rains" say the same, and they come apart only in a land far to the south, which the village's question never covered. Nobody needs a list of every rival there could be; the rivals someone has offered are enough (section 6).
- A question narrowed after a failure is a different question. On the question it failed, a wrong answer stays wrong, whoever offers it again. What can lapse is only the finding that showed it wrong, if an assumption behind that finding is withdrawn (section 7).
- A **system** is anything the theory is applied to, such as a person, an animal, a machine or an institution. The **link** between a system's pictures and what they picture has a **history**. It was shaped by selection, with nothing in that history picturing what the link is about; or built through guessing and criticism; or merely written into a model. Creativity lives only in the building (section 8). A system **originates** something when it builds it, the thing is new to the system, and the system uses it on a question. Finding a question counts too (section 9).
- Whether a fault was mended (a **repair**), who gets the **credit**, and whether knowledge was created are read from what actually happened. In "Two plumbers", the one who explained the leak correctly did not stop it, and the one who stopped it had no explanation (section 10). Whether a system has an ability is judged inside a stated **boundary**, the line that says what belongs to the system, and what it can do never goes beyond what the physics makes possible (section 11).

The theory's **central guess** is that creativity at explaining can be fully described with four things. They are not the four requirements above. They are: set-ups and the changes they allow; links between set-ups, judged only by how they behave under change; the history of those links and of the questions they serve; and how all of this is embodied in physical things. An achievement these four cannot describe would count against the guess. So would something that has all four and plainly explains nothing.

The theory does not claim that matter alone fixes what a thing stands for (a history is needed too), or that predicting is explaining. It does not say what is beautiful or worthwhile, how probable an explanation is, or which thinker is better, and it does not share out credit beyond what the course of events shows. It defines **classes** of systems, such as the universal class of section 11, and does not claim that any person or machine belongs to one.

The theory has two foundations: the physics and, where a question is about worth, a standard of worth taken from outside. Everything else is built from those two, from each claim's **frame** (what the claim is relative to, such as its range of changes), and from the **stated inputs** that a claim takes as given and states (section 12). The theory names five **main claims**, each with what would defeat it, and counts any mathematical error in its proofs as a further way to be shown wrong (section 12).

This document is also a progress report. Draft 4 has not yet been tried on the cases. Several verdicts are open (section 14). On one example, "The seasons and the sun god", one view of the text may go against the careful person's verdict (section 5). And one of the theory's listed ways of being shown wrong was triggered by the two older texts of the theory, not by draft 4 (sections 6 and 12).

## 2. The world as a set-up, and questions about it

### The fever and the thermometer

Hana says the child has a fever, since the thermometer reads thirty-nine. Later she says the reading is what makes the child shiver. Warm the thermometer in her hand: the reading climbs, and the child shivers as before. Cool the child in a bath: the shivering eases. *Fixed verdict:* her first statement is a good reason to believe there is a fever; her second mistakes the instrument for the illness.

### A set-up

Take the pole in the sun. The pole's height, the sun's height and the shadow's length are **slots**, places that each hold a **value**. The sunlight is a **piece**, a working element that ties slots together: it fixes the shadow's length from the other two. The flat ground belongs to the **circumstances**, held fixed while changes are made. Raising the pole is a **change**.

A **set-up**, then, has four things: slots, with the values they can take; pieces; circumstances; and **allowed changes**, the changes the set-up counts as ones that can be made. Changes can be chained one after another, and "no change" counts as one. Under each change and circumstances, each piece has a **behaviour**: the combinations of values it allows among its slots. A **state** gives every slot a value. The **consistent states** are those that every piece allows at once.

A few rules keep this description exact.

- A **removed piece** no longer restricts its slots at all. Take the sunlight away, and nothing fixes the shadow's length.
- A change that **sets** a slot fixes its value directly. It replaces the piece that used to fix that slot, rather than leaving the old piece in place beside a clashing second one. The board that fixes the shadow's length is such a change: it takes over from the sunlight's hold on the shadow.
- A changed status rule (below, and section 4) is a changed piece.
- Values need not be numbers: they may be paths, proofs or histories. Pieces may depend on one another in a loop. Where several states are consistent, the theory keeps them all rather than picking one.

Changes need not be physical. The seventh complaint says "mathematics has no interventions": nothing in mathematics can be reached into and altered, as a pole can be raised. The theory replies that removing a starting assumption, dropping a constraint, or changing a dimension (such as the number of rows of a table) are changes too. Section 3 works one through.

### Roles and direction

Roles are not labelled in advance; they come from the allowed changes.

- An **input slot** is a slot that some allowed change sets directly, such as the pole's height.
- An **output slot** is a slot that a piece fixes from its other slots, such as the shadow's length.
- A **reading** is a slot that reports another, where some allowed change alters the report without altering what is reported, as warming the thermometer does.

**Direction** follows too. Raise the pole and the shadow lengthens; set the shadow directly and the pole stays put. So dependence runs from pole to shadow. That holds whichever way round the calculation linking them is written: "shadow from height" and "height from shadow" use the same numbers, but only one of them behaves like the world when things are changed.

### A question

Take Hana's second question, "what makes the child shiver?" A **question** has five things.

- A **target**: the set-up in the world that the question is about. Here, the child, her temperature, the thermometer and its reading.
- A **range**: the **settings** it covers, a setting being one allowed change made in given circumstances. Here, cooling the child in a bath, or warming the thermometer in a hand. The **untouched setting**, with no change made and ordinary circumstances, is always in the range.
- **What is asked**: the exact thing read off the target at each setting. Here, whether she shivers.
- The demands and safeguards in play, which matter only when a question is about mending something (section 10).
- The history of its range: how the range came about (below).

The **answer** is what the target gives, for what is asked, at each setting. Here, she shivers at the untouched setting and when the thermometer is warmed, and shivers less after the bath. A candidate (section 3) also gives an answer at each setting, and it is right there when its answer is the target's. The word "answer" is never used here for a candidate itself.

A range holds the settings the question states, and no more. In **The table with empty columns**, Lea's question about turning two freezer knobs together is rewritten as a table with one column for each knob. Every row moves both knobs, and no row moves one alone. *Fixed verdict:* "The table is faithful. Its columns could have carried single-knob rows, and none was added. A form that could ask more has not asked more." *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* Nobody makes a mistake here. The point is that a range holds only the settings actually stated, however much more its form could hold.

The sort of question is fixed by what it reads and what its range holds, never by a label.

- A **producing question** asks what produces an outcome. Its range has settings that change what comes before the outcome. ("What makes her shiver?")
- A **pinning-down question** asks what a reading settles about a feature. Its range runs over the different readings that could come up. ("The thermometer reads thirty-nine: has she a fever?", asked for each reading it might show.)
- A **blocking question** asks whether one state can be reached from another at all, or whether something blocks the way. ("Why can no machine run forever?", below.)
- A **status question** asks what status something has under a **status rule**, a rule that gives things a status, as the rules of chess make a move legal. (No case in the books is about one.)
- A **purpose question** asks whether a purpose is achieved.

A different range, or a different thing asked, makes a **different question**, and an answer to one is not an answer to another. A reading that pins down an outcome, with a reliable forecast from it, answers the pinning-down question. Whether the thing read also produces the outcome is the producing question, and the first answer is not the second.

**Back to Hana.** *Ruled on file 11: agrees; draft 4 does not change it.* The thermometer's number is a reading: change only the reading and the child is unchanged; change the child and the reading follows. It answers "has she a fever?", a pinning-down question. "What makes her shiver?" is a producing question. Its range has settings that change her temperature, and there the reading does nothing.

### Pinning down and blocking: two of the theory's examples

**Two balances** (the theory's example). An object is weighed on two balances. Each shows the object's mass plus that balance's unknown error. The readings settle the difference between the two errors, but not the mass: put a gram more mass and a gram less error on each balance, and both readings stay the same. So the example explains a limit on what the readings can settle.

In general, the **look-alike states** of a reading are all the states that give that reading. A reading **pins down** a feature when some state gives the reading and all its look-alike states agree on the feature. The theory's **proof on pinning down** shows that a feature is pinned down by every reading that can occur exactly when the feature can be worked out from the reading alone.

Two things follow. Repeating the same weighing adds nothing, since it has the same look-alike states. A **calibration**, a reading taken against something already known, can add something. And "take balance B's error to be zero, because that gives the mass I favour" is not drawn from the readings: its reasoning goes in a circle, even if the mass it gives is right. (Section 17 gives the exact statement for balances.)

**Twenty-three tokens** (the theory's example). Twenty-three whole tokens cannot be shared equally among three people. Handing a token from one person to another never changes the total, and three equal whole shares always make a total that three divides, which twenty-three is not.

An **unchanging quantity** is one that no allowed change alters, like the total here. The theory's **proof on blocking** shows that no chain of allowed changes joins two states where an unchanging quantity differs. The reverse does not hold. Two states with the same total need not be **reachable** one from the other, where reachable means that some chain of allowed changes leads from one to the other. Allowing tokens to be cut makes different states, and a different question; it does not refute a claim about whole tokens.

### A case about blocking

**The machine that runs forever.** At a fair, visitors ask why no machine drives a water pump forever, with no fuel, no wind, no flowing water and nobody pushing it. Pia says hundreds of designs have been tried and every one has stopped, and perhaps someone will find one that works. Quentin says the energy anything gives out must be matched by energy put in or used up, and that holds for any design, tried or not. *Fixed verdict:* only Quentin's answer explains; Pia gives evidence and a hope, but no reason.

*Not yet ruled.* The list of changes expects the verdict to hold. It notes one risk: a reader might call Quentin's energy law the answer restated, which the third requirement forbids (section 3). *This document's view* of the reasoning: it is a blocking question. Quentin gives an unchanging quantity, energy, so no chain of changes leads to a pump that works with nothing used up, whatever the design. Pia gives a list of failures. It has no piece that responds to any change, so it is a table of observed answers (section 3), and it fails the first requirement.

### Where a range comes from, and faulty questions

A range has one of three histories.

- **Declared**: written in by the **modeller**, whoever writes the model down. ("The range is: raise or lower the pole.")
- **Selected**: the survivor of a pool of ranges that were varied and weeded out, with nothing in that history picturing the range (section 8 describes selection). No case in the books shows a selected range.
- **Constructed**: made by a system in an episode, roughly a stretch of inquiry from a noticed difficulty to a response (section 10 says what a complete one holds), with evidence of how it was made (section 9). Noor's question in "The rota" (section 9) is the example.

Judging whether a candidate is an account works the same way whichever history its range has. Only a claim that someone *found* a question needs a constructed range, with the evidence.

**Where the second compartment used to be.** A change removes a freezer's second compartment. Lea had asked for that compartment's temperature. After the change, a colleague answers with the temperature of the space where it used to be. *Fixed verdict:* "A meaning has been supplied, and the question now asks something. It is not what Lea asked." *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

A **faulty question** is one of three sorts.

- It misses its target. On *this document's view*, once the compartment is gone, Lea's question no longer picks out anything at that setting.
- It assumes an untouched setting that cannot be: its starting point has no consistent state, as a question about a freezer both running and unplugged would.
- It asks for things that clash, such as one temperature that is at once the compartment's and the empty space's.

A faulty question was still asked, and its asking is an event like any other. Showing that it is faulty is itself a different question, with its own range. What is forbidden is changing the range, or what is asked, during an **assessment**, one act of judging a candidate with the range held fixed, without recording that the claim has changed. Supplying a meaning for a replacement question makes a different question; it does not answer the first one.

The theory gives no way of choosing the level of detail at which a question should be asked (section 3 explains level of detail). Draft 4's one change to the theory's text on questions concerns limits, and is in section 7.

## 3. When an explanation counts: the four requirements

### The tide table with a real bell attached

Carla explains why the harbour water is high at noon today with two things. One is the printed almanac of high and low water for every day. The other is a real float that, rising past a mark, tips a lever and rings the harbour bell. *Fixed verdict:* "Carla has explained the bell. She has explained nothing about the tide. The almanac is the answer written down."

### A candidate

Take the explanation of the shadow as a candidate for the question "what fixes the shadow's length?", whose settings raise the pole and move the sun.

- The candidate has a set-up: slots for the height, the sun's angle and the shadow, and a piece, "light travels in straight lines", which fixes the shadow's length from the other two.
- Its **link** says which of the target's states, changes and circumstances go with which of the candidate's. Raising the real pole goes with raising the pole in the candidate. The link **carries over** each setting of the target to the candidate's matching setting.
- The link also ties each piece of the candidate to an **anchor**: a **cluster** (a connected bunch) of the target's pieces that the candidate's piece must behave like. The candidate's light piece is tied to the real sunlight.
- A **slot pairing** says which slot of the anchor goes with which slot of the piece: the candidate's height slot with the real pole's height, and so on.
- The **commitments** are the pieces the candidate offers as doing the work, whether or not anyone has described what work they do (section 5 has a case of a piece nobody described). Here, the light piece is the commitment.
- Everything else the candidate holds fixed is **background**: the values of its circumstances, and its other pieces, including any piece that gives an input slot its value. Here the pole's height and the sun's angle are input slots, and whatever gives them their values (the pole as built, the time of day) is background.

In general, anything offered as an explanation for a question, true or false, is a **candidate**. An **account** is a candidate that meets the four requirements below.

### The four requirements

A candidate is an **account** of a question exactly when it meets four requirements, each about how it behaves at the settings of the range. None looks at a label.

**1. Matching.** This has two halves, and both are needed.

- *Piece by piece:* at every setting of the range, each commitment allows exactly what its anchor allows, once the anchor's **hidden slots** are set aside (slots inside the anchor that the commitment does not mention). The candidate's light piece gives the same shadow for each height and sun as the real sunlight does.
- *As a whole:* at every setting, the target's consistent states, carried over, are exactly the candidate's consistent states; and a chain of changes in the target carries over as the same chain in the candidate.

Why both? A candidate can get the whole right while splitting it into the wrong pieces. For instance, an explanation of a door hung on two hinges might say that the top hinge carries all the weight: the door still hangs straight, but that piece does not behave like the real top hinge. And pieces that each behave right can lose something that holds them together. For instance, two pieces might each describe one of two meshed gears correctly, in an explanation that forgets the gears are meshed; then the whole allows one gear to turn while the other stands still, which the world does not allow.

**2. Answering the question asked.** At every setting of the range, the candidate gives the target's answer, with what is asked held fixed. An account of a different thing asked is not an account of this one.

**3. No assumed answer.** The answer must follow by working out the candidate's set-up in its circumstances. This has two halves.

- *The answer is not written in.* The target's answer may not appear unexplained, as a value put into an input slot or as a piece, even one labelled "law". This is judged at the stated **level of detail**, how finely things are described. Whether a statement is the answer written in is judged by its form at that level, not by treating every mathematically equivalent truth as the same statement. In mathematics every true statement is equivalent to every other, so on that view every proof would "assume its answer". A long working-out, step by step, is not the bare answer "this table cannot be undone", though the two are equivalent (the tables example, below).
- *Some commitments make the difference.* At some setting of the range, the answer must differ from the answer at the untouched setting (or not be fixed there in the way claimed). And some **group** of commitments must carry that difference: delete the group from the candidate, and the candidate's answers at the two settings come out fixed and equal, or one of them is no longer fixed. In the salt case below, salt spread and salt swept off give two different answers (melted, not melted); delete the salt's action from the explanation and nothing tells the two settings apart.

Both halves are about one thing: the answer must come from the commitments, not from what the candidate takes as given.

**4. No dodging.** The target has a consistent state at the untouched setting. And the range is a stated selection from the **possible** changes, those the laws of physics allow: any possible change the range leaves out is left out by a stated **limit**.

One consequence relates the fourth requirement to the third. Suppose a range's settings make only **renaming changes**, changes that rename things and alter nothing else, or leaves out every change at which the commitments could matter. Then the commitments have no difference to make, so the range fails the third requirement, and no account can be claimed on it.

**Back to Carla.** *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* Her only piece about the tide is the almanac, which is the answer written in, so she fails the third requirement. A real mechanism that answers another question beside it (why the bell rings) does not change this. Draft 4 also says which of her pieces are commitments, and no group of them makes an account of the tide.

**The domino that never falls.** A network of dominoes checks whether a number is prime: one particular domino falls only if a divisor is found. Fed 641, it never falls. Answer A: nothing ever knocks it over. Answer B: 641 is prime. *Fixed verdict:* B explains; A, though true, does not really.

*Not yet ruled.* "The domino never falls" and "nothing ever knocks it over" say the same thing, the second at the level of single dominoes. So A's only working piece is the answer restated. B has a piece that responds to a change: feed in 640, which has divisors, and the domino falls.

### What the requirements exclude, and what they do not

- A **table of observed answers** lists answers seen, with no piece that responds when the world is changed. It fails matching on any range with a setting that makes such a change. A **full response table** records how the set-up responds to every allowed change. It passes matching, and it is judged on the other three requirements like any other candidate. The word "table" settles nothing. (Carla's almanac lists answers, and it fails the third requirement.)
- A **calculation run backwards** works an earlier quantity out from a later one and offers that as what produces it. On the producing question it fails whole matching. Raise the pole, and the world's shadow lengthens; but in the calculation, where the shadow comes first, the shadow stays as it was. It may match on the pinning-down question, which is a different question.
- "It is so because it is so" fails the third requirement. So does a candidate whose only real piece restates the answer, whatever mechanism for another question sits beside it.
- A candidate fails the fourth requirement when the only real difference it makes is one that no possible change could bring about: its range was narrowed without saying so. For instance, an explanation of a pendulum whose only working difference lies between gravity as it is and gravity switched off, when nothing possible switches gravity off.

**The planets tonight and a thousand years ago.** Priya calculates where the planets were a thousand years ago from where they are tonight, and says they were there *because* they are here tonight. *Fixed verdict:* the earlier arrangement, with the laws of motion, explains tonight's; not the reverse.

*Not yet ruled.* In the forward explanation, the laws of motion are the commitments, and the old positions are background, giving the input slots their values. Removing the laws loses the difference between settings, so the forward explanation meets the third requirement. Priya's is a calculation run backwards. Under changes to the earlier positions, it fails whole matching. Under changes to tonight's positions, which leave the past as it was, there is no difference for it to carry. *This document's view* adds that her "because" answers "where were they?", a pinning-down question, following the calculation run backwards above.

**Salt on the icy step.** Ines says the ice melted because she spread salt: spread it, and the ice melts within the hour; sweep it off at once, and the ice stays. She knows nothing finer. *Fixed verdict:* a genuine explanation, a coarse one. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* A coarse account answers the coarse question, and its strength is fixed by its range, not by what a finer range would add.

Draft 4 states the reason more directly (*not yet ruled*). Her explanation has two things in it. Spreading the salt sets whether salt is on the step, an input slot, so the spreading is background. What the salt does to the ice is the commitment. The difference between spread and swept lies in the input slot; what carries that difference to the ice is the salt's action, and removing it loses the difference.

Nor do the requirements exclude a true mechanism guessed for bad reasons, or prefer an elegant account to a clumsier one that matches as well. A proof meets them for the question "why does this follow from these assumptions?" without answering "what caused this?". How deep an explanation goes, how far into finer workings, depends on the question, and the requirements do not measure it.

### Odd-sized tables: a mathematical example

This is the theory's example, and it replies to the seventh complaint, that mathematics has no changes. Take square tables of numbers with an odd number of rows, where each number is the negative of its mirror image across the diagonal (so the diagonal is all noughts). No such table can be undone: no other table reverses it.

Why? The question's range drops the mirroring, or the oddness, or both, holding ordinary arithmetic fixed. The long working-out of the number that decides whether a table can be undone, with the mirroring put in, meets all four requirements, so it is an account. Drop the mirroring, or drop the oddness, and a table that can be undone appears, so the difference is carried by those two commitments. A three-line proof is shorter, but shortness is not a fifth requirement. Here the changes are dropped assumptions, which is the answer to the complaint. Section 17 gives the working.

### Explanations of absence

**Denying the inner experience.** Wren says there are no inner experiences, and people are mistaken in thinking they have them. Yuri says the same, and adds that people looking inwards consult memories of what they have just perceived, through machinery that evolved to report experiences that never took place. *Fixed verdict:* Wren gives no reason why people believe they have inner experiences; Yuri gives one, though an incomplete one.

A question "why is there no such-and-such?" works like this.

- Its target lacks the pieces that the opposing view needs: here, the inner experiences themselves.
- Its range holds the changes that would add those pieces.
- An **explanation of absence** passes when adding those pieces would change its answer as it would change the target's, and leaving them out changes neither.
- The opposing view's imagined pieces are tied to a removed cluster, which restricts nothing (section 2), so pieces that claim to do work there have nothing to match.

Why the absent thing *seems* present is a different question. An account of the absence neither answers it nor needs to. A **bare denial** offers no piece that responds to a change in what produces the appearance, so it is no account of that second question.

*Not yet ruled.* Wren's is a bare denial. Yuri offers such a piece: machinery that reads memories and produces reports. Whether it is a good one, the question does not ask.

*Departure:* Deutsch asks an explanation that denies something to explain why it seems to exist; here that is a different question.

### Candidates and accounts

**A myth about winter.** A Greek farmer asks why winter comes every year, and is told the myth: Persephone must return to Hades for some months of every year, and while she is away her mother Demeter grieves and makes the world cold. Taken as it stood for the Greeks, the myth agrees with all they knew. *Fixed verdict:* it does not explain why winter comes every year; it is an explanation only in form, an attempt, and a false one.

*Not yet ruled.* The yearly return is written into Persephone's bargain, so the answer is assumed, and the myth is no account. A false explanation offered for a question is a candidate, which ordinary speech may call an explanation. One outside reader (of two) named this reasoning as a move toward the fixed verdict on an earlier draft, and the project kept the text.

Two **principles** of the theory stand behind the requirements.

- Whether a link matches is a fact, whether or not anyone accepts it. A system can be wrong about its links, its readings, its criticisms and its abilities.
- An explanation may hold a correct dependence alongside errors elsewhere. But an error in the very dependence said to do the work cannot count. In this principle, "explanation" means an account.

In this document, "explanation" is an everyday word for anything offered as the reason something is so; "candidate" and "account" are the theory's terms. Whether an account is easy to vary is a separate matter (section 6).

*Departure:* Deutsch counts a false myth as an explanation; here it is a candidate.

## 4. Kinds are how pieces respond

### The float that does two jobs

A float in a fuel tank turns a dial on the dashboard and, when it drops low enough, opens a valve to the reserve tank. Milan says the falling float opens the valve; Lena says the dial reaching "low" does. Bend the needle to read "full": the valve still opens. Hold the float up with a wire: the dial reads "full" and the valve stays shut. *Fixed verdict:* Milan is right; "The float is read by the dial and also does the work."

Two changes, not the names "float" and "dial", showed which piece does the work. That is the theory's whole view of kinds.

### Response patterns and kinds

Across the two changes above, the float's hold on the valve behaves one way and the dial another: bending the needle leaves the valve as it was, and holding the float up does not. A piece's **response pattern** is its behaviour at every setting of a range, taken together.

Two pieces are one **kind** on a range when their slots can be paired one to one so that their response patterns agree. So kinds depend on the range. Pieces that no allowed change tells apart are one kind, whatever they are called. They may come apart on a **finer range**, one with more settings. A response pattern is built from behaviour, not from the values the slots happen to hold, so pieces that differ only in their values are one kind.

The same comparison is made between a piece of one candidate and a piece of another, and between a commitment and its anchor.

**Two thermostats.** Two thermostats of one make are set to eighteen and twenty-two degrees. Rosa says they are one kind of thing in two states; Emil says they are two kinds, one keeping a warm room and one a cool one. *Fixed verdict:* Rosa is right about what the devices are; Emil's difference matters only when the question is why this room is warm.

*Ruled on file 11: agrees*, though several readers in the round marked the theory silent; the ruling weighed their reasons (section 1). Two points settle it, and draft 4 now states both outright. A change that sets a slot, such as turning a dial from eighteen to twenty-two, replaces only what fixes that slot's value; it does not change how the pieces that read the slot behave. And a change under which two pieces behave alike does not tell them apart. Eighteen and twenty-two are values of one slot, and both devices behave alike whatever it holds.

### Cause, measurement and status rule

Take the pole again. Set the shadow's length directly, with the board, and the sunlight's hold on the shadow is replaced. Misread the shadow with a badly held ruler, and the sunlight is unaffected. Now the thermometer: set the child's temperature directly, with a bath, and the thermometer's way of turning temperature into a number stays the same, while the number it shows follows the child. Warm the thermometer in a hand, and its way of turning temperature into a number is altered.

What ordinary speech calls a cause, a measurement or a rule is a family of response patterns, not an extra fact.

- A **cause** is a piece whose behaviour changes when its output slot is set directly or when the piece is replaced, and is unaffected when a reading of it is altered. The sunlight is a cause of the shadow's length.
- A **measurement** is a piece whose behaviour is unaffected when the thing measured is set directly, and changes when the measuring itself is altered. The thermometer's measuring is one.
- A **status rule** is a piece unaffected by changes to the world, which changes when the rule itself is edited. It answers a status question. Whether it governs a practice, or should, are different questions.

So the theory never asks whether a piece "is" a cause. A slot that reports another has a measurement's pattern: change only the report, and the other stays as it was; change the other, and the report follows. (The measuring piece's behaviour stays the same; the value it shows follows.) Which of the two an explanation offers as producing an outcome is settled by the pattern, not by the explanation's wording.

**Back to the float.** *Ruled on file 11: agrees; draft 4 does not change it.* The dial has a measurement's pattern, and the float has a cause's pattern for the valve.

### Three complaints

*"Without kinds you cannot tell a cause from a correlation."* You can, and only this way. A correlation has no piece that responds when its supposed input slot is changed; a cause has one. A label adds nothing. If some allowed change tells the two apart, matching already finds it, as holding the float up with a wire told the float from the dial. If no allowed change tells them apart, the label claims a difference that the level of detail does not have.

*"So a thing is whatever you can do to it."* No. A kind is only what the allowed changes at a level of detail can tell apart. The explanation itself keeps every one of its pieces, including pieces that no change at that level tells apart. Those are one kind at that level, and that is correct, not a loss. On a finer range, with more changes, they may come apart. So the claim is only that every claim about kinds is tied to a level of detail.

*"Kinds obviously exist. A rule is not a cause."* Agreed that a rule is not a cause, and the difference is one of response pattern, as above. What the theory denies is that the difference exists before, or apart from, the allowed changes.

### No requirement about kinds

Should each piece of an account have to be tied to a piece of the world "of the same kind"? Such an **anchoring requirement** would be redundant or empty. If the range has a setting that tells the two kinds apart, a piece tied to the wrong one already fails piece-by-piece matching. If it has none, they are one kind on that range. There is no third case.

The theory's **proof that kinds add nothing** shows that piece-by-piece matching gives each commitment its anchor's response pattern, setting by setting. So "kind" can be dropped from the definition of an account without losing any case. That is the third of the five main claims (section 12). It would be defeated by a label that told apart two accounts which no allowed change tells apart, where the difference did real explaining.

### Matching over many steps

**Keeping count with string.** Two groups of goatherds count their flocks with string, and every length of string they measure out is off by up to a tenth of an inch. In the first group, as each goat leaves the pen in the morning, a herder reels out a length of string equal to that goat's length, and reels it back in as the goat returns. In the second group, each goat stands for exactly one inch, and after every goat the herder trims or lengthens the string to the nearest whole inch. Both groups also add flocks together by joining strings, and split flocks by cutting a string in two. *Fixed verdict:* only the rounding method keeps count of a flock of any size; the first piles up error until it is larger than a goat.

Three proofs of the theory concern chains of changes like these.

- **Matching step by step.** If an explanation matches the world for each single change, it matches for every chain of those changes, so long as each step stays where the link applies. Nobody needs to check every chain.
- **Two-way and one-way matching.** Treating two set-ups as equivalent needs **two-way matching**, where each side can always follow the other's changes. **One-way matching**, the candidate following the target, is enough for a simplified copy. Two links chained together keep both directions when each covers what the next needs.
- **Error build-up.** This is for candidates that match only approximately. It says how small errors add up over many steps.

The **proof on error build-up** works like this, in the goatherds' terms. Suppose each step of the candidate lands at most a small **step error** away from where the target's step lands: here, a tenth of an inch per goat, join or cut. And suppose one step of the candidate never enlarges an existing gap by more than a fixed **widening factor**. Then, from a shared start, the gap after many steps is at most the step errors added up, each enlarged by the widening factor once for every later step. Without a widening factor, no ceiling on the gap follows at all. The proof is about approximate matching, and it never turns a question that asks for exact matching into one that accepts approximate matching. Section 17 gives it exactly.

*Not yet ruled.* For the first group the widening factor is one: a step never enlarges an existing gap. So the ceiling grows by a tenth of an inch for every goat, join or cut, without limit. The proof gives only a ceiling; it does not show that the error must grow. The rounding method is outside the proof: rounding corrects the error at every step, which the proof does not describe. So the proof says nothing about the second half of the verdict, which rests on the case's reasoning.

## 5. Which pieces do the work

### The seasons and the sun god

Tomas explains the seasons. The Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space as the Earth goes round the sun, as a spinning top keeps its axis steady. For half the year each half of the Earth leans towards the sun, and for the other half it leans away. Each step can be checked on its own. Then he adds: "And the sun god, who approves of this arrangement, keeps the tilt steady." *Fixed verdict:* "Yes, Tomas explains the seasons. No, the sun-god sentence is not part of what explains them."

The theory's verdict here is not settled. On one view of the text it gives the fixed verdict; on another it may not. The end of this section explains.

### Supports and load-bearing groups

Tomas's explanation has several commitments: the tilt, the axis keeping one direction, the point about spinning bodies, and the sun god. Keep only some of them, with the background held fixed, and you have a **cut-down candidate**, cut down in a stated way. Keep the first three, say.

A **support** is a group of commitments that by itself, with the background, makes an account. The theory does not assume that adding a commitment to a support always leaves a support ("One more commitment", below, shows it can fail). Nor does it assume that every support contains a smallest one.

A group is **load-bearing** in a support when the support stops being one without it. A group can be load-bearing though none of its members is alone. With two springs, each enough by itself to swing a door back (below), taking both away breaks the support, while taking either one away leaves the other doing the job.

Load-bearing is relative to the support being judged. And the supports judged are groups drawn from the commitments the candidate actually has, whether or not anyone has pointed to a particular group as a support. They are never groups that need commitments the candidate does not carry.

**A premise both routes use.** Two lines of proof, which the case calls routes, lead to the answer, and both use premise P. The second line could be rewritten without P, but nobody has written that version. *Fixed verdict:* P is load-bearing in each line as written; that another line could be written without it is "a fact about a candidate nobody has written". *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* Draft 4 adds that a candidate nobody has offered is no one's rival (section 6).

A **smallest support** is one from which nothing can be taken without losing it. Where there are finitely many commitments, adding a commitment never breaks a support, and the whole candidate is a support, the theory's **proof on smallest supports** shows something simple. A single commitment is load-bearing in some support exactly when it belongs to some smallest support. It is **needed in every support** exactly when it belongs to all of them. So, in that setting, finding the smallest supports tells you which commitments do the work. The proof holds only where its assumptions do. Section 17 states it exactly.

### Two springs, and supports that run

**The spring nobody mentioned.** An explanation of why a door swings back holds two connected springs, each enough alone. The second is never described as doing anything, until the first is deleted from the explanation; then the author says the second was always there. *Fixed verdict:* "It was already a route. That nobody said it did work does not make it a later addition."

*Ruled on file 11: agrees.* A route, in the case's word, is a support. The two springs give **redundant supports**: each spring alone is a support, and neither is needed. The second spring was a support from the start, whether or not anything ever set it working. This is one of the two cases on which file 11 came closer to the fixed verdict than file 10 had; the other is "The forbidden wire" (section 12). Draft 4 rests it on firmer text: commitments are the pieces offered as doing the work "whether or not anyone has described their work".

**The cable that used to be a spring.** After the first spring is deleted, the author takes the piece of the explanation that stood for the surviving spring, says it now stands for a cable, shows that the door still returns, and reports that the spring explanation survived. *Fixed verdict:* what survived is a replacement, and "the replacement's success is its own". *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* A piece tied to a different anchor makes a different candidate, judged on its own.

A support **runs** in a system when actual events carry it out. Those events form a **route that ran**, and section 10 says why credit follows routes that ran, not the supports a candidate contains.

Two machines can give the same outputs, whatever goes in, and still differ in which supports run. The theory names parallel and priority wiring as its example, without details. In plain terms: in parallel wiring two circuits work at once, and in priority wiring one works while the other waits, and the two can give the same outputs. The theory's **proof that outputs do not settle accounts** shows what follows. When two systems give the same outputs, whatever goes in, but differ on a claim about an account, no judgement made from outputs alone can be right about both. The same holds for judging where a text came from by the text alone, or judging from delivery records alone whether something delivered was actually used.

That is why predicting is not explaining. A prediction looks only at outputs. An account is judged by its pieces and the supports that run, and two systems with the same outputs can differ there.

### Interference, spread-out support and idle commitments

**One more commitment.** Every earlier addition to a finite collection of commitments kept its support. The newest, that the room was locked, conflicts with a premise every line of it uses. (The case gives no more detail; picture, say, an explanation of how someone got into a room, every line of which uses the premise that they came through the door.) *Fixed verdict:* the conclusion no longer follows; "additions could not always be made without loss". *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

That is **interference**: adding a commitment breaks a support. The whole fails although a smaller group succeeds. The added commitment is not simply renamed "not a commitment" to get round the failure.

**Spread-out support** is the theory's mathematical example. Endlessly many commitments together fix a quantity, while no single commitment and no smallest group does the work. No case in the books is about it; section 17 gives the example.

None of the four requirements asks that each commitment do some work. Take a candidate that has at least one support. An **idle commitment** is one such that adding it to every support leaves a support, and removing it from every support leaves a support. The candidate is then an account with it exactly when it is one without it, and it is load-bearing in no support.

Both halves are needed.

- The removal half keeps out a redundant spring. Take the support whose only spring is the second one. Remove the second spring from it, and no support is left. So the second spring is not idle.
- The adding half keeps out interference. Add "the room was locked" to a support, and the support breaks. So that commitment is not idle either.
- The definition applies only to a candidate that has a support. In a candidate with no support at all, even a wrong commitment would pass both halves, since there would be no support to break.

With endlessly many commitments, idle ones can together be load-bearing, as in spread-out support.

*Departure:* Deutsch counts superfluous details as a defect of an explanation; here nothing marks a candidate down for carrying one.

### Back to Tomas

*Not yet ruled.* One outside reader (of two) named this reasoning as a move toward the fixed verdict on an earlier draft, and the project kept the text.

If the god sentence restricts nothing, because the spinning-top point already explains why the tilt stays steady, then it is idle and load-bearing in no support, and the fixed verdict follows. But the text leaves another view open. If the god is offered as a second piece that keeps the tilt steady, there are two possibilities. Either it matches its anchor, and then it is a redundant support that does some explaining, which goes against the verdict. Or it does not match, and then piece-by-piece matching fails, which puts "Tomas explains" at risk.

Which view applies turns on what Tomas offers the sentence as doing. On no view is his explanation without the sentence, or with a different god, a rival of it (section 6).

## 6. Rivals, problems and tests: what "easy to vary" means here

### The unused joint setting

Two wiring arrangements agree on every setting that has been tried, and on almost every untried one. They differ at one setting of two switches together, which nobody has used and anyone could make by hand. *Fixed verdict:* "They are two arrangements, and the record has not chosen between them. One reachable setting is enough." (The record here is what has been observed so far.)

### Your decision

On 24 September you spoke to two points. On the set of all the versions of an explanation that would still fit, you said: "That assumes that the set even matters or that the creative agent can even list them. As far as I'm aware, that's not possible, even in principle. A variation is a competitor. Whether anyone can list all variations that still fit is beside the point. If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem."

On whether the theory should grade explanations, you said: "A record is redundant. Once the explanation is rescued, the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in." The record there is a record of past failures, and of the changes made to save the explanation after each. On 25 September you asked that this be logged, so that you would not have to correct it again.

Draft 4 writes your position into three paragraphs: "Rivals" and "Problems", in this section, and "A failed answer stays failed", in section 7. Your "competitor" is a rival here.

### Rivals

Suppose someone offers one of the two wiring arrangements in place of the other (the case does not say so). Then they are rivals: at the joint setting they give different answers.

In general, two candidates for one question are **rivals** when one has been **offered** for the question in place of the other, and they **conflict** at some allowed setting of the target that both links carry over, inside the range or outside it. "Both links carry over" means that each candidate's link gives a matching setting of its own there, so both candidates say something about it.

Two candidates conflict at such a setting in either of two ways.

- Their answers there differ. At the joint setting, one arrangement lights the lamp and the other does not.
- Or their answers agree, but they disagree about how the target's pieces behave there. Each could match, and give the answer, under some possible behaviour of the target, but no such behaviour lets both. For instance, two explanations of a lamp might both say "lit" at some setting, while one says a certain wire carries current there and the other says it does not. Whatever the wire really does, at most one of them matches. The theory's wording: two of their commitments tied to one anchor behave differently there.

A candidate offered for a question claims all four requirements on the whole range, at every setting, tried or not. Outside the range it claims nothing on that question, though what it gives there can still conflict with what another candidate gives.

Not every pair of candidates is a pair of rivals.

- A candidate and a **rewriting** of it, renamed piece for piece with every connection kept and its answers left as they are, conflict nowhere.
- Nor do two candidates, however differently they split the target into pieces, when some possible behaviour of the target would let both match, and give the answer, at every allowed setting both links carry over. Examples are a fuller and a leaner account (Bea's and Maya's, section 7), a duplicate (the same piece stated twice), an idle addition (the sun god, if it is idle), and a blurred copy (a less detailed version of the same account).

**What holds the universe up.** One story says an eternal dog outside the universe holds it up; another says a turtle; a third, turtles all the way down. Nothing inside the universe can touch or see the holder. *Fixed verdict:* none explains; and though the dog and the turtle differ in words, nothing anyone could see or do depends on which is true: "the difference between them is idle".

*Not yet ruled.* Dog and turtle conflict at no allowed setting, so they are not rivals. Since nothing inside the universe can touch or see the holder, no allowed change gets a different answer from the one story than from the other, and no possible behaviour of the holder tells them apart. They look like rival stories, but their difference is in words only, which is the verdict's "idle". And since no allowed change alters the answer, no group of commitments has a difference to carry, so none of the stories meets the third requirement.

A candidate's rivals are among those someone has offered, and a candidate nobody has offered is no one's rival. No list of all rivals is supposed. As you said, as far as you know nobody can make one, even in principle.

Nor do the rivals anyone offers decide what the history of a selected link leaves open; that is fixed by its pool (section 8). This keeps a merely imagined alternative from reopening what the pool has settled, as in "The forbidden wire" (section 12).

### What is established, and what fits

**The worn key and the diary.** Sam makes keys. His present key diagram, a drawing of how to cut a key for a lock, agrees with a worn trial key, an early attempt, found in his drawer. To say when that trial key was made, Sam wrote a diary last week, working the dates out from the stages visible in the diagram. *Fixed verdict:* "The worn key is evidence on its own. The diary is the diagram talking about itself." *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

The theory's words for evidence, step by step, with the worn key:

- An **evidence item** is a pointer to an event, with what it is taken to show. The worn key points to a trial key once cut to that shape.
- A **receipt** is a chain of reasoning over evidence items. A receipt for a claim is a receipt against its denial. Where there is no receipt either way, the claim is simply without one: missing evidence stays missing, and nothing fills the gap.
- A record worked out from the claim it is meant to count for is no receipt for that claim. Sam's diary was worked out from the diagram, so it is no receipt for the diagram's history.
- An argument is **usable** by someone when they are entitled to it, it applies to the case in hand, and every **assumption** it needs is **still held**. Withdrawing an assumption removes the entitlement, without making the conclusion false.
- **Blame is shared.** A failed prediction counts against the claim being tried, together with the other assumptions behind the prediction and the instruments used, and against nothing narrower.
- A **judge** is whoever is judging a candidate: a person, a system, the project. A **result** is what looking or experiment shows about the target. It is **established** for a judge who holds a usable receipt for it.
- A candidate **fits** what is established for a judge when no result established for that judge shows it failing a requirement.

Both wiring arrangements fit: nothing established shows either failing.

### Problems

Two rivals that both fit what a judge has established pose, for that judge, a **problem** for the question: a conflict between ideas that what is established has not settled. There are two sorts.

In a **problem inside the range**, the rivals conflict at a setting the range covers. Whatever the target does there, at most one of them is an account, whether or not anyone can find out. Establishing what the target does there, by looking or by experiment, is a **test**. A test establishes the target's answer at that setting, and also, where the rivals' answers there agree, how the target's pieces behave. A test solves the problem whatever it shows, since afterwards at most one rival fits. An answer it refutes stays refuted on that question (section 7).

**Back to the joint setting.** *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* That ruling rested on a remark in file 11, that a claim that two candidates really differ must name a separating change. Draft 4 narrows that remark to candidates that differ only in which piece is tied to which anchor, so the verdict now rests on what survival leaves open (section 8), and on the view below.

*Not yet ruled.* The joint setting can be reached by hand, so it is possible, so it is in the range unless a stated limit leaves it out. If one arrangement is offered in place of the other (the case does not say so), they are rivals that conflict there, and both fit. Setting the switches together is a test that solves the problem either way.

In a **problem outside the range**, the rivals conflict only at allowed settings outside it. Their answers agree throughout the range, so no established answer there tells against one without telling against the other. A test inside the range can refute one without the other only for a failure peculiar to that one. And where both meet the requirements, both are accounts of the question.

### Easy to vary

A candidate is **easy to vary** when it and a rival pose a problem outside the range. The rival is then easy to vary too, and the words say nothing about which of them is right. A criticism that a candidate is easy to vary must supply such a rival: offering the rival is the criticism.

The theory defines only "easy to vary". It does not define "hard to vary", and it counts nothing. It does not say why it leaves "hard to vary" undefined. *This document's view* of one reason: "not easy to vary" would be true of any candidate nobody has challenged with a rival, however loose it is, so it would not mark a merit (section 14).

**A farmer's rule carried south.** A village rule on the northern shore of the Mediterranean says: "Sow the wheat after the first autumn rains, in October, and it ripens in early summer, in June." Nobody knows why it works. A family moves to a land far south of the equator, where the cool rains come in April and May. *Fixed verdict:* the rule tells the father little he can rely on. Taken by its months it misleads; taken by its seasons it points to April or May; and it cannot say which to trust. He needs to know why it worked, "or else trial" (trial here in the everyday sense: trying it and seeing, which in this document's words is a test).

*Not yet ruled.* "Sow in October" and "sow after the first rains" are two ways of taking the rule. Once either is offered in place of the other, as the father must do when he chooses, they are rivals. At home they agree at every setting, and both fit generations of harvests; they conflict only in the south. So they pose a problem outside the home range, and each is easy to vary relative to the other.

**Your example.** "Winter comes because Demeter grieves while Persephone is underground" against "winter comes because Persephone is underground", with no grief in it. On every season the Greeks knew, the two say the same. They are rivals only if the target allows a change that sets the warmth received on its own. In the world it does: a veil of dust dimming the sun would bring cold on the grief story, whose grief is the warmth received, and not on the other. In a model that allows no such change they conflict nowhere, and they are not rivals.

Two partial explanations, each exactly right about a different step of a mechanism and both true inside the range, can still be rivals outside it. Then the finer question's test (below) can show both wrong. The draft's papers give this only in general terms, without a worked story.

### A finer question

If two rivals are both accounts on a range, they conflict only outside it. A claim that one is right and the other wrong is then a claim that some allowed change outside the range separates them, and it must name that change.

A finer range that holds a change at which the two conflict makes a different question. If both are offered for that question, and both still fit, they pose a problem inside its range there, and a test can decide. On the original question nothing changes: both remain accounts there.

The farmer's finer question covers sowing anywhere, the south included. A season's sowing in the south is a test that decides it: the verdict's "or else trial".

Whether a candidate is an account of a question is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world. It is not fixed by when anyone first asks the question, nor by whether anyone has checked the candidate against it.

**The tilt and the midnight sun.** An ancient astronomer proposes that the Earth's axis is tilted, to explain why the summer sun stands higher than the winter sun. It follows, though nobody works it out for centuries, that far north the summer sun never sets. *Fixed verdict:* the idea already accounted for the midnight sun (the case's words); no one, she included, had explained it before the connection was worked out.

*Not yet ruled.* The first half is the sentence just given: the idea's standing was fixed by the idea, the question and the world. The second half needs a distinction the theory draws in section 9. An idea is an account of a question whether or not anyone uses it. Explaining, as something a system does, needs the idea to have been used on the question, and nobody had used it.

A range narrowed to leave out the settings where two rivals conflict also makes a different question. It solves nothing on the first question, where the problem stands until what is established leaves at most one rival fitting. Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates.

This links problems to section 10. A problem that a system pictures can be a noticed difficulty. For instance: a system has stated, as a safeguard, that it keeps every rival that fits, and one of its demands could be met only by dropping one of two rivals, which fits as well as the other.

### Same anchors, one account

The theory's **proof on same anchors**, narrowed in draft 4, says this. Two candidates that both match, and both give the answer, on a range give the same answers there. If in addition their commitments pair off, each pair tied to the same anchor through the same slots, then each pair is one kind, and, so far as matching and answering go, the two are one account.

Without the same anchors, nothing more follows. Candidates that split the target differently are different candidates with the same answers. A claim that one way of tying pieces to anchors is "really" the right one must name a separating change, which makes a finer range, a different question. A blurred copy, with less detail, is not a rewriting (section 8).

Files 10 and 11 said more: that any two such candidates are one account, piece paired with piece. A review in September found that false as written, and the outside readers agreed. Draft 4 corrects it. But the theory's list of ways it could be shown wrong includes any mathematical error in this proof. So that entry stands triggered for files 10 and 11, the older texts, and not for draft 4 (section 12).

### Departures

- Deutsch judges ease of variation before any rival is offered, and would reject a bad explanation without experiment. Here it shows only when a rival is offered.
- He holds that an explanation which could easily explain anything explains nothing. Here a candidate with such a rival is still an account if it meets the requirements, and a finer question is where a test can decide between the two.
- Here "easy to vary" is relative to the question, and applies to both rivals alike. On the question of the seasons the Greeks knew, the tilt of the Earth's axis, which he calls hard to vary, is easy to vary relative to the myth, when the two are offered in place of each other and the myth fits what is established. Whether the myth does fit is a point still open (section 14).
- Deutsch sets the Greek myth beside a Norse myth of the seasons about the god Freyr, and finds that they assert incompatible things, yet come down to one core explanation. Myths that differ only in their gods conflict at no allowed change, so here they are not rivals at all.
- His "reach", the power of an explanation to apply beyond the challenges it was made for, has no counterpart here: the word is not defined.

## 7. Narrowed questions, and why a failed answer stays failed

### Greta's dough

Greta's rule says her dough doubles in two hours. It fails in January, and she restates it for rooms above twenty degrees, saying why: the yeast works slowly in the cold. "On days other than Tuesdays" would come with no such why. *Fixed verdict:* a legitimate narrowing; the limit follows from a piece of her explanation.

### Limits

"For rooms above twenty degrees" is a limit: it says which possible changes Greta's range leaves out (cold rooms). A range is a stated selection from the possible changes, and a stated limit is what makes it one. An account at a stated limit answers the question at that limit. It does not answer a broader question that failed.

A **narrowing** is a limit added after a failure. It makes a different claim in a different **frame**. A frame is what a claim is relative to: its level of detail, its range, and, for a claim about a system's ability, the system's boundary and sameness rule (section 11). A frame is neither true nor false; it is chosen and stated.

What makes a limit right for the question belongs to the claim, and is open to criticism. The theory records the limit and gives no way of certifying it as right. Meeting the requirements on the narrowed range does not certify it as right either. (The fourth requirement does check two things: that the limit is stated, and that the range is not left empty.)

Where the claim states its reason for the limit, the theory's verdict on the limit is given with that reason. Where the claim states none, and a verdict turns on one, the reason is a **missing input**, and the verdict is **unsettled**. The theory never picks a reason to suit the verdict wanted.

**Back to Greta.** *Ruled on file 11: agrees*, though contested. Four of the five marks in the round were silent, one outside reader called the ruling wrong, and a fresh look at the texts upheld it, though not with full confidence (section 1 says how rulings are made). Greta states her reason, and it is tied to a piece of her explanation, the yeast; "not on Tuesdays" states none. Draft 4 now says outright that a verdict on a limit comes with the stated reason. Her narrowed claim is still a different question, and the January failure stands on the first one.

A limit stated from the start is no narrowing. In **Two bakers**, Maya and Bea both explain why dough rises: yeast gives off gas, which the stretchy dough traps. Maya adds limits of 18 to 35 degrees, found by experience; Bea gives the same limits and also says why. *Fixed verdict:* both explain; Bea also explains the limits.

*Not yet ruled.* Maya's limits are an honest stated range. Bea's candidate is also an account of the further question why the rising stops outside the limits. The two conflict nowhere, so they are not rivals. That would change only if Maya's explanation said something outside her limits that conflicted with Bea's there.

The fourth complaint says that if everything is relative to a range, nothing is objective. But the physics fixes what is possible, and within a stated range there is a fact about matching. A range that quietly leaves out changes to protect an account is caught by the fourth requirement, that every exclusion be stated, and then by any criticism that supplies the change left out. Objectivity lies in the physics and in matching. Honesty about limits lies in stating them openly. (That is a different thing from your "record", which was a record of past failures and of the changes made after them.)

### A failed answer stays failed

**The forecaster who keeps finding honest limits.** Bruno's rule for when the village stream floods fails in March, and he limits it to months outside the thaw. It fails after a dam release upstream, and he limits it to days without one. It fails again, and he limits it to days with a west wind. Each limit is described in ordinary terms anyone can check, each narrowed rule has so far been right inside its limits, and he records every failure openly. *Fixed verdict:* "Bruno has no explanation of the floods. Each limit is honest taken alone, and the series as a whole is a retreat."

Draft 4 adds a paragraph, the theory's form of your "the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in". Take Bruno's first rule, on the question "when does the stream flood?", whose range has a setting on that day in March. On that day the rule gave an answer that the stream did not give. Then any candidate that gives that same answer there is no account of that question. That holds whether it is the rule that failed, the same rule offered again, a rival, or a changed rule that keeps that answer on that day. It holds too on every question with the same target and the same thing asked whose range holds that day.

Once a judge has established what the stream did there, this is established for that judge of every such candidate alike, from the answer each gives there. No record is needed of who failed before, or of how anything was changed.

Because blame is shared (section 6), the finding can lapse. If an assumption behind the test that established it, about its background or its instruments, stops being held, then the exclusion lapses for all such candidates alike. None of them is thereby shown to be an account. What lapses is the establishing, not the fact.

A range that leaves the failed setting out, or a change in what is asked, makes a different question, and the failure on the first question stands. **A claim keeps its frame**: it stays the claim it was, even when later thinking changes which range is in use.

**The test that fitted neither.** A remote expert sends a robot two diagnoses to choose between, and the one test that would separate them. The robot runs the test, and the result fits neither. It notices that the test assumed a pressure sensor was accurate, swaps to a second sensor, runs the test again, and finds a third fault that neither diagnosis named. *Fixed verdict:* the achievement is mostly the robot's. *Ruled on file 11: silent*, on "mostly" (section 10). On the test itself, *not yet ruled*: once the sensor is in doubt, the exclusion of both diagnoses reopens for both alike, and the rerun establishes a further result.

**Back to Bruno.** *Ruled on file 11: silent. Not yet ruled on draft 4.* The first half of the verdict now follows. Each narrowed rule has a narrower range, so it is a different question, which does not answer when the stream floods, and the failures on that question stand. The second half does not follow. Nothing in the theory judges a series of narrowings. And Bruno's limits say where his rule applies, not why it fails outside them: the thaw and the dam release are where it failed, not a reason drawn from a piece of his explanation, as Greta's yeast is. So a verdict on his limits lacks a stated input.

So, on a fixed question, a correction sticks with no record of past narrowings, only a receipt for what the target did. That is how draft 4 renders your "the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in". It does not reach other settings: the same mistake at a neighbouring setting goes unnoticed until that setting is tried. Nor does it reach a series of narrowings, a change of level of detail, or a coarser thing asked. The project's analysis of 24 September, on whether correction sticks, found that a record still does one job: telling a retreat like Bruno's from a limit that was honest from the start. Draft 4 does not settle whether that job is needed.

### Fixed judging, moving questions

The tenth complaint says that holding the question fixed while judging, yet letting questions change, is having it both ways. It is, on purpose, and the two are kept apart. An assessment holds its range fixed. An episode of inquiry can replace a range with a constructed one, provided every change is recorded.

The theory's **proof that fixed judging and moving questions agree** shows these are consistent. "An account on the old range" and "not an account on the replacement range" are claims in different frames, and both can be true. **Moving the goalposts**, changing a claim's frame without saying so, is a failure of the record, not something the theory permits.

A case whose verdict turns on one of the stated inputs listed in section 12, or on the standard of worth, when the case does not state it, is a case with a missing input, not a refutation.

*Departure:* both source books hold that a limit of scope needs a further explanation. Here a limit is recorded, and not certified as right.

## 8. Where links come from: selection, pictures and surprise

### The discarded lamp controller

Each lamp controller has two switches, A and B. A testing machine keeps a controller if, with B left off, its lamp is dark with A off and lit with A on; it discards the rest. Only two designs exist, and both can be built. The machine never tries the setting with both switches on, and the two designs behave differently there. One design also fails a setting the machine does try: its lamp stays dark with A on. It was discarded, and the other kept. Ivo reasons that since the two designs differ at the untried setting, and the machine never looks there, passing cannot tell you what a kept controller does there; he points to the discarded design as proof.

*Fixed verdict (high confidence):* no. The discarded design failed at a setting the machine does try, so it says nothing about controllers that pass. Passing tells you which design you have, and that design does one definite thing with both switches on. Ivo would need two designs that both pass and still differ.

### Carriers, contents and two layers

Sam's key diagram on paper, and the same diagram turned into raised marks that can be read by touch, are two carriers of one content. A **carrier** is a physical thing or event, at a place and time, that bears content. A **content** is a set-up together with what it claims on its range.

Carriers are not the same because they carry the same words: two copies of one page are two carriers. And contents are not the same because they give the same outputs: the parallel and priority wiring of section 5 give the same outputs with different supports running.

A **recoding** writes a carrier's content in another code, with the key stated. It keeps the content when a reader using the key recovers every pairing of the original, such as which tooth of a key goes with which catch in the lock. In **Mirrored marks**, Sam's diagram is turned into raised marks by a reader who writes right to left, with the convention stated on the sheet. *Fixed verdict:* the content was kept, and anyone using the convention recovers every pairing. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

The theory describes a system as having at least two set-ups, called layers.

- In the **thing layer**, the slots are lasting things with edges and identity. The pieces are the **threads** that keep each thing the same thing from moment to moment, such as a ball's path as it rolls. The allowed changes include **moving** a thing, **hiding** it, and **telling which is which again**. It is the layer at which there are objects, rather than a blur of sensation.
- The **expectation layer** sits over it. Its pieces are dependences among things, and it makes predictions.

The two layers are a description the theory uses to say what it would be for a system to have objects and expectations. The theory does not claim that any real system has them.

To **picture** something is, roughly, for a carrier to stand for it by matching it, with a history of the right sort. The exact sense comes below, after the three histories.

### Three histories of a link

A link between set-ups of a physical system has exactly one of three histories, fixed by what physically happened.

- **Selected.** There is a **pool** of links to be selected from. There is **variation**, a physically possible process that makes fresh members of the pool. There are **trials**, the settings the link actually met while being shaped. And the surroundings decide what survives: a link **survives** when it is in the pool and matches on every trial. Nothing in the trials pictures the link, the trials, or what counts as survival. The pool belongs to the claim: it is the links that the physics and the **stated design** (how the members are built) allow, whether or not they were ever built.
- **Constructed.** The link is made in an episode of inquiry (section 10) that prepares it, as its construction evidence shows (section 9). In that episode the system pictures the link, or what the link leads to. Episodes of inquiry hold criticism, so a constructed link has both a pictured target and criticism in its history.
- **Declared.** Written into the model by its author. The theory allows this only as a convenience for building models, and no claim of creativity may rest on it.

Selected links are to be expected at the thing layer and constructed ones above it, though this is not required. Construction can work on selected material, and selection can go on beneath it. Neither history reduces to the other: a selected link has no pictured target and no criticism in its history, and a constructed one has both. Creativity lives in construction; selection supplies its raw material.

**Dark moths.** Where smoke has blackened the tree bark, moths become mostly dark-winged over generations, as birds eat more of the pale ones. *Fixed verdict:* the wing colour, inherited and put there by selection, tells the bark's colour, though no moth has any idea of it. *Not yet ruled:* this holds under draft 4's wording of selection.

Two complaints are answered here. To "'selected' is simply laid down, like 'is a cause'": selection is a physical history, and whether it happened can be checked, at least in principle; a kind-label claims nothing. To "you have replaced explanation with evolution": selection appears once, at the bottom, producing the thing layer that explanation works on, and every claim of creativity needs construction.

### Pictures

Take the dark moths again (this document's illustration). Their inherited wing colour is a carrier. At the level of detail of "dark or pale", the set-up it embodies (dark wings where the bark is dark) has a link to the content "the bark here is dark". That link matches on the content's range, blackened bark or clean, and its history is selection. So the wing colour pictures the bark's colour.

In general, a physical thing **embodies** a set-up when, described at some level of detail, it has that set-up. A carrier **pictures** a content, at a level of detail, when the set-up it embodies at that level has a link to the content that matches on the content's range, with a history of selection or construction. So what a carrier stands for is a fact about matching with a history, not something given.

- A system can picture a false explanation: the link from carrier to content matches, while the content's link to the world fails.
- A declared link makes no picture. It makes only a modeller's claim that there is one.
- A carrier keeps its history when access to it is lost. A later record worked out from it is no second, independent witness to that history (Sam's diary, section 6).
- Which set-up a physical carrier embodies is the one thing here taken from outside: the physics supplies it.

**The card nobody can read.** A forecast card's ink has faded, and the one person who could read its raised marks by touch has died. A copy made earlier gives its content. *Fixed verdict:* the marks still say what they said; the card's history is intact, and access to it is not. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

In **Half from the source, half from the shelf**, a copier fills one damaged patch of a diagram from an old stored diagram that happens to agree with the source. *Fixed verdict:* that patch's agreement is luck, and the record says which lines came from where. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* Whatever is filled into a carrier from another source keeps that source's history.

### Expectation, miss and surprise

**Two footbridges on opening day.** Rhea designed her footbridge from a worked-out theory of sway, calculated for crowds of the size that came. Dov copied an eighty-year-old village footbridge, not knowing it had never carried more than a few dozen people at once. Under large opening-day crowds both bridges sway badly. *Fixed verdict:* the swaying went against what both expected, but contradicted only what Rhea had reason to expect.

The theory's words for this. For a link to the expectation layer, at a setting that actually happens:

- the **expectation** is what that layer gives;
- a **miss** is the link failing to match there;
- a **surprise** is a miss by a selected link at an **untried setting**, one not among its trials.

With no link, or with trials that cover the whole range, there can be no surprise. A constructed link that fails has a miss, not a surprise. A miss that the system pictures can be a noticed difficulty (section 10).

*Not yet ruled.* Rhea's link was constructed, and its range covered that crowd, so she has a miss that contradicts her grounds. Dov's copy is taken as a link shaped by the village bridge's eighty years of use, which serve as its trials; that is why it counts as selected, not constructed. The large crowd was a setting outside those trials, so Dov has a surprise, contradicting nothing he had grounds for. The list of changes marks this half as watched: it depends on taking Dov's link as selected, and a reader might not.

A miss by a selected link can be met by **re-tuning**: adding the setting to the trials, and letting variation adjust the link within its pool. Any miss can be met by a **response by construction**: building a fresh set-up or link. Only the second can originate anything (section 9).

### What survival leaves open

The theory's **proof on what survival leaves open** says this. Suppose some other member of the pool also survived the trials, and behaves differently at an untried setting. Then the trials leave the selected link's value there **left open**. An untried setting alone does not show that such a survivor exists: it must be in the pool, and it must survive. Where there is none, the pool itself fixes the value.

So a selected link matches where it was tried and, wherever its pool allows an alternative, is unconstrained elsewhere. That is why the thing layer can be wrong, and why surprise can happen. The older blanket claim, that every untried value is open, is given up (section 12, "The forbidden wire").

**Back to the lamp controller.** *Not yet ruled.* The pool is the two designs, and the trials are the settings the machine tries. The discarded design failed a trial, so it did not survive. "Both on" is not left open: passing tells you which design you have. In the terms of section 6, the discarded design does not fit what is established, so no problem arises. Whether one could arise is a fact about the pool, not about which designs anyone points to.

This answers the third complaint, that selection makes truth a matter of survival. Survival is how a link got there; whether it matches at changes it never met is a fact about the link and the world. The theory's **proof on surprise** adds that a system can be surprised only if it holds a selected link whose trials fall short of the possible settings. Surprise is not a feeling added to the theory. Re-tuning and construction are the difference between learning and creating, and they are told apart by their evidence, not their outcomes.

If a programmer designed the pool, success at untried settings reflects the programmer's knowledge. The theory cannot yet say so (section 14).

### The hidden thing: the theory's worked example

A row of cells holds two things, each with a position and a speed. The allowed changes are: move a thing, set its speed, hide a cell behind a screen, and swap the two things. The senses show only which cells are filled, not which thing fills them.

An expectation layer was selected on trials of moves and speed changes, but never of hiding. It predicts filled cells from recently filled ones. Then a thing passes behind a screen and keeps moving. The layer expects nothing there, and misses when the thing comes out where its speed puts it: a surprise. Re-tuning fails if every member of the pool predicts from filled cells alone: no member survives the wider trials, so the fault is in how the whole pool is built, not in any one member.

Construction builds a fresh layer, with a **tracker** for each thing: a piece that carries the thing's position and speed through the time it is hidden, linked to that thing's thread. The fresh layer matches on the wider range, and by the proof that kinds add nothing, the trackers have the response pattern of things. Nobody declared them "objects"; they are objects because they respond as objects do. If the system built the layer in a stretch of events that it owns (section 11), with a real join of tracker to thread (section 9), and the layer was new to it, then it originated the layer. Section 10 returns to this example, as created knowledge.

Swapping the two things changes nothing the senses show. Take a range that allows each change for both things alike. Swap which thread each tracker is tied to, and you get a second link. Swapping the two things carries the first link's matching and answers over to the second link: that is the **proof on rewriting**, below. Then pair each tracker in the first link with its swapped partner in the second: each pair is tied to one thread, so they have the same anchors, and by the proof on same anchors (section 6), so far as matching and answering go, the two are one account. "Tracker one is really thing one" needs a change that tells them apart, and on this range there is none: at this level of detail, which thing is which is nothing more than its path.

The example shows that the theory's definitions can all hold at once. It is not a claim about any infant, animal or program.

**Nadia and the cards she has never seen.** Nadia learns a card game only by watching cards played. For weeks she predicts the next card from those just played, and is often surprised. Then she starts keeping a tally of cards "waiting somewhere", and her predictions improve sharply. *Fixed verdict:* "Nadia built something new: a part that stands for cards she has never observed. That is construction, and it is her own." *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* The tally is a response by construction. A first picture can be constructed from a challenge, without ever observing what it pictures. Whether her early misses were surprises in the theory's sense does not affect the verdict. "Her own" is taken up in section 11.

The theory's **proof on rewriting**: **rewriting** everything (carriers, behaviours, links, histories, ranges) by renaming piece for piece, with every connection kept, keeps accounts, originations, repairs and created knowledge. It does not cover **blurring** (describing with less detail), changed boundaries, or descriptions that no longer tell which event is which.

### Departures

- Deutsch calls criticism and experiment a sort of selection; here selection has no pictured target.
- For Deutsch a problem is a situation in which conflicting ideas are experienced, and it can arise with no observation. Here surprise is kept for selected links. A problem for a question, in section 6's sense, is narrower than his: two rivals that both fit what is established. A problem in his wider sense can be a noticed difficulty (section 10).

## 9. Building something new: understanding, construction and finding questions

### The apprentice and the third lock

On Monday an apprentice tries keys from a ring until one opens the lock. On Tuesday, at another lock, she feels where a blank catches and files it until it turns. On Wednesday, at a third lock, she picks a key from the ring at once, saying it looks like Tuesday's. *Fixed verdict:* "Monday is blind fitting. Tuesday is making something new. Wednesday is neither: she is reusing what Tuesday gave her."

### Understanding

**The phrasebook.** After a month abroad, Kofi can order the same dishes with his phrasebook shut. Asked for a sentence that was never in the book, he has nothing to say. *Fixed verdict:* "Kofi has kept a narrow skill by his own effort. He has yet to understand the language." *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

A system **understands** a content when three things hold. It holds a picture of the content. It uses that picture in its work on challenges. And it keeps that use as a **lasting** ability for a stated **use task**, the task by which a claim of understanding is judged. For Kofi, ordering those dishes is one use task, and he keeps it. Making sentences that were never in the book would be another, and he does not have it.

A system's **stock**, at a moment, is the contents it understands for some real use. A system may understand a false explanation. A narrow lasting use is just that: it shows neither a wider understanding, nor that the wider one can never come.

### Construction

Take the apprentice's Tuesday. She feels where the blank catches, forms a picture of what the lock needs, and files the blank to suit it. That stretch of events is hers, it prepares a pictured set-up (the shape the key must have) for use in explaining why a key turns, and it holds a real join, between her picture of the lock and the shape she files.

In general, **construction** is an actual **stretch** of events, with a start and a stated end, owned by the system (section 11). It prepares a pictured set-up for explaining with the content. It holds a real **join**, a connection the system makes, relevant to that use. And it is not merely a chain of passings-on that keep the content as it was.

**Construction evidence** names the processes, the carriers that came in, the joins made and the picture that came out. **Reconstruction**, working received content out afresh, is construction. **Relay**, passing content on without making any join, is not. A small join made inside received content is construction of that join, and the rest keeps its **inherited history**.

**The parrot in the lecture hall.** A parrot repeats a professor's sentences word for word, answers questions about them with another sentence or a squawk, and would have repeated recipes as readily. A student learns the ideas by working out what the parrot's sentences mean. *Fixed verdict:* the parrot has only the sounds; in the student's learning it was a carrier. *Not yet ruled:* the parrot relays, since its responses follow wording, not content; the student reconstructs.

**The one tooth.** A learner receives a full key diagram from Sam, finds a catch it never covered, files one tooth for it, and keeps the rest as given. *Fixed verdict:* the tooth is hers; the rest is relay. *Ruled on file 11: agrees*, on the sentence about small joins, which file 11 added.

### Unspoken pictures

**The order of adjectives.** Ngozi always says "a lovely little old green French knife", never another order, even in phrases she has never heard. She cannot state any rule, and nobody taught her one. *Fixed verdict:* she knows the rule tacitly, and formed her grasp of it herself.

The theory's illustrations are like this: a pianist imagines a passage without playing it; a geometer handles a spatial relation without naming every piece; an investigator notices an inconsistency before she can say what it rests on. An **unspoken picture** is not an absent one. Picturing, understanding and construction ask for no written form. They ask only whether the distinctions and moves are there, with matching and history, and are used where use is asked.

A picture can be partial, spread out, or drawn out over time. It can be kept in memory, imagery, rehearsed action, or a notebook. Keeping a criticized item in view can mean being able to bring back the distinction that matters, rather than storing every word.

So construction evidence may pick out a join by its use. With Ngozi, for instance:

- her responses keep what the join ties together: size comes before age, whatever the words;
- she treats the same content alike in another code: the order is the same whether she speaks or writes;
- a change in content brings the change the join calls for: an added word of colour takes its place after age and before origin;
- and the use lies on a route that ran: her grasp of the order actually produced her sentences.

That is how using an objection is judged too (section 10). But use alone does not construct. Received content used as it was received keeps its inherited history, and passing it on without such use is relay.

*Not yet ruled.* Ngozi was given examples, never the rule, and her use of it on phrases she has never heard picks out the join. One outside reader (of two) named this as a move toward the fixed verdict on an earlier draft; the other called the passage faulty, and the project kept the text.

### New, origination and finding questions

On Wednesday the apprentice picks a key that works like Tuesday's. Two contents are **equivalent**, at a level of detail, when links run both ways between them, each matching on the content's range. That is sameness of structure, not of wording or looks. A content is **new** to a system when nothing already in its stock is equivalent to it.

A system **originates** a content for a question when three things hold: it **used** the content **on the question**, the content was new to it, and it constructed the content. What is originated can be a set-up, a link, or a range with what it asks; each can be treated as a content.

**Back to the apprentice.** *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* Monday is selection: a pool (the ring), trials, and a surviving key, with nothing picturing the lock. Tuesday is construction, a real join made to her picture of what the lock needs, and the key is new to her, so she originates it. On Wednesday the key is equivalent to one in her stock, so she originates nothing.

**"The seal was tight."** An investigator's notes mention in passing that the seal was tight. Later she measures the seal's compression as something that varies, and finds it matters. *Fixed verdict:* she added a dimension (something that can vary: in this document's words, a slot), by measuring it, not by mentioning it. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* A matter mentioned in passing becomes a slot of the account only when the account allows changes to it, and adding it is construction.

**The rota.** A team argues for weeks about who is lazy, until Noor asks which tasks have no owner; the arguing stops, and the work gets done. Nobody had said that every task should have an owner. *Fixed verdict:* "Noor found a better question, and its merit is real, even though the need it met was only recognised afterwards."

Originating a range, with what it asks, is **finding a question**. Noor's range can itself be treated as a set-up. Its slots are the changes and circumstances it holds (which tasks, who does them). Its pieces settle which changes belong. And its own allowed changes add or remove a change, or alter what is asked, as Noor altered "who is lazy?" to "which tasks have no owner?". The theory's **proof that a range is a set-up** says this. So construction and newness apply to questions, and finding one can, where the rest holds, create knowledge (section 10).

Finding the right question is creative on the same footing as answering one, and a description of creativity that takes questions as given cannot say so. Part 0 of the theory puts it strongly: finding questions is "at least half of creativity". The theory measures nothing here; the phrase says how much weight it gives to finding questions.

*Ruled on file 11: silent*, on the merit only. If Noor constructed the range, it was new to her, and she used it, then she originated a question. Whether it was better is a claim of worth, which needs a standard of worth from outside, and the case supplies none. On draft 4 (*not yet ruled*), the last clause of the verdict, "the need it met was only recognised afterwards", is in line with the theory's episodes: the difficulty the team pictured was that the work was not getting done, and the demand her question met was stated only afterwards.

This gives the second half of "The tilt and the midnight sun" (section 6). Explaining, as something a system does, needs the idea used on the question, and nobody had used it.

That questions can be found is the fifth main claim. It would be defeated by a showing that treating a range as constructed, new and originated either cheapens creativity, or misses a real case of finding the right question.

*Departure:* Deutsch holds that grasping any idea, even one received from someone else, takes a guess, so that every acquisition is creative. Here working a received idea out afresh is construction, and merely passing it on is not. The likenesses to Deutsch on relay and on unspoken knowledge are named as likenesses, not claimed as borrowed.

## 10. Criticism, repair, credit and created knowledge

### Two plumbers

Ana inspects a leak, says the washer has cracked, and explains why that lets water past. Before she can replace it, Ben tightens a nearby joint at random, and the leak stops, because the tightening shifted the washer back into place. *Fixed verdict:* "Ben's act stopped the leak. Ana's explanation is correct and produced nothing. Nobody here made a repair that came from an explanation."

### Routes that ran

**The route that started and was stopped.** (Ana and Ben again, in a different story.) Ana's bypass and Ben's valve begin to move at the same moment. The bypass jams a quarter open; the valve closes, and the flow stops. The operating log records it all before any meter is read. *Fixed verdict:* Ben stopped it; Ana's route started and did no stopping; the log says so without the meter. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

The **course of events** is everything that actually happened, as events in an order that never loops back, with the physics saying which processes took place and which connections were really made. A **route that ran** is a connected cluster of actual events with three marks:

- it joins something the system pictures to an outcome;
- its pieces behaved as they should;
- along it, the outcome really depended on the pictured difference: had that difference been otherwise, among the alternatives the claim states, the outcome would have differed.

A route that ran is made of events. A support (section 5) is made of commitments, and it runs when events like these carry it out. A route that started and did no work, or had already stopped when the outcome came, did not run for that outcome. This is read from the course of events, not from the outcome.

In **The diagram in his hand**, Sam holds his old key diagram while cutting a blank, but takes every measurement from the lock itself. *Fixed verdict:* the diagram did no work; had it said something different, he would have followed the lock. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.* A change in the diagram would have changed nothing Sam did, so no route ran from it.

Credit for a repair follows the routes that ran, not the supports a candidate contains (below).

### Criticism

Three principles of the theory come first. An idea may be entertained before anything justifies it. A criticism is aimed at something, and is itself a **guess**, an idea put forward without proof. And a system may act on a judgement without first proving it.

**Two students, ten drafts each.** Jana hands each draft to her teacher, gets back a bare mark, and in the end hands in the best-marked draft. Kasia shows nobody; after each draft she writes down its faults, such as a claim with nothing to back it, and fixes them in the next. The final essays are about equally good. *Fixed verdict:* both created what is good in their essays; for Jana, the teacher chose which draft was handed in.

Take one of Kasia's notes, "this claim has nothing to back it" (this document's illustration). A **criticism** has four things:

- a **criticized item**: here, the claim in her draft (in general, a candidate, question, range, link or method);
- an **alleged fault**: that the claim is unsupported;
- **grounds**: she looked, and her draft gives no evidence for it;
- a **connection**, the argument from grounds to fault: no evidence is given, so the claim is unsupported.

A criticism **bears on** its item when its connection, taken as a candidate for the **fault question**, is an account. The fault question is: does the item have that fault, in respect of the original question (here, the essay's)? So a criticism is judged by the same four requirements as any candidate. A criticism can exist without bearing on anything.

A **warning sign** is something that tells against an item. It is not a criticism until something pictures it as grounds for an alleged fault.

*Not yet ruled.* Kasia's written faults are criticisms. Jana's bare marks allege no fault, so they are at most warning signs. And the teacher, who pictures what she wants as she chooses among Jana's drafts, is not a selection history in the theory's sense (section 8): selection has nothing in it that pictures its target. This keeps "the teacher chose" from being read as selection.

An **objection** is a guess that something is wrong. The theory uses the word for what a response in an episode responds to, and does not define it further. A response **uses an objection** when the objection shapes it in the right way and it lies on a route that ran. "In the right way" has three parts. With Kasia's note, for instance:

- each role in the objection keeps its role in the response: the claim criticized is the claim she fixes;
- the objection put in other words brings the same response: "no evidence for this" would lead to the same fix;
- a change in what the objection says brings the change the responder's way of deciding calls for: had the note said "this claim is false", she would have cut it rather than backed it.

Using an objection that is not valid does not make it valid.

### Episodes and noticed difficulties

**The novelist's two demands.** Odile wants two things from every chapter: rich, lyrical prose, and a story that keeps readers turning the pages. Planning chapter five, before writing it and before anyone has read it, she sees that a long description of a valley at dawn would stall the story at its tensest point. Her first draft cuts the description to one plain sentence. Her second has the heroine flee across the valley at dawn, described through what she sees as she runs: rich and fast at once. *Fixed verdict:* she had a problem as soon as she saw the clash, before anyone read the chapter; only the second draft solves it. ("Problem" is the case's word, in its everyday sense: in this document's words, a challenge.)

The theory speaks of an episode loosely, as a stretch of inquiry from a difficulty to a response, and defines only a complete one. A **complete episode of criticism** holds four things: a noticed difficulty; an item that was there before it was criticized; an objection; and a response that depends on what the objection says.

A **noticed difficulty** is one of two things, when the system pictures it: a failed demand; or a clash in which what the system holds meets one demand only by breaking a safeguard (demands and safeguards are explained under "Repair", below). A **creative episode** is an episode of criticism holding an origination connected to its inquiry. Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof.

*Not yet ruled.* Her two stated demands clash in the chapter before anyone reads it, which is a noticed difficulty. It needs no reader and no observation. The first draft meets one demand by breaking the other, so it is no repair. The second draft is a repair.

### Repair

In **Written on the job sheet**, the job sheet for a plumbing repair states in advance that the kitchen tap runs at all times during the work. During the work the tap stops for four seconds while the mains is switched over; nobody draws from it. *Fixed verdict:* the safeguard was broken, for four seconds, and calling it incidental changes nothing. *Ruled on file 11: agrees*, because the moments it covers were stated.

A **contribution** is a stretch of events, with the changes of content it makes. **Demands**, what is to be repaired, and **safeguards**, what must go on holding, are **stated inputs**: the theory records them without supplying them, and stating them claims nothing about their worth. Each is stated with the **occasions** it covers ("at all times during the work"), and a safeguard is lost exactly when it fails on one of them.

A contribution makes a **repair** when three things hold:

- some demand that failed before holds after;
- every safeguard that held before held on every occasion it covers in between, not only at the end;
- and the repair was **produced by** the contribution, meaning that a route that ran joins the two.

The theory ranks no repair above another. Losses outside the safeguards must be put on the record.

In **Four seconds**, the same stop happens, but the case names only "the protected tap". *Fixed verdict:* the safeguard held in every way that mattered. *Ruled on file 11: silent.* Whether the safeguard covers every moment, or only moments when someone draws water, is not stated, so the verdict turns on a missing input.

### Credit

**Two valves in one second.** Ana and Ben each close a different valve in the same second, and either alone would have stopped the leak. *Fixed verdict:* both did something that would have stopped it; neither can claim it alone; there is no way to say whose water stopped. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

**Credit** is which contribution an achievement is attributed to. The theory credits each contribution whose route ran to the achievement, as the course of events shows. Where two contributions that were each enough both ran, both are credited. It gives no **sharing of credit** that the course of events does not contain.

Three attributions stay apart: a correct account that produced nothing (Ana, among the plumbers); an act that repaired without an account (Ben); and a repair produced through the use of an account (nobody there). *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

Three cases have fixed verdicts that call an achievement "mostly" someone's.

- In **The expert's question**, a remote expert sends a robot two diagnoses to choose between, and the one test that would separate them; the robot runs it on its own evidence and picks the right one. The verdict: the diagnosis, as an achievement, is mostly the expert's.
- In **The test that fitted neither** (section 7), the verdict calls the achievement mostly the robot's.
- In **The same afternoon, three achievements**, the robot sees a faulty assumption in the test, the expert proposes the fix, and the robot carries it out and finds the fault. The verdict credits each of the three achievements separately, and calls the whole inquiry mostly the expert's.

*Ruled on file 11: silent on all three.* "Mostly" needs a **weighting of credit** beyond what the course of events contains, a stated input that none of them states.

In **Two hands on the test**, the robot saw the faulty assumption and the expert saw the fix. The fixed verdict, "Neither can say 'mostly'", is *ruled on file 11: agrees*: with no weighting stated, neither has grounds for it.

### Created knowledge

Return to the hidden thing (section 8). Suppose the system originated its new layer, with its trackers, and that the layer met the demand "hold, and be able to use, an account of how things reappear after hiding", while keeping the safeguard "predict moves correctly". Then it made a repair. And since the layer is an account on its range, and the system understands it, it created knowledge. Among the plumbers, by contrast, nobody created knowledge: Ana holds a correct account that produced nothing, and Ben repaired without one. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

A **demand for knowledge** is a demand that a correct account be understood, or that some way of using something be corrected through such an account. **Created knowledge** needs all of these:

1. a creative episode (above);
2. a contribution in it that makes a repair;
3. among the demands repaired, a demand for knowledge, which failed before and holds after;
4. a content that the system originated, for some question, by the end of the episode;
5. that content is an account of its question, on the range fixed when it was originated;
6. it is among **what the contribution prepared**;
7. the system understands it afterwards;
8. and its join lies on the route that ran to that repair, so that the repair was **produced through the account**.

A later narrowing of the range, made to save the account, is a different claim and does not count.

### Worth

Noor's question stopped the arguing, and the work got done (section 9). Whether it was a *better* question is another matter. Repairing a demand shows that it was repaired; it does not show that the demand, or the question behind it, was worth having.

Where a claim invokes **worth**, the theory takes the **standard of worth**, one of its two foundations, from outside, and marks the place. The eighth complaint asks where beauty and art come in. The theory keeps three things apart in the artistic case:

- a work's **effect**, what it does in a context;
- its **purpose**, what it aims at;
- and its **artistic value**, taken from the standard of worth, with its **reasons of taste**, whenever artistic value is claimed.

**Achieved purpose** is defined from the first two: a work achieves its purpose when it has some effect, and every effect falls within the aim. None of the three is defined as another. The theory does not pretend to know which reasons of taste are true, and no view of beauty follows from achieving a stated effect. Whether Odile's rich prose is beautiful, for instance, is a claim of artistic value, which needs the standard of worth.

*Departure:* Deutsch holds that there are objective truths about beauty; the theory abstains.

## 11. Owning an ability: boundaries, tasks, barriers and the universal class

### The technician's whisper

An assessment is stated at the start as robot-only. Midway, a technician tells the robot which gauge to read, and the robot completes the diagnosis; the log records the instruction. *Fixed verdict:* the boundary was robot-only, and the instruction crossed it; "The diagnosis is a team result." *Ruled on file 11: agrees*, as on file 10.

### Owning

A stretch of events is the system's **own** when its processes run inside the **boundary** stated for the system, which says which processes and resources are the system's, and within its stated **resource terms**, which say which resources count as the system's. Work supplied from outside the boundary, such as a diagnosis, a decisive question, or an instruction about what to read, stays an **outside contribution**, however it is carried out inside.

A process run inside the boundary is the system's own, whoever wrote it. Where the boundary is drawn decides, not where the process sits in the casing. Credit for content and owning a process are different things: a routine written outside and run inside is the system's process, and its content is still its writer's contribution.

Three more cases are *ruled on file 11: agrees*.

- In **The routine uploaded that morning**, a robot compares pressures using a routine a remote person uploaded that morning. The comparison is the robot's work; who wrote the routine is a separate fact.
- In **The module in the casing**, a bought module sits inside a controller's shell but outside its stated boundary. It is not the controller's.
- In **The robot's log and the maker's manual**, the log shows which routine, running inside the robot, produced today's diagnosis, and the manual says the routine is the robot's own "because the robot can diagnose". Owning rests on the log, which shows the routine running inside. It never rests on the ability being attributed, as the manual's sentence would have it.

An ability is attributed under a stated boundary and a stated **sameness rule**, what makes the system the same system through change. Both are stated before the attribution, not chosen after it. In **New board, old memory**, a robot's controller board is replaced mid-task, and its memory and routines, stated at the start as its sameness rule, are copied across. *Fixed verdict:* the same robot finished. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

Where the boundary is not stated outright, two sorts of statement are told apart.

- A statement that names the system and says whether a process runs inside it or outside it states the boundary for that process. "The robot compares the pressures itself" says the comparison runs in the robot.
- A statement that a process is the system's own, without saying where it runs, is the attribution itself, not the boundary. "The routine is the robot's own" is one.

Nadia's case (section 8) states no boundary outright. But "she starts keeping a tally" names her and places the process in her, which under draft 4 states the boundary for that process (*not yet ruled*). On file 11, the project's readers in the round had noted a doubt there, since the ruling read "her own" in the ordinary sense.

### Tasks, and what is possible

Take a kettle, as an everyday illustration. Boiling water is a task: a physical thing, the water, goes from a starting property, cold, to an end property, hot, using stated resources (electricity), with stated side effects (warm air). The kettle is the doer. Switching it on and waiting is the procedure. One boil is a carrying-out.

The theory states its physics in terms of tasks. (The sources note adds that this wording is from Deutsch's 2013 paper on "constructor theory"; the paper is not one of your two source books.)

- A **physical thing** has **properties**, each a set of its states.
- A **task** changes a physical thing from a stated starting property to a stated end property, with its resources and side effects stated.
- A task is **possible** when no law of physics limits, short of perfection, how well it can be done and how well the doer can be kept ready. This is how the physics decides what "possible" means for tasks: the word keeps its one meaning, allowed by the laws of physics. One success does not make a task possible.
- A **doer** performs a task by a stated **procedure**, and is left able to do it again. A **carrying-out** is one run of the procedure.
- In a **lasting performance**, every carrying-out finishes with a right end property and leaves the doer ready again. That holds from any start where the doer is ready, for anything within the task's starting property, and with the aids given. A kettle that never switches off is **getting stuck**, and getting stuck is not success.
- **Staying able** is the widest set of ready states from which every carrying-out finishes and brings the doer back into that same set. The theory's **proof on staying able** shows that a widest such set always exists.

An **ability** is an owned lasting performance, or an owned, possible, finite way of **making** one, under the same sameness rule and resource terms. A theorist's description of a procedure is not the system's having it.

A system is **able to advance** a challenge when, from each starting point in a set stated in advance, every carrying-out ends with the challenge advanced and the system still in shape to go on. A first discovery cannot be repeated: Nadia's first idea of hidden cards can happen only once. So what must last is the set-up that lets the system go on inquiring, not the discovery.

The physics also supplies **degrees of accuracy**, ever more demanding and short of perfect, for how exactly a task is done and how reliably the doer is kept ready. At each degree, what a system can do never exceeds what is possible at that degree. A task that a system can do at every degree is possible. But an ability at some degree does not make a task possible. For instance, a machine might cut to within a millimetre, while the physics forbids doing that cut better than some finer limit; then the task is not possible, though some system can do it at the coarser degree.

### Criticism at any depth

Kasia criticizes her draft. She could also criticize the way she criticizes, say her habit of looking only for missing evidence, and then criticize that criticism in turn. A principle of the theory: any aspect of a system's practice (a question, a range, a link, a method, what it attends to) can be criticized, with an outcome able to change how the system goes on.

An aspect is **reviewable** when some owned way of going on makes it pictured, criticized, and able to change how it is used. Ranges and links are among the reviewable aspects. A system has **criticism at any depth** when, for every finite chain of such items (a criticism of a criticism of a method, and so on), it has an owned way to go on.

### Barriers and the universal class

An **aid** is help a system may be given in doing a task. A **fair aid** is a legitimate aid that does not assume what is to be shown.

A **barrier** is a field of tasks, described independently, not by anyone's failures, for which every fair aid leaves the ability unavailable. A finite list of failures proves no barrier. And a narrow lasting use, like Kofi's phrasebook (section 9), shows no lasting inability to go further. A **way round** refutes a proposed barrier.

**Two sealed sorts of matter.** A universe has two sorts of matter that pass through each other without effect. Each sort's people have built a computer for any calculation their sort allows, and some calculations are possible only in one sort. Asha says the two computers together make a machine for any calculation the laws allow; Bram says none can exist, since a question written in one sort can never be read by the other's computer. *Fixed verdict:* Bram is right, and his reason largely explains why, together with the stated fact that each sort allows calculations the other cannot.

A principle of the theory is that any sort of matter may carry any set-up, and that every claim that a physical thing has a set-up must agree with the physics. Draft 4 adds a limit. This holds only so far as the physics lets contents pass between sorts of matter. Where two sorts cannot exchange what they carry, what only one sort can carry is a barrier for a system built of the other.

*Not yet ruled.* For a system of either sort, the calculations only the other sort allows are a barrier, so no system is in the universal class. Bram's reason explains this, together with the stated fact. Asha's weaker claim, that every calculation can be done somewhere, answers a different question, so she and Bram are not rivals.

The **universal class** is the systems, each with its boundary and sameness rule, that have three things. They have criticism at any depth. From their starting state, with some fair aid, they can understand every explanatory content, by doing its use task. And with some fair aid they can advance every **well-posed challenge**, one stated clearly enough for progress to be judged. The list of all explanatory contents and the list of all well-posed challenges are both fixed without reference to the system being judged.

Criticism at any depth does not bring membership. A growing history does not certify it, and a finite record does not establish it. The theory shows no person, machine, institution or line of descent to belong.

*Likenesses and departures:* draft 4's limit on what sorts of matter can carry is like Marletto's principle of exchange between sorts of matter, a likeness named, not claimed as borrowed. Deutsch holds that people are universal explainers; the theory abstains. Marletto defines knowledge as information that can keep itself embodied in physical things; the theory leaves that to the physics, outside its classes.

## 12. What the theory rests on, and how it could be refuted

### The uncited textbook

An explanation builds up the ideas it uses, step by step, from a starting base. It justifies every idea that way except one, which it justifies by a later theorem; but the proof of that theorem, as the explanation gives it, uses that same idea. A separate proof of the theorem exists in a textbook the explanation does not cite. *Fixed verdict:* as written, the order has a circle in it; the textbook proof would break it, and has not been used; the order is not settled until it is. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

### Foundations, frames and stated inputs

Recall "Four seconds" (section 10). Whether the safeguard was kept turns on which moments it covers, and the case does not say. The theory does not fill that in; it leaves the verdict unsettled. That is how the theory treats what a claim takes as given.

The theory has two **foundations**.

- **The physics.** It says what states and properties physical things have, which processes are possible, which processes a system controls (what counts as its deliberate action, used in construction evidence), what resources are, the degrees of accuracy, and which set-up a physical carrier embodies at a level of detail.
- **The standard of worth.** It is used only where a question invokes worth, is taken as given, and is never derived.

Every claim is relative to a frame (section 7), which is neither true nor false. A **stated input** is something a claim takes as given and states; the theory records it and does not supply it. The stated inputs are:

- a repair's demands and safeguards, with their occasions;
- a range's limit, and what makes the limit right;
- an attribution's boundary and sameness rule;
- a weighting of credit beyond what the course of events contains.

Boundary, sameness rule and limit belong at once to a claim's frame and to its stated inputs. A verdict that depends on a stated input, or on the standard of worth, is given with it. Where the stated input is missing, the verdict is unsettled, and the theory says so rather than choosing the stated input to suit the verdict wanted. No probability of truth, merit score or ranking of thinkers is supplied, and a claim that needs one is unsettled.

### Everything else is built from these

Roles come from allowed changes; kinds from response patterns; pictures from matching and history; histories from what physically happened; accounts from matching under change; and from these, everything else, up to criticism at any depth and the universal class. Nothing rests on a basic term meaning "really explains", "is a cause" or "is knowledge". The theory's **proof that nothing rests on "really explains"** follows each definition down to confirm this. The foundations are a theory of matter (the physics) and, where needed, a standard of worth; neither is about explanation.

The **building order** says which definitions rest on which.

- Frames and stated inputs rest on nothing in the theory; they are stated, not derived.
- Kinds rest on set-ups and ranges; accounts on matching; a criticism's bearing on accounts.
- Pictures rest on matching and physical history; understanding on pictures and lasting performance.
- Owning rests on the course of events and a boundary; an ability on owning, lasting performance and a sameness rule.
- Construction rests on owning and accounts; origination on understanding and construction.
- Repair rests on origination, accounts, understanding, routes that ran, and the stated demands and safeguards with their occasions; created knowledge on all of those and on repair.
- Criticism at any depth and the universal class rest on all of the above.

The order has **no circles**. A picture justified only by the construction that made it, or an owning and an ability justified only by each other, has no place in it. A separate proof counts only once the explanation uses it, as with the uncited textbook.

In **Owned means capable**, a routine is defined as the robot's own when it can run it, and runnable when it is its own. *Fixed verdict:* the definition goes in a circle, and only the histories of what happened give the distinction meaning. *Ruled on file 11: agrees.*

The theory's definitions leave places to be filled for any particular system: its set-ups, links, histories, boundary, and so on. A **filled-in model** is those places filled in for a real or imagined system. Its **classes** are these.

- The **base class**: every place filled with the right sort of thing, and every physical claim in agreement with the physics.
- The **creative-episode class**: with an origination connected to an episode of criticism.
- The **knowledge-creation class**: with created knowledge.
- The **criticism-at-any-depth class**.
- The **universal class**.

The creative-episode class lies inside the base class, and the knowledge-creation class inside the creative-episode class. The universal class lies inside the criticism-at-any-depth class. The others are not ordered. A single origination does not put its author in the universal class.

### How the theory could be refuted

The five **main claims**, in order of how much would fall, each with what would **defeat** it. None is protected by the theory's notation, or by the label of any draft.

1. **The four requirements are enough.** Defeated by a candidate that meets all four on a range of possible changes, with a link that is not merely declared, and plainly explains nothing. The known tricks each fail one requirement (section 3). A full response table passes matching; like any other attempt, it would defeat this claim only if it met all four and still explained nothing. An outside reader tried to build such a candidate, and offered none (section 14 notes one boundary case).
2. **They are needed.** Defeated by a real explanation whose set-up no link can match on any range of possible changes. Explanations of absence are the most exposed place.
3. **Kinds can be dropped.** Defeated by a label that tells apart two accounts which no allowed change tells apart, where the difference does real explaining.
4. **The two histories, selection and construction, are kept apart.** (The third history, declaration, supports no claim of creativity.) Defeated by any of three things. A selected link fixed at an untried setting by its trials, although its pool holds a survivor that differs there. (A pool with no such survivor is a limit the claim itself states, not a refutation.) A showing that every construction can be rewritten as a selection without loss. Or a showing that explanation does not work on the thing layer.
5. **Questions can be found.** Defeated by a showing that treating a range as constructed, new and originated cheapens creativity, or misses a real case of finding the right question.

A mathematical error also counts. That means a counterexample, under their stated assumptions, to one of these: the proof on smallest supports (section 5), the proof on pinning down (section 2), the proof on blocking (section 2), the proof on error build-up (section 4), the proof on staying able (section 11), the proof that kinds add nothing (section 4), the proof on same anchors (section 6), or the proof on what survival leaves open (section 8).

A case whose verdict turns on one of the stated inputs listed above, or on the standard of worth, when the case does not state it, has a missing input, and is not a refutation.

### The record so far

**The forbidden wire.** The only arrangement that would respond differently at the untried setting needs a wire between two terminals, and every device in the stated pool is built without it. *Fixed verdict:* "Then there is no alternative in that population. The different response is imaginable and is not available." *Ruled: file 10 disagrees; file 11 agrees.*

File 10's proof on what survival leaves open said that trials always leave every untried setting open. This case refuted that, and the list of defeats counted it. File 11 corrected the proof by requiring a differing survivor in the pool, and draft 4 keeps that, adding that rivals are not a pool, so a merely imagined arrangement reopens nothing. The cost: the corrected claim now holds almost by its wording, so it says less and is hard to defeat. The theory does not say how much that matters.

Three more defects, found in September, are corrected in draft 4.

- The old proof on same anchors was false as written, so the entry for a mathematical error stands triggered for files 10 and 11 (section 6).
- The proof on error build-up lacked assumptions it needs (section 4).
- "No assumed answer" left open which pieces are commitments (section 3).

On the cases, file 11 agreed with 46 of the 52 fixed verdicts, was silent on 6, and went against none.

### Three older views

The fifth complaint says the theory is three older views renamed. The first holds that what a sign stands for comes from a history of selection. The second, that science captures the structure of the world. The third, that a mind is defined by what it does, not by what it is made of.

The theory takes from each and differs from each. Links have a history, but selected and constructed histories are kept apart, with creativity only in construction. Structure is kept, but piece by piece and under change, and the theory does not require an explanation to copy the world's whole structure at once. Any sort of matter may carry a set-up, but every claim that a physical thing has one must agree with the physics. Whether the mix is new is a question for scholars.

## 13. What draft 4 changed

Draft 4 is file 11 with 57 changes. The main ones are gathered here by section, each said in terms a newcomer can follow.

**Questions (section 2).** Only one change touches the theory's text on questions, and it concerns limits (section 7 below).

**The four requirements (section 3).**
- It now says which pieces are the commitments: those offered as doing the work, whether or not anyone has described their work. The rest, including any piece that gives an input slot its value, is background.
- The second half of the third requirement changed. File 11 asked the range to hold a change that itself removes or replaces some commitments. Draft 4 asks instead that deleting a group of commitments lose a difference between two settings of the range. File 11's wording also left open which pieces were commitments, and, on one way of taking it, found no account where the fixed verdict finds one, as in the salt on the step.
- Whether a statement is the answer written in is judged by its form at the stated level of detail, not by treating all mathematically equivalent truths as one.
- File 11 said outright that a full response table is an account. Draft 4 says it passes matching, and is an account only if it also meets the other three requirements.
- On absence, it adds that why the absent thing seems present is a separate question, and that a bare denial is no account of it.
- The principle on errors now says that "explanation" there means an account, that a false explanation offered for a question is a candidate, and that whether an account is easy to vary is a separate matter.

**Kinds (section 4).** It says how a piece of one candidate is compared with a piece of another. It says that response patterns are built from behaviour, not from values, and that a change that sets a slot replaces only the piece that fixes it. The proof on error build-up now states the assumptions it needs; file 11 left them out, a drafting slip.

**Which pieces do the work (section 5).** File 11 compared which changed forms of an account still pass as more "explanatory jobs" are demanded of it (the jobs were never defined), and used the word "reach" without defining it. Draft 4 drops the comparison and the word, so nothing counts, grades or ranks candidates. It adds the idle commitment, with its definition applied only to a candidate that has a support. It says that a cut-down candidate's commitments are the ones it keeps, and that the supports judged are groups of the written commitments. And it separates a support from a route that ran, crediting by routes that ran.

**Rivals and problems (section 6).** The two paragraphs "Rivals" and "Problems" replace file 11's paragraph on hard to vary (the comparison just described). The proof on same anchors is narrowed: it now needs the same anchors.

**Narrowed questions (section 7).** Meeting the requirements on a narrowed range does not certify the limit as right. A verdict on a limit comes with the reason the claim states for it, and where none is stated, the verdict lacks a stated input. The paragraph "A failed answer stays failed" is new. And the theory's list of defeats now says that a case with a missing stated input is no refutation.

**Where links come from (section 8).**
- Surviving the trials is now defined: being in the pool and matching on every trial.

## 17. The mathematics, for readers who want it

**Pinning down, for sums (section 2).** Suppose each reading, and the feature asked about, is a sum of the unknowns with fixed weights. Then the feature is pinned down exactly when every shift of the unknowns that leaves all the readings unchanged also leaves the feature unchanged. For the two balances, the shift "one gram more mass, one gram less error on each balance" leaves both readings unchanged and changes the mass, so the mass is not pinned down. The same shift leaves the difference of the two errors unchanged, and every shift that leaves both readings alone is a multiple of it, so that difference is pinned down. Repeating a weighing adds a reading with the same weights, so it rules out no shift. A calibration, against something already known, can rule one out.

**Odd-sized tables (section 3).** Take square tables of numbers with an odd number of rows, where each number is the negative of its mirror image across the diagonal. The question is why none of them can be undone. The range drops the mirroring, drops the oddness, or drops both, holding ordinary arithmetic fixed, and holding fixed the tie between being undone and the table's **determinant**, one number worked out from the table that is nought exactly when the table cannot be undone.

- The full working-out of the determinant, with the mirroring put in, matches: each step is a smaller working-out whose hidden slots are set aside.
- It gives the answer: in the family of tables asked about, none can be undone, and under each dropped assumption the family holds one that can.
- It assumes none. Without the mirroring, the three-row table with ones down the diagonal and noughts elsewhere can be undone. Without the oddness, the two-row mirrored table with a one above the diagonal and a minus one below it can be undone.
- It does not dodge: a mirrored table of odd size that is not all noughts exists, so the untouched setting has a consistent state.

So it is an account. A three-line proof is shorter, but shortness is not a fifth requirement.

**Error build-up (section 4).** Choose a distance between states. Suppose that at every state within stated bounds, one step of the candidate lands at most a step error away from where one step of the target lands, carried over. And suppose one step of the candidate never enlarges the distance between two states more than a fixed number of times over, the widening factor.

Then, from one shared start, as long as the target's states stay within the bounds, the gap after a number of steps is at most this: the step error once for the latest step, plus the step error times the widening factor for the step before, plus the step error times the widening factor times the widening factor for the one before that, and so on back to the first step. With a widening factor of one, the ceiling is simply the step error times the number of steps. Without any widening factor, no ceiling follows.

**Smallest supports (section 5).** Suppose three things: the commitments are finitely many; adding a commitment to a support always leaves a support; and the whole candidate is a support. Then a single commitment is load-bearing in some support exactly when it belongs to some smallest support, and it is needed in every support exactly when it belongs to every smallest support. Where adding can break a support (interference), the second assumption fails, and the rule does not apply.

**The tipping edge (section 5).** Take a stated family of changes to a candidate. The tipping edge is the set of pairs of changed candidates of which one is an account and the other is not. The theory defines it and uses it nowhere else.

**Spread-out support (section 5).** Take endlessly many commitments: the first says a quantity lies within one of nought, the next within a half of nought, the next within a third, and so on. Any endless selection of them puts the quantity at exactly nought; no finite selection does. So no single commitment and no smallest group does the work; the whole group does, together. For the same reason, with endlessly many commitments, commitments that are each idle can together be load-bearing.

**Staying able (section 11).** Call a set of the doer's states "safe" when every carrying-out from a state in it finishes and returns the doer to a state in it. Put all the safe sets together, and the result is again safe. So there is always a widest safe set, and that is staying able.
