## 1. The world as a set-up, and questions about it

### Example: the fever and the thermometer

The thermometer reads thirty-nine, and Hana says the child has a fever. Later she says the reading makes the child shiver. Warm the thermometer in a hand: the reading climbs; the shivering does not change. Cool the child in a bath: the shivering eases.

The **fixed verdict** (a careful person's, written in advance and never changed): the first statement is a good reason to believe there is a fever; the second mistakes the instrument for the illness. The **ruling**, the project's settled view of what file 11 (the text in force) says here, is that the theory agrees.

### The idea in plain words

For the theory, the world and each explanation of it are **set-ups**: pieces that depend on one another, and changes made to them. An explanation, shaped to a question, is tied to its target by a **link** (which state and change go with which) that is judged only by what happens when things are changed, never by whether a piece "is the same kind of thing" as a piece of the world (section 3).

### What the theory says

#### A set-up

A pole stands in the sun. Its height, the sun's height in the sky and the shadow's length are **slots**, places that each hold a **value**, here a number. The sunlight is a **piece**, fixing the shadow's length from the other two. The **circumstances**, such as flat ground, are held fixed while changes are made. Raising the pole is a **change**.

A set-up has slots, each with the values it can hold; pieces, each tying some slots together; circumstances; and **allowed changes**, those it counts as ones that can be made. Under each change and circumstances, each piece has a **behaviour**: the combinations of values it allows among its slots. A **state** gives every slot a value; the **consistent states** are those all the pieces allow at once.

A **removed piece** restricts its slots not at all. A change that **sets** a slot directly (a board laid so the shade ends at a chosen mark) replaces the piece that fixed it, leaving no second, clashing piece. A changed **status rule** (one saying what status something has, such as "adult at eighteen") is a changed piece. Values may also be paths, curves, proofs or sequences of events; pieces may form loops; several consistent states stay several.

To one of eleven expected **complaints**, that mathematics has nothing to change, the theory replies: drop the starting assumption that a surface is flat, and a triangle's angles stop adding up to two right angles. Removing an assumption, dropping a property or changing the number of dimensions all count as changes, and a proof explains, for a question, when its pieces respond to them as the mathematics does.

#### Roles and direction

An **input** is a slot some allowed change sets directly: the pole's height. An **output** is one a piece fixes from its other slots, in all circumstances: the shadow's length. A **reading** reports another slot, and some allowed change alters the reporting without altering what is reported: warm the thermometer, and the child is unchanged.

**Direction**, which way dependence runs, follows too. Raise the pole and the shadow lengthens; set the shadow's length directly and the pole stays as it was. So dependence runs from pole to shadow, however the equation is written; the words "pole" and "shadow" settle nothing. On a range that holds raising the pole, the forward set-up **matches** (behaves as the world does at every setting). Working the pole's height out from the shadow, offered as what produces it, is a **calculation run backwards**, and does not: the world's shadow lengthens; the calculation's stays. It does match on a range of changes to the shadow as seen, a different question.

#### A question

"Has she a fever?" is about the child, its **target**. A **question** is made up of its target; its **range**, the **settings** it covers (a setting is one allowed change, in given circumstances), always including the **untouched setting**, no change in the ordinary circumstances; **what is asked**, the exact thing read off the target; the **demands** it serves and the **safeguards** it keeps; and the **history** of its range. The **answer** is what the target gives for what is asked, setting by setting.

#### Sorts of question

"Has she a fever?" is a pinning-down question: its range holds changes to the reading, which the reading answers. "What makes her shiver?" is a producing question: its range holds changes to what comes before the shivering, where the reading does nothing. A reading that pins down an outcome, even with a reliable forecast, does not answer whether the thing read produces it. The sort is fixed by what is asked and what the range holds, never by a label:

- a **producing question** is about an output, over changes to what comes before it;
- a **pinning-down question** asks which states give a reading (its **look-alike states**), over changes to the reading;
- a **blocking question** asks whether one state is **reachable** from another by some chain of allowed steps;
- a **status question** asks what status something has under a status rule;
- a **purpose question** asks whether a purpose is achieved.

The same target with a different range or what-is-asked makes a **different question**; an answer to one is no answer to the other.

#### Pinning down

One object is weighed on balances A and B, each showing the mass plus that balance's unknown error. A gram more mass and a gram less error on each would leave both readings unchanged, so the readings settle only the errors' difference, not the mass. For the theory this is an **account** of a limitation, an explanation passing its four requirements (section 2).

A reading **pins down** a feature when some state gives that reading and all its look-alike states agree on the feature; it does so at every reading that can occur exactly when the feature can be worked out from the reading alone. Where readings and feature are sums of the unknowns times fixed numbers, the feature is pinned down exactly when every shift of the unknowns that leaves the readings alone leaves it alone too. So repeating a weighing adds nothing; a **calibration**, a reading against something already known, can. "Take B's error as zero, since that gives the mass I favour" is not drawn from the readings: it argues in a circle, though the mass might be right.

#### Blocking

Three people are to share twenty-three whole tokens equally, passing them about but never cutting them. Every handing-over keeps the total at twenty-three, and three equal whole shares make a multiple of three. So no chain of allowed steps leads to equal shares.

An **unchanging quantity** is one that no allowed step alters; no chain of allowed steps joins states where it differs. Equal values of it do not show that a state is reachable. Allowing cutting makes different states; it does not refute the claim, made for whole tokens.

#### The machine that runs forever

Why is there no wheel that drives a water pump forever with nothing going in? Pia: hundreds of designs have all stopped; perhaps one will work. Quentin: the energy anything gives out must come from energy put in or used up, for any design, tried or not. Fixed verdict: only Quentin's answer explains.

A **proposed case**, used in no round: what follows is this section's view of the text, not a ruling. Quentin answers the blocking question with an unchanging quantity, the total energy: for every design at once, no chain of steps leads to water pumped with nothing used up. Pia's list, like a **table of observed answers** (section 2), has no piece that responds to any change, and a finite list of failures does not prove a "cannot" (section 10). So the theory gives the verdict, separating "cannot" from "not yet" as Chiara Marletto's book, a **source book** of the theory, does.

#### Histories of a range

Ranges have three histories:

- **declared**: written in by the **modeller** (whoever writes the model down, as a textbook lists "raise the pole; move the sun"), and nothing more;
- **selected**: the survivor when a **pool** of ranges was varied and weeded out (section 7);
- **constructed**: made in an **episode** of inquiry, with **construction evidence**, a record of how (section 8).

Any **assessment** (one act of judging an explanation on a question) may use any, but a claim that an episode **found a question** needs a constructed range, with the evidence.

#### A faulty question

"Why did the king of France go bald?" misses its target: France has no king. A **faulty question** may miss its target, assume an untouched setting that cannot be, or ask for things that clash. Asking it is still an **event**: it happened. Exposing the fault is a different question. Changing the range or what is asked during an assessment, without recording that the claim has changed, is forbidden. Giving a replacement question a sensible meaning can make a different, coherent question; it does not answer the first.

### What is open

- The theory gives no way to choose a question's **level of detail** (how finely its set-up is described): it can say an answer belongs to a different question, but not which level of detail is right.
- Nor does it say how to tell a question is faulty: exposing a fault is a different question.
