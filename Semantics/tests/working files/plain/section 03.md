## 3. Kinds are how pieces respond

### Example: the float that does two jobs

A float in a fuel tank turns a dashboard dial and, when low enough, opens a valve to the reserve tank. Milan says the falling float opens the valve; Lena says the dial showing "low" does. Bend the needle to read "full": the valve still opens. Hold the float up with a wire: the dial reads "full", and the valve stays shut.

The **fixed verdict**, written in advance: Milan is right; the float is read by the dial and also does the work. The **ruling** on file 11, the project's settled view of the theory, agrees.

### The idea in plain words

Two changes, not labels, showed which piece does the work. For the theory a kind is just how a piece responds to the changes a question allows; it never asks whether a piece is "the same kind of thing" as the world's.

### What the theory says

#### Response patterns and kinds

A piece's **behaviour** is the combinations of values it allows among its **slots**, places holding values. Gathered over every **setting** a question's **range** covers (each one allowed change, in given circumstances), it is the piece's **response pattern**. Two pieces are one **kind** on the range when their slots can be paired one to one so that their patterns agree.

So kinds depend on the range: fewer changes lump more pieces together, and pieces of one kind may come apart on a **finer range**, with more changes. At any **level of detail** (how finely things are described), pieces no allowed change tells apart are one kind, however labelled.

Is a thing then whatever you can do to it, as one of the theory's eleven expected **complaints** says? No: a **content**, a set-up with what it claims, still holds all its pieces, even two of one kind at this level of detail. Every claim about kinds is tied to its level of detail.

#### Two thermostats

Two thermostats of one make are set to eighteen and twenty-two degrees. Rosa says they are one kind in two states; Emil, two kinds, keeping a warm room and a cool one. The fixed verdict: Rosa is right about what the devices are; Emil's difference matters only when the question is why this room is warm.

Patterns are built from behaviour, not from the **values** slots hold. Eighteen and twenty-two are values of one slot, the set temperature, and both thermostats do the same with any value: one kind, whatever the difference is called. Turning the knob **sets** that slot, replacing only the piece that fixed it, not the thermostat that uses it; and a change under which two pieces behave alike tells nothing apart. Emil's difference belongs to a **different question**.

The ruling on file 11 found agreement, though several readers in the **round** (a complete trial of the theory on the cases) found it silent. Draft 4 now says this outright, untried in any round.

#### Pieces of two explanations

Pieces of two **candidates** (explanations offered for one question) are compared the same way, each taken through its candidate's **link**, tying it to the world; section 5 uses this. So is a **commitment** (a piece offered as doing the work) with its **anchor** (the world's pieces it is tied to), setting aside slots the commitment does not mention and pairing the rest by the **slot pairing**.

#### Cause, measurement and status rule

Ordinary speech's cause, measurement and status rule are families of response patterns, adding nothing:

- a **cause**'s pattern changes when its output is set directly or the piece is replaced, and not when a **reading** of it (a slot reporting it) is altered;
- a **measurement**'s pattern stays the same when the measured slot is set directly, and changes when the measuring is altered;
- a **status rule**'s pattern stays the same under changes to the world ("adult at eighteen" is untouched when someone ages), and changes when the status rule is changed. It answers "which status, under this rule?"; whether it governs a practice, or should, are different questions.

The theory asks what a piece's pattern is, not whether it "is" a cause. A piece reporting another has a measurement's pattern: change only the report, and the other stays; change the other, and the report follows. So with the dial, while the float has a cause's pattern for the valve. That, not wording, settles which piece an explanation offers as opening the valve. Unchanged in draft 4.

This answers two more complaints. Without kinds named in advance, one can still tell a cause from things that merely go together, and only so: these have no piece that responds when the supposed input is changed. And a status rule is indeed not a cause, but the difference is one of pattern, not there before, or apart from, the allowed changes.

#### No requirement about kinds

An **anchoring requirement**, that each piece be tied to a piece of the world "of the same kind", would be redundant or empty. If the range holds a change telling the two kinds apart, a piece tied to the wrong one already fails **piece-by-piece matching** (each commitment behaving like its anchor at every setting). If it holds none, they are one kind there; there is no third case.

The theory proves that piece-by-piece matching gives each commitment its anchor's pattern: setting by setting, that is what it says. So "kind" can be dropped from what makes an **account** (a candidate meeting section 2's four requirements), losing no case.

#### Matching over many steps

If each step of a walk carries over to a street plan, the whole walk does. By **matching step by step**, if each basic allowed change gives the same outcome whether made in the **target** (the world's set-up) and then carried over, or carried over and then made in the candidate, so does every chain of them, while each step stays where the link applies.

A link may pair states loosely, several with one. **Two-way matching**, in which each side, staying paired, can always follow the other's moves, is what treating two set-ups as equivalent needs; **one-way matching**, the candidate following the target, suffices for a simplification. Chained links keep both directions where each covers what the next needs.

A string a millimetre short, laid end to end a hundred times, can be a hundred millimetres out. On **error build-up**, the theory proves this. Start both from one state. Suppose that from any state within stated bounds, one step of each leaves the candidate at most a given **step error** from the target, carried over; and that one candidate step never stretches the **distance** between two states by more than a fixed **stretch factor**. Then, while the target stays within those bounds, the gap after many steps is at most the step errors added up, each stretched once per later step. Without a stretch factor no such ceiling follows, and an exact question is never quietly swapped for an approximate one.

#### What would defeat this

That kinds can be dropped is the third of the theory's five **main claims**. A case where a label tells two accounts apart that no allowed change does, and the difference does real explaining, would **defeat** it, refute the proof above, and make "this piece corresponds to that one" a **foundation**, given rather than checked.

### What changed in this revision

- Draft 4 says how pieces of two candidates are compared.
- Patterns are built from behaviour, not values, and setting a slot replaces only the piece that fixes it; two readers in the round had erred on the thermostats.
- Error build-up states the assumptions its proof needs and which gap it measures. Taken loosely, file 11's wording was false, as a review found in September and the **outside readers** (two AI systems that cross-examine the project) confirmed.

### What is open

- No round has aimed a case at dropping kinds; an outside reader tried and failed to break the claim.
- Whether a slot pairing may convert values, say Celsius into Fahrenheit, is not said. This touches **recoding**, rewriting in another code (section 7).
- The theory says every piece of a candidate gets its anchor's pattern; the proof covers only commitments (section 2, on "active").
