*Log S95, under decision S23 (the owner, 25 September 2026). Draft 5 of revision 2 (`tests/Revision 2 - file 13 draft 5, theory text.md`, md5 7f1d8ad02adf96e27622593bd263252e) is read here and not edited; line numbers are draft 5's. The owner's instruction (decision S23, verbatim in part): "Get rid of all words that imply verificationism and see if the semantics still holds." "Anything that is accepted is always tentatively, and mean anywhere that "accept" or "accepted" is used." "Also, argument is short hand for: reasons why this and not that. Not reasons for this and not that."

This file proposes the words. Whether the semantics still holds, in the owner's words, once they are in is the next step; section 4 gives one paragraph of Part VI in the new words as a first look, and section 5 names the places where no replacement could be seen that keeps the theory's claim.

---

## 1. Three rules the whole vocabulary follows

**Only ruling out.** Wherever draft 5 says that something counts for a claim, the new text says that an argument rules out the claim's denial or its rival, for a named assessor, while that argument stays usable (K2). Nothing in the theory is ever said to be *for* a claim. A candidate that is not ruled out gains nothing from that: it is only not ruled out. This is the owner's sense of argument: a structure strung together to decide why this and not that.

**Accept is always tentative.** "Accept" appears only as "tentatively accept", everywhere, with one technical use: an assessor *tentatively accepts* a result when the assessor holds a usable argument that rules out the result's denial (Part IX). The one ordinary use in draft 5 (l. 67, "whether anyone accepts it") becomes "whether anyone tentatively accepts it".

**Meet, and hold.** Whether a condition is satisfied is said with one word, "meets" / "is met", which draft 5 already uses for (E) ("a candidate meets (E)"), and always at an index: a contract, grain, boundary or history. "Hold" stays only for possession: an assessor holds an argument, a system holds a transport. "(E) holds", "(G) holds", "the same holds for" become "(E) is met", "(G) is met", "the same is met by".

## 3. How the named things are to be said

### 3.1 The proofs and Derivations 1–10

Part XVI becomes "Arguments", and each item "Argument n": a **Claim**, then *Why not the denial*, then ∎. Each argument is the owner's kind: strung together to decide why the claim and not its denial, given the stated assumptions. Nothing in an argument is a reason *for* the claim; every step rules out a case in which its premises are met and its conclusion fails. The same heading replaces *Proof* in Part VI (the finite monotone claim) and Part VIII (the composition claim, l. 353).

The theory's own arguments are no firmer than anyone else's. Deutsch: "Proof is a physical process" (*The Beginning of Infinity*, ch. 8); an argument can contain an error nobody has found. So each Claim is tentatively accepted for as long as its argument stays usable, and (K2) applies to the theory's arguments as it applies to any assessor's. Part XV's list is then what would rule each claim out.

### 3.2 "True" and "false" in definitions and in mathematics

In a definition, "a predicate that could be true or false" becomes "a predicate: a case meets it or fails it", which is the contrast an index lacks. In mathematics, satisfaction is "is met" (rule 3), a truth value is named by its two values ("reachable or unreachable"), and "mathematical truths" are "mathematical statements". "A false theory" becomes "a theory in error", which draft 5 already defines as a theory whose transport to the world fails (l. 211). Nothing in the theory then says "true"; what it says instead is always indexed (meets (E) on C, at a grain, for a boundary), and none of it depends on an assessor (hard case 1).

### 3.3 Part IX's receipts, said contrastively

Draft 5 had two sets, P_j(φ) and N_j(φ), "the usable receipts for and against", and said "Negation exchanges them": whatever counted for φ counted against ¬φ. The proposal keeps one set, R_j(ψ), the usable arguments for assessor j that rule out ψ. Then:

- an assessor tentatively accepts φ when R_j(¬φ) is nonempty, which is draft 5's "established";
- a candidate is ruled out for j when R_j contains an argument that rules it out, which is draft 5's "does not fit";
- "evidence for a claim is evidence against its denial" stops being a claim at all, because there is only one kind of argument: one that rules something out. To decide φ against ¬φ *is* to rule out ¬φ.

For a claim with two sides this changes the words and not the set (hard case 2). With more than two alternatives, as with rivals, it changes more: ruling out one rival leaves the others as they were, and backs none of them.

### 3.4 "Accept", marked tentative at every use

"Tentatively accept" is the only form. It appears in Part VI (the definition); in Part VIII, l. 369 ("Once an assessor tentatively accepts that the target's answer at (a,b) is not y, every such candidate alike is ruled out for that assessor, from the candidate's own answer there, with no record of which candidates failed before or of how any was changed. Here 'tentatively accepts' is meant as in Part VI … If a premise about them ceases to be live, the argument is not usable and those candidates are no longer ruled out by it, every such candidate alike; no candidate becomes an account by that (K2)."); in (K3); in the dependence order of Part XIV ("tentative acceptance on usable arguments; not being ruled out on tentative acceptance and (E); a problem for p on rivals neither of which is ruled out"); and in Part I, l. 67.

### 3.5 "Knowledge"

Draft 5 uses "knowledge" in the name (EK), created explanatory knowledge, and in "knowledge-creation class". (EK) is met when a creative critical episode repairs an explanatory aim through an account c that the episode originated, that is an account on its contract, is deployable, and ran on the active route to the repair. None of the conjuncts is a belief, and none is Deutsch's own sense either, which is "information which, when it is physically embodied in a suitable environment, tends to cause itself to remain so" (*The Beginning of Infinity*, ch. 6). So the word names nothing the definition needs. The proposal renames it **created explanation** (EX), which says what the conjuncts say, and loses nothing. If the owner wants Deutsch's sense in the theory, that would be a new condition (the created explanation tends to keep itself in being in its environment), not a rename.

"Understanding" (Part X) is kept. It is defined by Deploy, a retained capability, and is not an attitude.

## 4. A first look: Part VI's problem in the new words

Draft 5, l. 315–317, with only the vocabulary changed:

> A result is **tentatively accepted** by an assessor who holds a usable argument that rules out its denial (Part IX); by (K3), a result rules out a candidate only together with the background and instruments of the test that yields it. A candidate is **not ruled out** for an assessor when nothing that assessor tentatively accepts rules it out as failing a condition of (E). … Two rivals, neither ruled out for an assessor, pose for that assessor a **problem for p**: a conflict between ideas that nothing the assessor tentatively accepts has decided. (i) The rivals conflict at some (a,b) in C. Whatever the target does there, at most one of them is an account of p, whether or not anyone records what it does; recording it, the target's relations as well as its answer where their answers there agree, is a **test** that solves the problem whatever it records, since once the record is tentatively accepted at least one of them is ruled out; an answer it rules out stays ruled out on p for as long as the argument stays usable (Part VIII). … the problem for p remains until at least one of them is ruled out for that assessor.

The structure is intact: rival, conflict, problem, two kinds of problem, test, easy to vary. Two things changed that are more than words. The permanence of a ruling-out is now explicitly as long as its argument stays usable, which Part VIII already implied. And the definition of problem no longer says anything positive about the two rivals, only that neither is ruled out, which is the owner's S21: "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached."

## 5. Hard cases

These are places where no replacement could be seen that keeps the theory's claim without a forbidden idea, or where the replacement keeps an idea the owner may count as forbidden. They are findings for the next step.

1. **The theory's one assessor-free predicate.** "Meets (E) on C" names no assessor: whether a candidate is an account is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world (l. 317; Part I, "Explanatory realism", l. 67). The new words remove "true", "fact" and "fact of the matter", but the idea stays: the world lets a candidate meet (E) on C or it does not, whether or not anyone ever records it. Part VI needs this. A test solves a problem of the first kind *because* at most one rival can be an account at a pair where they conflict, whatever anyone tentatively accepts. Without it, "account" would become "what an assessor tentatively accepts", and a test would decide nothing. The owner's S21 ("The case for science is reality itself. It must match reality, but not by some fixed infallible metric.") reads as keeping it: the world is not a metric anyone applies. Whether "meets" at an index counts, for the owner, as truth "in any absolute sense" is a question only the owner can answer. The same goes for "error" and "in error", which assume a target that can be missed, and for fidelity, the theory's word for a transport keeping its target's relations.

2. **Two-sided claims.** For a result with two sides (the answer at (a,b) is y, or it is not), "an assessor holds an argument ruling out ¬φ" and draft 5's "φ is established" pick out the same cases. The new words change the stance (the decision is always against a named alternative, and always lapses with its argument), but not what the theory says. This does not break anything; it just means the scrub cannot find a difference to test there.

3. **The dependence order has end points.** Part XIV's order stops at the two imports, the indices and the declared inputs. Structurally, end points of an order are what a foundation is. What stops them being a foundation in the owner's sense is that only meaning passes along the order: no term gains anything but its meaning by being defined in terms of the imports, and the imports are declared and replaceable (Θ is "the adopted physics"). The new words ("defined in terms of", "import", "no cycle and no endless descent") keep that. A reader who takes any order with end points as foundational would still find one here.

4. **"Survive" is already taken.** The owner's S21 uses "survive" for candidates that are not ruled out. Draft 5 defines "survives" for selection (Part IV, l. 195: a transport survives H when it meets the survival condition on H), and Derivation 3 turns on "a differing survivor". Using one word for both would join selected and constructed provenance, which the theory keeps apart ("Neither provenance is reducible to the other", l. 201). So the proposal uses the owner's other phrase from the same sentence, "ruled out", and reads "Both may survive" as "neither is ruled out".

5. **Worth, as someone's appraisal.** The theory must still be able to say that a claim invokes worth or aesthetic value and that the semantics supplies none (Part XI). The words "worth" and "value" stay, but only as the content of an appraisal someone declares (N, now the appraisal relation), never as the theory's own claim. If the owner wants no ranking even inside a declared appraisal, the aesthetic case of Part XI (achievement AR against a purpose, and N) cannot be stated, and that part of the theory goes.

6. **Ruled out, tentatively, and S20.** The owner (S20): "Once the explanation is rescued, the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in." The new wording makes every ruling-out by an assessor last only as long as its argument stays usable. Part VIII, "A failed answer stays failed" (l. 369), already has two levels, and they keep the two together. At the level of the world, by (A), a candidate that answers y at a pair where the target does not is no account of p, whoever offers it and however it was changed; this needs no assessor and no record, and it is where the mistake is kept out. At the level of an assessor, the exclusion lasts while the argument does; it lapses only when a premise stops being live, which is a change someone makes openly, and even then no candidate becomes an account by it. The mistake cannot creep back in; the argument by which an assessor excludes it can lapse, visibly. This is a finding, not a failure. It also depends on hard case 1: the world level is the assessor-free predicate.
