## In plain words

**The gardener.** A gardener says the south bed ripens first because the south wall gets the most sun. One summer the north bed ripens first. One rescue says the neighbour planted an early variety along the north side. Another says that in a wet spring the north bed ripens first. Both fit every summer seen. That is a *problem*: two ideas that conflict, which what has been seen does not settle. No one needs a list of every rescue anyone could invent; the two offered are enough. Her question covers every kind of spring and planting, and the two rescues disagree about, say, a dry spring with the early variety. Try that, and at most one of them survives, whichever way it comes out: a problem of the first kind, which a test solves. Had she asked only about the summers she has seen, the two rescues would still disagree, but only about summers her question does not cover. Nothing the question allows could then tell them apart, and "windy spring" would do as well as "wet spring". That is what it is to be *easy to vary*: to have a rival that fits as well and differs only where the question does not reach. The criticism is the rival, and the cure is a wider question, on which a test can decide.

**The two myths.** The Greeks explained winter by Demeter's grief while her daughter Persephone is underground. A rival says winter comes because Persephone is underground, with no grief in it. On every season the Greeks knew they say the same, so no Greek observation separates them. They still claim different things: if something dimmed the sun on its own, a veil of dust say, the grief story, whose grief is the warmth received, gives cold, and the other does not. So they are rivals whose conflict lies outside the Greek question, each easy to vary relative to the other. The same holds of the true explanation, the tilt of the Earth's axis, on the Greeks' question: offered against the myth, it is easy to vary there too. The term takes no side. Two myths that differ only in their gods, Persephone against the Norse Freyr, are not rivals at all: nothing that could happen separates them.

**The narrowed question.** After the failure the gardener says "my explanation only covered the summers I had seen". That is a new question. Her sun story answers it, but it does not answer the question she first asked, and there it stays failed. No record of her retreat is needed: any explanation that gives the failed answer at that summer fails there, whoever offers it and however it was changed. In the same way, narrowing a question so as to drop the settings where two rescues disagree solves nothing on the old question; the problem stands until something is found out.

**The redescription.** Writing the early-variety rescue with other names, or with its values coded the other way round and the convention carried along, gives the same explanation, not a rival: the two never conflict. Nor is it a rival to add a part that does nothing (a sun god who keeps the axis steady and does no work), to state a part twice, or to say less than the other without contradicting it.

**D3-T.** A tester keeps a lamp controller if it passes two settings; only two designs exist. Ivo points to the discarded design as proof that passing leaves open what a controller does at the untried setting. The discarded design failed a tried setting, so it does not fit what is established, and it poses no problem with the kept one. A problem would need two designs that both pass and differ at the untried setting. Whether such a pair exists is a fact about the designs there are (Derivation 3), not about which designs anyone has offered, and the new text says so.

**What changed in the theory.** The counting lemma of Part VI ("Hard-to-vary", a containment between sets of variants indexed by "explanatory jobs") is gone, and the definition of reach with it. Two paragraphs, "Rivals" and "Problems", say what hard-to-vary comes to without a list, a count or a grade. A paragraph in Part VIII, "A failed answer stays failed", says why a correction sticks without a record. The first wording of "Rivals" was refuted by both attacks and replaced; everything else the attacks asked for was applied.

## The new wording, one plain sentence each

- **W34.1 (Part VI).** The lemma over "explanatory jobs" and the definition of reach are removed, and the paragraph is headed "Commitments that do no work".
- **W33.1 (Part VI).** What stays says that (E) does not require every commitment to do work, with a test for an idle commitment that now applies only to a candidate that has a support.
- **W59.1, "Rivals" (Part VI).** Two candidates are rivals when one has been offered in place of the other and they conflict somewhere the target admits a change, inside the question or outside it: their answers differ there, or each could be right there under what the physics allows and nothing it allows makes both right.
- **W59.1, "Problems" (Part VI).** Two rivals that both fit what is established pose a problem: if they conflict inside the question, a test there solves it whatever it shows; if only outside, the question cannot hold either to account, both are easy to vary relative to each other, the criticism is the rival, and a finer question is where a test can decide.
- **W60.1, "A failed answer stays failed" (Part VIII).** A candidate that gives, at a pair of the question, an answer the target is established not to give fails that question and every question with the same target and query that contains the pair, whoever offers it and with no record of earlier failures; a narrowed contract or a changed query is a new question, and the old failure stands.
- **W36.1 (Part I).** "How hard an account is to vary" becomes "whether an account is easy to vary".
- **W38.1 (the sources note).** Rivals and problems are mapped onto Deutsch's pages, and four departures are recorded.

**W59.1 (lines 317–319; R2-25; change of claim).** Inserted before the rule that opens Part VII:

```text
**Rivals.** Two explanatory candidates for one question \(p\) are **rivals** when one of them has been offered as an answer to \(p\) in place of the other and they conflict at some admitted edit–boundary pair of the target that both their transports translate, in \(C\) or outside it. Two candidates **conflict** at such a pair \((a,b)\) when their answers there differ, or when each of them could meet (F1), (F2) and (A) there under some relations of the target that the adopted physics admits (Part I) and no such relations let both, as when two of their active components with one anchor have different relations there. A candidate offered for \(p\) is offered for the whole of \(p\): it claims (F1), (F2) and (A) at every pair of \(C\), tested or not, and the rest of (E) on \(C\); outside \(C\) it claims nothing on \(p\), but what its organization and transport give there can conflict with what another's give. Two candidates that differ only in how they are written, one carried onto the other by a structure-preserving bijection that takes its transport with it and leaves its answers as they are, conflict at no pair (Part VIII, Recoding; Derivation 8), and nor do two candidates, however differently they are cut, that some such relations of the target would let both meet (F1), (F2) and (A) at each admitted pair both translate; neither pair is a pair of rivals. A result is **established** for an assessor who holds a usable receipt for it (Part IX); by (K3), a result that tells against a candidate tells against it only together with the background and instruments of the test that yields it. A candidate **fits** what is established for an assessor when no result established for that assessor shows it failing a condition of (E). No list of all rivals is supposed: a candidate's rivals are among the candidates someone has offered, and a candidate that nobody has offered is no one's rival. Nor are rivals a selection population: whether a selected transport is underdetermined at an unseen pair is fixed by its population (Derivation 3), whatever rivals anyone offers.

**Problems.** Two rivals that both fit what is established for an assessor pose, for that assessor, a **problem for \(p\)**: a conflict between ideas that what the assessor has established has not settled. It is of one of two kinds. (i) The rivals conflict at some \((a,b)\in C\). Whatever the target does there, at most one of them is an account of \(p\), whether or not anyone can establish what it does; establishing it, the target's relations as well as its answer where their answers there agree, is a **test** that solves the problem whatever it shows, since afterwards at most one of them fits; an answer it refutes stays refuted on \(p\) (Part VIII). (ii) They conflict at no pair of \(C\), only at admitted pairs outside it: the contract does not contain their conflict. Their answers then agree at every pair of \(C\), so no established answer holds one of them to account without the other; a test inside \(C\) can refute one of them without the other only for a failure of its own; and where both meet (E) on \(C\) both are accounts of \(p\). A candidate is **easy to vary**, in the sense used here, when it and a rival pose a problem of the second kind; the rival is then easy to vary too, and the term says nothing about which of them is right. A criticism that a candidate is easy to vary must supply such a rival (Part IX). Two rivals that are both accounts on \(C\) conflict only outside it, and a claim that one is right and the other wrong is a claim that some admitted change outside \(C\) separates them, and must supply it, as a claim that one assignment of anchors is "really" right must (Derivation 2, Consequence). A finer contract that contains a change at which two rivals conflict makes a new question (Part III), on which, offered for it, they pose a problem of the first kind while both fit; whether a candidate is an account of a question is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world, not by when anyone first asks the question or by whether anyone has checked the candidate against it (Parts I and V). A contract narrowed to leave out the pairs at which two rivals conflict also makes a different question; it solves nothing on \(p\), and the problem for \(p\) stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting. Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates. A problem that a system represents can be a recognized difficulty (Part X), as when choosing one of two rivals meets a claimed obligation only by dropping the other, which fits as well, where keeping every rival that fits is among the protected obligations (Part XI).
```

## 5. Gains and losses

**Gains.**
- Part VI no longer carries a count over a family nobody declares, on jobs nobody defines; "job" leaves the theory.
- Hard-to-vary is stated without a list, a count or a grade: rivals conflict; a problem is two rivals that both fit; its kind says whether a test the question admits can solve it; to be easy to vary is to have a rival of the second kind; the criticism is the rival.
- The crucial test (O24) is stated directly, and a narrowed question solves no problem on the old one.
- Redescriptions, compatible accounts, duplicates and idle additions are not rivals.
- A correction sticks on its question with no record of candidates, only a receipt of what the target does.

**Losses.**
- About 1,000 words of theory text (W59.1 about 830, W60.1 about 240, less the lemma and the reach sentence), and seven bold terms.
- "Easy to vary" is symmetric and relative to the question and to what an assessor has established. It prefers neither rival, and on the Greeks' question it applies to the tilt as much as to the myth, once the myth or a variant patched outside the known world is offered.
- A candidate nobody has challenged is not called easy to vary, however loose it is; Deutsch's judgement before any rival is given up.
- A candidate with a kind-(ii) rival is still an account of its question if it meets (E); Deutsch would say it explains nothing.
- Variants that differ only in their gods are not rivals at all, though Deutsch calls them incompatible.
- The containment of file 00 and the definition of reach go; Deutsch's reach has no named counterpart.
- W60.1 does not reach pairs beyond the failed one, runs of saves, grain changes, or a coarser query settled by the same receipt.

## 6. What remains uncertain

- **No outside reader has seen any of it.** A cross-examination should carry the six entries before the freeze.
- **The owner's example depends on an admitted change.** "Demeter grieves" and "Persephone is underground" are rivals only if the target admits a change that sets the mediating state on its own. In the world it does (a veil of dust); in a model that admits only place and half they are not rivals, and their difference is idle.
- **What the physics admits at a pair.** The conflict quantifies over the target's relations that the adopted physics admits (Part I). Part XIV does not list them, and an assessor's view of them is not modelled.
- **Failures derivable from the candidate alone.** Whether a derivation from the candidate alone (non-circular dependence, a component tied to nothing) is an established result is not said. A myth tied to nothing is a rival of the tilt on the Greek question, and poses a problem with it or not according to this.
- **Recoding.** Whether Part IV's port translation may carry a map of values is not said; the models ran both forms, and neither gives a rival.
- **The residual.** Two partial claims, each exact about a different link and both true inside the question, can be rivals of the second kind; the finer question's test refutes both.
- **Symmetry.** If the owner wants "easy to vary" to point at one side, a further condition is needed, and any such condition risks becoming a grade.
- **The models are small.** The spaces of relations ("every relation", "functional") stand in for what a physics admits; only the splitter model fixes a law.
- **W33.1's scoping** changes a sentence the S90 cross-examination ruled KEEP (R24); it adds a condition and removes nothing said of a candidate with a support.
- **The dependence order** does not place the new terms; a clause is available (the change list, "Found in draft 4").
