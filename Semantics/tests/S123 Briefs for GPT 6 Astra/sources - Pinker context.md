## 1. The mind as evolved computation (How the Mind Works, 1997)

- **Thesis.** The mind is "a naturally selected system of organs of computation": specialised faculties, designed by natural selection to solve problems our foraging ancestors met **[checked: P2005 abstract; Edge1997]**.
- **Method: reverse engineering.** Ask what a faculty was selected to do, and explain its structure from that function **[checked: Edge1997]**. Fodor's objection: present function would do; evolutionary history adds little **[checked: F1998]**.
- **Computation** means representations with both causal and semantic properties, whose transitions mirror logic, probability or cause and effect; not a Turing machine's architecture, and including analogue and graded computation **[checked: P2005, pp. 2–3, 6]**.
- **Knowledge, on this view**, is represented "as patterns in bits of matter", in a system built so that if the old representations were accurate, the new ones are too **[checked: P2005, p. 2]**. Knowledge is accurate representation used in inference; nothing requires it to copy or preserve itself.
- **Specialised domains.** Reasoning is organised around core intuitions about objects, living things, minds and artifacts; specialised, but not sealed off from other information **[checked: P2005, pp. 15–16]**.
- **Combinatorial structure.** Thought needs structured representations (individuals versus kinds, variables, composition, recursion), which plain associative networks lack **[unverified: memory of the book; the parallel critique of connectionist models of language by Pinker and Prince is checked in SEP-Lang]**.
- **Learning is a designed faculty.** Pinker calls the learned-versus-innate dichotomy incoherent and the brain "a highly structured and adapted organ" **[checked: Edge1997, his reply to Quartz]**.
- **Designed for the ancestral world, not for truth.** Outside mathematics and logic "there is no such thing as a universal true-belief-fixer"; inference rests on fallible built-in assumptions about the world **[checked: P2005, p. 18]**.
- **Not universal.** In 2023 Pinker called intelligence "an algorithm that can solve certain problems in certain environments, and maybe not others in other environments", and AGI an incoherent idea **[checked: DP2023]**.

## 2. What it takes to learn from data (the learnability work)

Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition* (1989) builds on *Language Learnability and Language Development* (1984) and the paper "Formal models of language learning" (1979). His own list of books has no title "Learning and Cognition" **[checked: Pinker's book list]**.

- **The problem.** Infinitely many generalisations fit any finite sample, so any learner that generalises correctly must bring prior constraints on its hypotheses; "the data alone are insufficient". Every theory has such constraints, whether in a network's features and wiring or in grammatical principles **[checked: P2004, pp. 949–950]**.
- **Four parts of any learning problem**: a class of possible targets; an environment (the data, its order, any feedback); a learning strategy (proposing hypotheses, testing them on the data); a success criterion **[checked: P1995 §4.1, paraphrased; P1979]**.
- **Gold's results, as Pinker uses them.** A learner that guesses a grammar after each example and must eventually settle on the right one can learn any primitive recursive language if it is told which strings are not sentences, but nothing beyond the finite languages from sentences alone, because "if you guess too large a language, the sample will never tell you you're wrong" (Gold, quoted by Pinker). No general strategy beats enumerating grammars, and that is astronomically slow **[checked: P1979, pp. 225–228]**.
- **Ways out**: data in an informative order; a weaker success criterion; a known statistical distribution; or a constrained hypothesis class (a weak nativism), neither too broad nor too narrow **[checked: P1979, pp. 228–232]**.
- **Meaning as a second input.** Pairing each sentence with its meaning, read from context, removes Gold's barrier without negative evidence, but costs strong assumptions about how meanings map onto forms **[checked: P1979, pp. 243–244]**. Children do not learn language from radio or television, because they cannot tell what is being talked about **[checked: P1995 §6.5]**. In 2023 Pinker said children use what a speaker probably means, so their learning is "not pure cryptography over the signals themselves"; language "is more like a transducer than just a pattern signal" **[checked: DP2023]**.
- **What children actually get.** Speech to them is almost all grammatical; correction for grammar is absent or too weak to use **[checked: P1995 §§6.1–6.2]**. Children raised without language are mute; children who hear only a pidgin, among other children, build a full creole **[checked: P1995 §6.1]**.
- **Bootstrapping (1984).** To find nouns and verbs in speech at all, the child assumes that early on things are named by nouns, actions by verbs and agents by subjects, and learns the exceptions later from where they occur **[checked: P1995 §8.2]**. The book works this out for a whole grammar **[unverified: details from memory]**.
- **Baker's paradox (1989).** Productive patterns, arbitrary-looking exceptions and no negative evidence cannot all hold **[checked: Cop2008, p. 1]**. Children say *Don't giggle me*; adults accept *give the museum the money* but not *donate the museum the money* **[checked: P1995; Amb2013; SEP-Lang]**. Pinker's answer: exceptions are not arbitrary. Constructions have semantic cores; verbs whose meanings fit narrow semantic subclasses take part; Latinate verbs like *donate* also resist the double-object form **[checked: Amb2013; Cop2008, pp. 63–66]**. Children taught a new verb for an event whose salient part was either a zigzag motion or a colour change chose the direct object the meaning predicts, without hearing the verb in a sentence **[checked: P1995, argument-structure section]**. Critics add learning from absence (pre-emption, entrenchment); Pinker answers that these still need constraints, only left unstated **[checked: Amb2013; P2004, pp. 951–952; SEP-Lang]**.

## 3. Where Pinker meets Deutsch and Marletto

- **Agreement: data alone do not make knowledge.** Deutsch: knowledge consists of "conjectured explanations", not extrapolations from sense data **[checked: Aeon2012]**. In 2023, Deutsch said of children "Yes, they're forming explanations." and Pinker: "They're forming explanations, exactly." **[checked: DP2023]**
- **Agreement: inherited adaptations carry knowledge** (Deutsch: biological knowledge exists but "is non-explanatory, and therefore has limited reach"; Marletto: genes as self-preserving, transformation-enabling information) **[checked: the project's records S89 and S110, quoting the books]**.
- **Agreement: new knowledge is limited by the rate at which the world can be tested** **[checked: DP2023]**.
- **Conflict: universality.** Deutsch: universality guarantees "there exists some program which will meet the criteria for being an AGI"; the problem is the knowledge to write it **[checked: DP2023]**. Pinker: intelligence is specialised; AGI is incoherent **[checked: DP2023]**.
- **Conflict: the source of the prior constraints.** Pinker: natural selection. Deutsch: creative conjecture by a universal explainer, with almost nothing built in **[checked: DP2023; Aeon2012]**.
- **Conflict: explanation.** For Pinker, large-scale explanation (science) is a social, cumulative achievement of many minds **[checked: P2005, pp. 10–11]**; for Deutsch, creating explanations is a universal capacity of each person **[checked: Aeon2012]**.

## 4. What this suggests for Avida (Claude's reading, not Pinker's)

- **Transports learning from data needs** (a transport, in the project's semantics, is a structure-preserving map from one organization to another, faithful when it tracks the target under every admitted change): T1, a data channel from a source outside the learner, carrying a regularity the learner lacks; T2, a second channel to the same events (context, meaning); T3, a fixed prior link from the learner's categories to features of the data (bootstrapping); T4, the learned mapping itself; T5, a bounded class of candidates. The semantics' own Argument 3, that what a finite history leaves open about a selected transport is what its population leaves open, is the logical problem of learning in other words.
- **Avida now**: inputs are numbers the execution environment hands out (a fixed set in one order, or a designed stream), so T1's source is the selector's own code; there is no T2 and no T3; the candidates (all mutable instruction sequences) are very broad; and pay for correct outputs is in effect negative evidence, the reverse of a child's situation.
- **A lead in Avida itself.** In a modified Avida, associative learning evolved when environmental patterns varied between generations but held within a lifetime, while patterns stable across generations produced reflexes, which were its building blocks **[checked: Pontes2020, abstract via PubMed]**.
- **Three readings of knowledge to test against each other**: K1, information that causes its own copying, resists change and remains (the project owner's three properties; constructor theory); K2, Deutsch's, with explanatory knowledge a further kind that selection does not make; K3, Pinker's, accurate representations built within a lifetime by a learner whose constraints were selected. Avida has instances of K1, none of K3.
- **Is a learned grammar selected or constructed** in the semantics' terms? Three readings: selected (a candidate surviving its data history), though the child represents the meanings, which the semantics' definition of selection forbids; constructed (the semantics: "Reconstruction by a learner is construction; relay is not"); or constructed on selected material. Avida's evolved capabilities are selected; nothing in Avida is constructed.
