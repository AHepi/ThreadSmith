Original conceptual critique and proposal, not book evidence. No machine has been implemented and no experiments have been run. The attached books were not read for this independent assessment.

The strongest objection is that successful classification can conceal where the knowledge came from. A machine may appear to discover circles and squares while its sensor supplies curvature, its primitive operations count corners, its candidate generator offers the two completed definitions, or its evaluator reveals which answer is wanted. Those choices can produce a legitimate classifier, but they cannot establish that the machine constructed the relevant distinctions. The investigation must trace each successful decision through the actual sensor readings, retained state, constructed representation, and executed operations. This traceability follows from the aim of locating the machine’s contribution. The particular representation and implementation remain design choices.

The earliest decisive task should use two episodes that finish with exactly the same observation but require different answers because of earlier observations. A moving aperture could reveal scattered portions of a boundary, then stop on an identical uninformative patch in both episodes. The machine must combine earlier observations with their spatial relationships. Position, duration, and observation order must vary independently of the answer so that a clock or fixed action sequence cannot substitute for memory. Removing or scrambling the relevant retained state should selectively destroy the distinction. This would demonstrate a causal contribution from temporal memory. It would not establish that the memory mechanism resembles human memory.

The machine must also encounter observations that do not determine an answer. A short boundary fragment can fit several shapes. If the environment allows rounded squares and other curves, finite, noisy observations may never justify an exact mathematical classification. The machine should preserve multiple possibilities, request a discriminating observation when one is available, and leave the question unresolved when none is available. What counts as sufficient evidence depends on the declared environment and measurement tolerance. Universal certainty about circles cannot be obtained merely by improving the classifier.

Recognition should transfer across irrelevant changes without receiving new shape instructions. Translation, size, rotation, observation order, and harmless sensor noise are useful variations, provided the original learning conditions did not already give away the corresponding answers. The machine should also fail appropriately on a missing boundary, an unfamiliar polygon, or a rounded square outside its learned distinction. A compulsory choice between circle and square hides ignorance. The requirement to separate supported conclusions from unsupported ones follows from reliable thinking; the particular uncertainty representation remains open.

The candidate constructor is the next place to look for a concealed solution. “Construct a model” is not an executable mechanism until we know which expressions can be built, which compositions are legal, how candidates are generated, and what limits terminate a search. “Revise an explanation” likewise needs a concrete account of what changes after a failed prediction. A sufficiently large library of completed explanation patterns could imitate model construction. That is not automatically useless, but the report must identify which structure was supplied and which structure the machine produced. Giving every component an intelligent name supplies no evidence that the required operations exist.

A useful test would withhold a whole compositional structure rather than merely withholding new examples of familiar templates. After learning simpler relations, the machine could face a task requiring a new combination of them. Its successful representation should be inspectable, and removing the newly constructed relation should remove the corresponding ability. This would support a limited claim about compositional construction. Whether the initial operation set is adequate for indefinitely many kinds of reasoning would remain unresolved.

Active experimentation needs a stronger test than allowing the machine to move a sensor. Arrange two currently viable explanations that agree about everything already observed but predict different outcomes for an available action. Compare its chosen action with actions that cannot distinguish them. The machine should seek the separating observation and then revise only what the result warrants. A single contrary observation can reject a precisely stated prediction when the observation and background assumptions are secure. It does not automatically reject every related model, identify the correct replacement, or prove that an unobserved possibility is impossible.

Resource limits matter because exhaustive search can disguise the absence of a useful construction process. Record the available memory, allowed operations, candidates considered, and time to action. Then increase task complexity while holding the implementation fixed. A finite search policy is necessary for an executable proposal, but no particular policy follows logically from the theory. Handwritten priorities, reusable constructions, and learned search guidance are competing design choices whose contribution should be tested rather than assumed.

Finally, circle and square recognition can establish grounded discrimination, temporal integration, and some transfer. Calling that result a thinking machine requires further evidence: using learned relations to plan actions, anticipating consequences, detecting mistaken expectations, constructing a changed account, and transferring useful structure to a new task. Each further ability needs its own demonstration. Linguistic learning can motivate these mechanisms, but success in language does not by itself establish their adequacy for perception or action. Neither the initial classifier nor a broader successful program would, on its own, establish consciousness.

The defensible theory is therefore conditional: a machine could exhibit increasingly general thinking if its executable operations can construct grounded representations, preserve task-relevant history, generate and compare consequence-bearing models, and change its actions and models when evidence warrants it. The proposed experiments can discover whether a particular construction achieves those abilities. They cannot turn the names of those abilities into a sufficient recipe.

Removing or replacing proposed parts gives different verdicts. Some retained influence of temporal history is logically necessary when identical current inputs require different answers because of earlier events; an explicit timestamped event log is replaceable by another state mechanism that preserves the needed distinctions. The exact symbolic implementation is replaceable if structured role-binding and a causal trace of the machine’s contribution are preserved. Whether the proposal contains a sufficient search and abstraction mechanism remains unknown. Goal priorities are supplied engineering choices, and their particular ordering does not follow from the general ambition to build a thinking machine.
