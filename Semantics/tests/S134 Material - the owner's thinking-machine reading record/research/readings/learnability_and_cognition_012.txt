**Subject and central claims.** This section closes Chapter 3's analysis of the passive and opens Chapter 4's problem of negative exceptions. Steven Pinker argues that the passive carries an "agency effect": *The ship was sunk* entails some agent or force, unlike *The ship sank*, and purposive adjuncts confirm this (*The ship was sunk to collect the insurance* versus the impossible *The ship sank to collect the insurance*) (file page 128). He proposes a thematic core: X is in a circumstance characterized by Y's acting on it, or more generally Y's being responsible for it. The passive rule's primary operation suppresses or demotes the agent from the top of the semantic structure into an embedded position (file pages 128–129). Crucially, semantic structures may contain "implicit" arguments that are not linkable to grammatical functions but are still entailed to exist and can control adjuncts; full passives merely mark the agent as "open," linked to a *by*-phrase because *by* alone encodes a quasi-agentive role (file page 129).

**Compositionality and permitted scope.** Because the passive subject is redefined as a theme, a "predication effect" follows: *Dams are built by beavers* differs in interpretation from *Beavers build dams* (file page 129). Verbs whose objects cannot be construed as themes resist passivization. Idiom chunks passivize in proportion to how interpretable they are as themes (*Tabs were kept on subversives*, *The hatchet was finally buried*) versus *The bucket was kicked*; fake objects fail (*A hearty laugh was laughed*) (file page 129). Prepositional passives work with unergatives (*The bed was slept in by the Shah*) but not unaccusatives (*The package was accumulated on by dust*), and require an affected or status-altered object (*This bridge has been driven on*) (file page 130). Symmetrical predicates (*resemble*), pure spatial or possessive verbs (*contain*, *have*), and measure verbs resist passivization because no agent–patient asymmetry can be construed (file page 131). Pinker stresses that this is a constraint on a rule's permitted scope, not memorized exceptions: it is "a powerful constraint on children's passivization of newly learned verbs" (file page 131). He explicitly leaves three questions open, including why semantically similar verbs differ in passivizability (file page 131).

**Universality and its limits.** Pinker notes this universality was not predicted by his earlier view, in which criteria were abstracted from a database of verbs and learned individually, nor is it inevitable: each language could have its own linking rules (file page 132). He reviews cross-linguistic evidence for universal linking (agents as subjects, patients as objects) and for parallel constraints on causatives, passives, datives, and locatives (file pages 132–135), while conceding that languages differ in exactly which verbs alternate (file page 135). He flags gaps: no surveys for oblique arguments (file page 132) or locatives (file page 135).

**Negative exceptions.** Chapter 4 concedes that coarse semantic criteria do not rule out fine-grained nonalternating pairs like *threw* versus *carried* (file pages 136–137). Because these are negative exceptions, Baker's paradox remains unsolved; subtle meaning appeals are sketched but not endorsed (file page 137).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, the passive analysis models how a compositional semantic structure can carry an unexpressed but entailed participant that constrains inference and adjunct control—a mechanism for representing absent causes. The thematic-core idea suggests representing events as structured role configurations whose compatibility with a construction licenses or blocks a transformation, generalizing beyond stored verb lists. The negative-exception problem warns that coarse semantic features underdetermine productivity: a machine needs finer-grained meaning representations or learning mechanisms to delimit a rule's scope. Nothing here establishes universal learning laws; these are language-specific findings with cross-linguistic tendencies.
