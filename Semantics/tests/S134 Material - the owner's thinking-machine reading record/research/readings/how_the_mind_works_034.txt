**Subject and scope.** This section moves from artifacts and folk biology into "mind reading" (attributing beliefs and desires to others), then into a "trivium" of abstract tools: logic, arithmetic, and probability. The printed page labels run from 329 to 338 (file pages 192–197); the arithmetic passage is cut off mid-sentence at the end.

**Artifacts and design.** Steven Pinker reports Daniel Dennett's three stances: physical for rocks, intentional for minds, and design for artifacts, where one imputes an intention to a designer (file page 192, printed 329). Axes and telephones have obvious purposes; paintings may be deliberately inscrutable; Stonehenge or a shipwreck's gears probably have functions we cannot recover. Because artifacts depend on human intentions, they invite interpretation and criticism — "artifact hermeneutics." Pinker notes folk-biological categories like "vegetable" and "pet" are artifacts defined by human needs and literally shaped by selective breeding (file page 192).

**Other minds.** Simon Baron-Cohen's Mary story shows we explain action by beliefs and desires, not physics; such explanations are correct because beliefs and desires really cause behavior (file page 192, printed 329–330). Philosophers define mental states as attitudes (believes-that, desires-that) toward propositions. Content is separate from fact: "there are unicorns" can be false while "John thinks there are unicorns" is true. Ascription requires setting a thought aside in mental quotation marks, and nesting ("Mary knows that John thinks…") needs special computational architecture plus recursive grammar (file page 192, printed 330). We infer minds indirectly from words, faces, and best explanation — recognizing a dog in a pantomime is harder than seeing one.

**Development and autism.** Infants stare at eyes at two months, follow gaze at one year, and by eighteen to twenty-four months separate pretense from belief (banana as telephone). Three-year-olds use "think, know, remember"; four-year-olds pass the false-belief (Smarties) test, attributing beliefs they know are false, though three-year-olds can be coaxed into it (file pages 192–193, printed 330–331). Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie, and Uta Frith propose that autistic people are "mind-blind": a damaged mind-attribution module. Autism is universal, lifelong, and neurological/genetic — not caused by "icebox mothers" as Bruno Bettelheim claimed (file page 193, printed 331–332). Autistic children fail pretense and false-belief tasks yet pass a logically identical non-mental task (the Rubber Ducky photograph), showing that world contents require suitable mental machinery (file pages 193–194, printed 332–333). Pinker adds that chairs and surfaces are "knots and patterns" of matter that a mind must grasp; George Miller calls the experienced world an adaptive interpretation.

**Logic.** Logic infers truth from form, not content (file pages 194–195, printed 333–334). Students fail the archeologist/biologist puzzle, yet people use logical words before age three and reason well in the restaurant-menu example, where world knowledge supplies premises ("or means not both") that logic then combines (file page 195, printed 334–335). Failures arise because "or" is ambiguous, because any truth spawns infinitely many trivial consequences (the frame problem), forcing heuristic guessing grounded in cooperative conversation, and because logic is enmeshed with world knowledge rather than a free-standing calculator (file pages 195–196, printed 335–336).

**Cheater detection.** Peter Wason's card task shows only five to ten percent choose the falsifying cards, yet people succeed on the beer-and-age version. Leda Cosmides showed success tracks contracts (benefit contingent on requirement): people search for cheaters, even reversing logical direction when costs and benefits flip, and the same story yields different answers depending on whose viewpoint is taken. Findings replicate among the Shiwiar of Ecuador (file pages 196–197, printed 336–338). Robert Trivers predicted a cheater-detector from the evolution of reciprocation; Cosmides appears to have found it. Pinker's verdict: the mind uses logic, recruited by language understanding, mixed with knowledge, and superseded by content-specific rules.

**Number.** Infants discriminate two from three at one week, track up to four items across modalities, and by five months expect one Mickey plus one to yield two (Karen Wynn). Pinker sketches an accumulator mechanism — like water filling from a faucet — registering quantity without language (file page 197, printed 338).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** A thinking machine needs attitude-plus-proposition representations with quotation-like nesting, a design stance for artifacts, and domain-specific inference rules (such as cheater detection) alongside formal logic. It must budget inference against the frame problem using relevance heuristics, and separate perceptually constituted objects from raw physics. These are design proposals; the section does not establish them as sufficient for intelligence, and it says nothing about non-neural implementation.
