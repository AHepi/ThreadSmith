**Subject and central claims.** This section, spanning file pages 223 to 227, continues Steven Pinker's evolutionary-psychological treatment of emotion, moving from disgust to fear. The printed page markers visible in the text run from 381 to 388. Pinker argues that disgust is an adaptation against ingesting dangerous animal matter, and that fear comprises several adaptations tuned to ancestral hazards (file pages 223–227).

**Disgust: scope and learning.** Disgusting things are almost always of animal origin; people eat only a tiny fraction of available animal parts, and unfamiliar parts are avoided (file page 223). Disgust obeys "sympathetic magic": contagion (contact transmits contamination permanently) and similarity (resemblance evokes revulsion), as with rubber vomit or a sterilized roach (file page 223). Yet the permitted list varies by culture, implying learning: toddlers eat imitation feces and grasshoppers, while four-year-olds refuse them, and only by age eight do children reject juice briefly dipped with a grasshopper (file page 223). Pinker rejects Rozin's proposal that disgust is learned in middle childhood through scolding, citing the toddler evidence, childhood finickiness after age three (Cashdan), and the fact that animals are presumptively disgusting rather than presumptively edible (file page 223). Cashdan's alternative: the first two years are a sensitive period when mothers control intake; tastes then shrink to what was permitted, persisting into adulthood (file page 223).

**Function of disgust.** Rozin's "omnivore's dilemma" holds that a broad menu brings poisoning risk from toxins, parasites, and microbial spoilage (file page 224). Disgust deters dangerous animal matter; local species and diseases differ, so specific tastes cannot be innate, and children use elders as food tasters (file page 224). Similarity effects are not irrational: simulacra are crafted to trigger the response, like movies or pornography (file page 224). Contagion intuitions track germ reproduction — unlike plant poisons, microbes have no safe dose and spread by contact; "disgust is intuitive microbiology" (file page 224). Small "animalitos" are avoided where large game exists, per optimal foraging theory, not sanitation (file page 224).

**Food taboos.** Pinker dismisses rabbinical health and ethical rationales for Jewish dietary laws as easily refuted (file page 225). Harris's ecological account — pigs compete with desert resources; Indian cattle are more valuable alive — is more plausible but incomplete, failing to explain shrimp taboos among desert tribes or Polish and Brooklyn Jews (file page 225). Pinker's own "cynical" proposal: taboos are ethnic weapons. Shared eating builds alliances; banning a neighbor's favorite food makes fraternization costly and exploits disgust learned in the sensitive period, deterring defection; elders cloak this in "talmudic sophistry" (file page 226).

**Fear: a short universal list.** Named phobias are largely wordplay; real fears cluster around snakes, spiders, heights, storms, carnivores, darkness, blood, strangers, confinement, deep water, scrutiny, and separation — ancestral hazards (file page 226). Captive chimpanzees and tamarins fear snakes and even plastic tubing (file page 226). Fear is probably several emotions: different phobias respond to different drugs and elicit distinct reactions — fleeing an animal, freezing at a precipice, fainting at blood (file page 227). Island animals without predators lose fear, supporting adaptation over neural bug (file page 227). Modern fears misfire: city children fear lions and tigers, not cars or bathtubs (file page 227).

**Constraints on fear learning.** Watson conditioned a child to fear a rat, and rats learn to fear a shock room, but conditioning is not general: children will not fear opera glasses, and rats learn black-room fear slowly (file page 227). Seligman's "prepared" associations explain this; few phobias trace to neutral trauma cues (file page 227). Marks and Nesse treat phobias as innate fears never unlearned: infants fear strangers and separation; ages three to five bring standard phobias, later mastered (file page 227). Social learning calibrates fear — children copy parents, therapy uses models, monkeys imitate (file page 227).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests: default-deny categories with a small permitted set learned from trusted exemplars during a bounded acquisition window; contamination semantics where contact propagates taint; similarity-based generalization of affect; separate hazard-response policies rather than one fear value; and prepared, non-arbitrary association slots constraining what can be learned from pairing. Innate hazard templates plus local calibration from observed survivors mirror "innate capacities versus acquired knowledge." None of this is demonstrated machine intelligence; the text is about human emotion, not architecture.
