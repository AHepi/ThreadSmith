**Subject and central claims.** This is the opening of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition*, Chapter 1, "A Learnability Paradox" (file pages 22–32; printed labels 1–11 visible). Pinker frames language acquisition as a logical induction problem: children hear finitely many sentences yet must master an open-ended language, so infinitely many hypotheses fit the input while differing from the target (file pages 26–27). The learnability approach asks which mental representations and processes make this succeed, demanding an unbroken explanatory path from children's experience to adult knowledge (file page 23).

**Argument structure and representation.** Verbs are choosy: *fall* and *dine* take only subjects, *devour* requires an object, *put* requires subject, object, and oblique object (file page 25). These demands are stored in lexical entries as argument structures (also called subcategorization frames or theta grids), and general principles such as Chomsky's Theta-Criterion and Projection Principle require the specified arguments to be present at every level of representation (file pages 25–26). Pinker notes a provocative suggestion that language acquisition may be nothing but acquiring information about words (file page 26). This is a claim about linguistic representation specifically, not a general theory of concepts or perception.

**The learning logic.** Constraints restrict the learner to a hypothesis space containing the correct grammar; predictions are compared with input so wrong hypotheses can be rejected (file page 27). Positive evidence suffices to falsify hypotheses that are disjoint from, intersecting with, or subsets of the target, but a hypothesis generating a superset of the target cannot be falsified by positive evidence alone; it requires negative evidence about ungrammatical strings (file pages 27–28). Brown and Hanlon found parents do not approve or disapprove contingent on grammaticality, so children apparently lack such evidence (file page 28). The tension: children must generalize beyond heard sentences yet must not generate supersets (file page 28).

**Baker's paradox.** Hearing *give*, *pass*, *tell* in both dative forms invites a rule mapping prepositional dative to double-object (file page 29). But *donate* and *report* resist the double object, and absence of such sentences in input is uninformative, since infinitely many grammatical sentences are never heard (file page 30). Without negative evidence, exceptions should be regularized in one generation. The same pattern appears in passive (*touch* versus *resemble*), lexical causative (*roll* versus *cry*), and locative (*load* versus *pour*) alternations (file pages 30–31).

**Three components and attempted solutions.** The paradox rests on lack of negative evidence, productivity of rules, and arbitrariness: near-synonyms like *give*/*donate* or *load*/*pour* differ unpredictably, so no simple semantic guideline marks where rules apply (file page 31). Proposed solutions deny one of these. Pinker dismisses vague proposals (Discovery Procedures, Competition Model, abduction, distributional analysis) as unable to represent the adult distinctions, let alone their acquisition (file pages 31–32). He then examines "subtle negative evidence": mothers sometimes repeat, question, or follow up differently on ill-formed utterances, but studies lump all ill-formedness together and do not show reliable feedback for every ungrammatical string (file page 32). True negative evidence would require information about the ungrammaticality of every ungrammatical string, which no study demonstrates (file page 32).

**Limits and scope.** The section is explicitly about verb argument structure and lexical learning, not perception, memory, time, causation, goals, analogy, creativity, emotion, or social cognition; those topics are absent here. Pinker distinguishes a productive rule's permitted scope from mere memorization of heard forms (file pages 29, 31) and treats the child's hypothesis space as constrained, though innate versus acquired sources of those constraints are not yet resolved in this excerpt. The claims are narrow language-learning results, not established universal learning laws.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For an inspectable machine, the useful design ideas are: store per-item structural requirements (argument structures) rather than flat associations; maintain an explicit hypothesis space with permitted generalizations and item-specific exceptions; and record which input sentences support or fail to support each hypothesis, since absence of a form is not evidence against it. A system that generalizes a transformation rule must also carry a mechanism marking exceptions, and must not treat silence as disconfirmation. Pinker does not supply such a mechanism here, nor address grounding in perception, temporal memory, or action; those remain open engineering problems. Nothing in this section demonstrates human-level intelligence or consciousness.
