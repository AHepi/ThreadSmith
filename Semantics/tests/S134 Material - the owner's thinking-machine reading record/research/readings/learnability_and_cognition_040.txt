This section, from Chapter 7 of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition*, examines children's verb errors and argues that semantic development causally shapes syntactic development. The material is text, not bibliography, and is largely legible, though some examples are visually formatted and the final paragraph is cut off mid-sentence.

**Source claims.** Pinker first considers whether children's verb substitutions reflect stable wrong meanings or online speech-production slips. He and Larry Rosen found such errors rare: only four in transcripts of Adam, Eve, and Sarah, and four clear errors across 570 elicited invitations (file page 413). Yet the errors are systematic across children, verb-focused rather than noun-focused, and unself-corrected, unlike adult slips; Pinker concludes children's verb meanings must be "shifting or poorly consolidated" (file page 414). Whether errors stem from fetching the wrong stem or from a misconstrued entry remains open, since English densely fills the motion-and-possession semantic space and some errors fit the used verb's own prepositions (file pages 414–415).

The central proposal links semantics to argument structure. Children first use *put, give, make, let* correctly in narrow contexts, then err in thematically structured ways: *put* for *make* (change of location versus state), *put* for *give* (location versus possession), *make* for *let* (active versus permissive causation), with parallel confusions in prepositions and in spatial metaphors for time (file page 416). Unrelated substitutions are rare. Pinker infers early meanings were undifferentiated event gestalts or cluttered with situational detail, later reanalyzed into abstract notions like cause and change; only then can children extract broad-range lexical rules from conservatively learned verb pairs (file pages 416–417). Rough age correlations in Eva and Christy support but do not prove this (file page 417).

Pinker then sorts argument-structure errors into three sources: overapplication of a lexical rule (e.g., *Brush me my hair*), wrong-stem retrieval (*Keep me a favor*), and stable mislearned meanings, with many cases ambiguous (file pages 417–419). Hebrew and Hungarian causative errors, where morphology blocks simple input generalization, suggest stem–meaning mispairings (file pages 418–419). He estimates an unknown but nonzero proportion are stem intrusions.

A key representational claim follows: when a stem and a meaning diverge, the intended meaning usually determines argument structure (*Put Eva the yukky one first*, not *Put the yukky one into Eva*), contrary to views pairing verbs arbitrarily with frames (file page 420). Yet past-tense morphology tracks stems, not meanings: irregulars keep one past form across meanings, synonyms differ (*hit/hit, strike/struck, slap/slapped*), and children's overgeneralizations (*brang, bote*) follow sound, never semantic analogy (file pages 420–421). Pinker concludes lexical entries contain distinct kinds of information governing different generalizations. Finally, documented acquisition biases—sensitivity to manner of causation, undersensitivity to end state, and treating instruments or themes as primary patients—predict recurring errors like Christy's *Touch your toes… To what?* and Eva's *fill some salt into the bear* (file page 421).

**Engineering implications.** For a thinking machine, this suggests separating a concept's semantic features (cause, change, location, possession) from its surface form and from frame-selection logic, so that composition is driven by intended meaning while morphology or phonology can follow form. It motivates representing verb meanings as structured event decompositions rather than holistic labels, and constraining generalization by thematic fit rather than raw frequency. The developmental sequence—conservative use, then abstraction, then occasional overextension—offers a staged learning schedule. However, Pinker's evidence concerns narrow English verb acquisition, not universal learning laws; he explicitly flags causal claims as unproven without intervention. The text leaves unsolved how meanings are grounded perceptually, how abstract notions like cause are first acquired, and how memory, time, goals, or social cognition are represented. Nothing here demonstrates that a non-neural symbolic machine suffices for human intelligence or consciousness.
