**Subject and central argument.** This section continues Steven Pinker's "robot challenge": using the difficulty of engineering everyday capacities to argue that the mind contains complex, targeted machinery rather than a single master principle such as culture, learning, or self-organization (file page 15). The reasoning is by engineering analogy: if a robot designer finds sight, movement, common sense, motives, morality, and love each a "tour de force," then human ease at these tasks conceals specialized mechanisms (file pages 10–15).

**Perception and motor control.** Pinker notes that artificial shape recognizers handle pristine worlds—check digits printed by special equipment for template matching—while brains match stored records of faces, letters, animals, and tools to distorted retinal images (file page 10). He promises the mechanism in Chapter 4, so this excerpt does not explain it. Legs outperform wheels on rough terrain but demand control: unloading the foot, keeping the center of gravity within the support polygon, minimizing lurch; bipedal walking is "almost a recipe for disaster," and no one has figured out how we do it (file pages 10–11). Pointing an arm requires solving inverse kinematics and compensating for limb weight; the hand configures into named grips (hook, chuck, squeeze, disc, spherical), each needing precise muscle tensions, as when lifting a milk carton without dropping or crushing it (file page 11).

**Categorization and semantics.** An intelligent being must categorize to apply past knowledge, yet definitions fail: the "bachelor" cases (Arthur cohabiting, Bruce's sham marriage, teenage David, Eli and Edgar, polygynous Faisal, Father Gregory) show that a crisp necessary-and-sufficient definition misses ordinary intuitions (file pages 11–12). This is a limit on rule-based categorization, not a claim that categories are unlearnable.

**Common sense, rules, and the frame problem.** Common sense cannot be a downloadable database: tacit facts (a dog put in a car leaves the yard; a person's head travels with them; a living being alive at 9 and 5 was alive at noon) were never taught and cannot all be listed (file page 12). A smaller stock of core truths plus rules is needed, but rules misfire: two Freds are distinct while two Chicagos are one; milk in a bag in a car is in the car, but blood in a person in a car is not (file page 12). Acting requires computing side effects (not lighting a match near fuel), yet enumerating all side effects is fatal—Daniel Dennett's robot versions fail by ignoring, exhaustively deriving, or sorting relevance while a bomb ticks (file page 12). This "frame problem" emerged only when artificial intelligence tried to duplicate common sense (file pages 12–13).

**Goals, morality, emotion.** Motives must be engineered. Pinker endorses Isaac Asimov's insight that self-preservation does not emerge automatically and must be programmed, but questions the other laws: malice is not intrinsic to intelligence; mayhem comes from chaos or human-written viruses (file pages 13–14). Michael Frayn's Samaritan robots show that programmed self-sacrifice lacks discrimination or moral standing (file page 14). Love, too, is not a malfunction but involves "intricate calculations" of attraction, courtship, jealousy, and pair-bonding (file page 15).

**Limits and evidence.** Pinker concedes no one yet knows how bipedal balance works (file page 11) and that goodness is slippery to define (file page 14). He closes with neuropsychology: brain damage removes selected visual aspects—hemispatial neglect, achromatopsia with "grimpy, rat-colored" surfaces—implying separable analyzers (file page 15).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** The excerpt supports modular, inspectable mechanisms for recognition under distortion, grip selection, relevance filtering, and goal scheduling, but supplies no algorithms; the frame problem and category boundaries remain open.
