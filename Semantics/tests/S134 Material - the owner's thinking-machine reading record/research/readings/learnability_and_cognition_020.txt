This section (file pages 205–213, printed pages 184–192) closes Chapter 4 with evidence about "Haigspeak" — argument-structure forms that are deviant yet not plainly ungrammatical — and then opens Chapter 5 (file pages 214–215, printed pages 193–194) on lexical semantic representation.

**Source claims.** File pages 205–208 catalogue attested oddities: verbs used in dative or locative patterns outside their usual classes ("trained other students the complex procedures," "tutoring students musical improvisation," "fill it into the zucchini," "probe in electrodes," "rob the front wheels off some support bikes"), plus rare odd passives ("That was led up to by what happened"). Some locative-like forms are not products of the ordinary alternation at all, since the closest related form takes unexpected prepositions ("settled $125 million on her," "charged your Visa account with $300") (file pages 205–206).

Pinker argues these are not grounds for abandoning constraints on lexical rules: speakers confronted with their own utterances cringed or were incredulous (file page 208). Nor are they ordinary speech errors — many are written, uncorrected, and not derivable from a target by noise or decay (file pages 208–209). Following Chomsky, he treats grammaticality as multidimensional: Haigspeak violates narrow-range rules while obeying, or at least conforming to, a broad-range rule or its conflation class, which shows the broad-range rule is psychologically real (file page 209).

The central distinction, borrowed from Janet D. Fodor, is between property-predicting rules (what properties a form would have if it existed) and existence-predicting rules (which license adding a related form automatically). Pinker proposes that non-affixing broad-range rules are only property-predicting; only narrow-range rules predict existence. Haigspeak is thus heard as possible-but-not-English, an innovation (file page 209). Conflation classes alone cannot predict existence because they specify meaning but not sound; lexical rules supply the stem. Innovations like "enfant terrible'd gracefully" borrow stems unpredictably, and Clark and Clark (1979) show their role-mapping depends on speaker beliefs about hearer inference. Narrow-range lexical rules reduce this guesswork by dictating why a sound was chosen (file pages 209–210).

Why restrict existence-prediction? Pinker posits lexical conservatism: the default is not to add entries freely; productivity extends only to words minimally different in morphological type and meaning from attested ones — classwise rather than itemwise conservatism (file pages 210–211). He floats the possibility that the language faculty simply cannot "see" differences among fully productive verbs (file page 211). A monetary analogy illustrates why new tokens must be tightly controlled (file page 211). Two functional costs of unconstrained rules follow: rampant ambiguity among transitive/intransitive alternations (Atkins, Kegl, and Levin list six; Levin suggests a dozen), though real ambiguities exist ("Call me a taxi") (file pages 211–212); and indeterminate semantic composition, as with "Sam came Bill out in favor of Nixon" or the locative with-form's added state. Restricting rules to semantically cohesive subclasses yields one interpretation scheme per subclass (file pages 212–213). Basic words rest on arbitrary sound–meaning pairings learned by brute-force association (file page 213).

**Chapter 5 opening.** Baker's paradox can be resolved via semantic verb classes because children learn verb meanings anyway; the nonobvious discovery is that meaning distinctions correlate with syntactic privileges (file page 214). No simple feature like [±obligatory] decides class membership; the learner judges whether a meaning is "compatible" with a conflation class. Two hypotheses: Unrestricted Conceptual Representation (verb meanings are ordinary cognitive categories, similarity captured by semantic spaces, overlapping features, or microfeature vectors; grammar imposes no constraints) versus Grammatically Relevant Subsystem (a small set of elements like "causation" and "location" that alone trigger or block rules, as in "to butter") (file pages 214–215). Either fits the paradox's resolution, but the second is more satisfying; without it, meaning differences stay vague and learning obscure (file page 215).

**Limits.** Pinker concedes some Haigspeak may be dialectal; the currency analogy is tentative; the "cannot see differences" idea is called radical and deferred to later chapters; the microfeature options are illustrative, not endorsed.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, the property/existence split suggests two rule layers: a permissive checker that scores candidate structures against broad patterns, and a conservative generator that instantiates new forms only within tight semantic neighborhoods, with explicit stem provenance. A small privileged vocabulary of relations (causation, location) could anchor grounded representations and constrain composition, while broader world knowledge stays outside the rule engine. This is a design sketch, not evidence of human-like intelligence; the section says nothing about perception, memory, time, goals, analogy, creativity, emotion, or social cognition beyond inference about hearer knowledge.
