**Subject and central claims.** This section of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition* (file pages 195–204, printed pages 174–183) closes the analysis of argument-structure alternations and begins Section 4.5 on the relation between narrow-range and broad-range rules. Pinker argues that affixation cues learners to treat an argument-structure change as a productive broad-range operation: the affix carries the meaning change, and the stem is largely ignored (file page 195). Without affixation, as in *rise/raise* or *sit/set*, the learner instead treats the alternation as polysemy and analyzes verb meaning in detail (file pages 195–196). He concedes that "affixation" must be made precise — English irregulars like *hit/cut* passivize without overt affixes, and phonologically similar pairs may not involve genuine derivation — and refers this problem to Pinker and Prince (1988) rather than solving it (file page 196).

**Why the English passive is broad.** Pinker rejects a single "breadth parameter." He proposes that the passive's apparent range in English reflects the many rules creating patient-object verbs, so that the passive is fed eligible forms (file page 196). Functional pressures plausibly favored this: English lacks free constituent order, deletable subjects, and topic marking, so the passive focuses objects, permits the "evasive passive" ("mistakes were made"), and reduces memory load in relative clauses (file pages 196–197). Some Bantu languages, unable to question subjects, use an even less restrictive passive (file page 197). Historical evidence shows passivization spreading gradually in English — double-object passives from the thirteenth century, prepositional passives in the fourteenth–fifteenth, predicative in the fifteenth, complex verbs mainly in the eighteenth–nineteenth — with sampling caveats (file pages 197–198). The summary claim: the passive applies to any verb with a patient object, and its affix-plus-category-change makes the stem-plus-affix composition transparent (file page 198).

**Narrow versus broad rules.** For dative, locative, and causative alternations, the broad rule supplies necessary conditions (e.g., possession change for datives), while narrow conflation classes supply sufficient conditions (e.g., ballistic motion), excluding *She pulled John the suitcase* (file pages 198–199). For the passive, narrow rules play no role. Pinker asks whether broad rules could be eliminated in favor of lists of narrow rules; he notes the commonality and motivation arguments do not strictly require mental broad rules (file page 199). But he cites "Haigspeak" coinages — *weatherproofed*, *disappear him*, *last it*, *grown it* — that follow the broad causative rule while licensing no narrow rule, as evidence for on-line broad-range operation (file pages 199–201).

**Scope versus memorization.** The collected adult errors (causatives, anticausatives, middles, datives) show speakers extending rules beyond memorized subclasses, including forms the conventional broad dative rule cannot generate (*shoot me a look*, *strip him the ball*, *present you this trophy*), suggesting erosion or reanalysis of prepositional classes (file pages 201–204). Pinker marks uncertain judgments with "?" and notes some forms are marginal (file pages 201, 204).

**Limits.** The morphological analysis of genuine affixation is deferred (file page 196); historical causation is speculative ("could have arisen," "not surprising if"); and the section concerns English lexical rules, not universal learning laws.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests representing operations at two grains: a broad rule with necessary semantic conditions (patient object, possession change) plus a library of narrow sufficient-condition schemas, with a composition marker (stem-plus-affix) that licenses productive extension. A memory of attested forms should be separable from the rule's permitted scope, so novel coinages can be generated and flagged as unattested. The affix-versus-polysemy cue is a useful signal for deciding whether to attribute a change to a compositional operator or to reanalyze stored semantics. None of this demonstrates general intelligence or consciousness; it is a design pattern for constrained generalization grounded in semantic structure.
