**Subject and central claims.** The section opens by defending human statistical reasoning: when single-event probabilities are recast as frequencies, up to ninety-two percent of people reason correctly, including the HIV counseling case where two positive tests among ten thousand men imply roughly a fifty-fifty chance of infection (file page 203). Steven Pinker then follows Gigerenzer and von Mises in arguing that single-event probabilities are mathematically problematic, since any event belongs to infinitely many reference classes whose frequencies conflict and whose specificity shrinks sample size (file pages 203–204). The Simpson trial example shows reference-class choice matters: among batterers, one-tenth of one percent murder their wives, but among batterers whose wives are murdered, over half are the killers (file page 204). Pinker concludes that single-event claims are not precisely true or false but fallible inductive judgments weighed through evidence and argument (file page 204), and that people may interpret probability as "degree of belief warranted by the information just presented," a legal notion (file pages 204–205). He adds that probabilistic inference assumes a stable world; a crocodile attack justifies abandoning river statistics while a single car breakdown does not, so weighting recent anecdotes can be rational (file page 205). He concedes people do reason fallaciously and should learn statistics, but insists a species without instinct for probability could not learn or invent it (file page 205).

**The metaphorical mind.** Pinker then dissolves Wallace's paradox: the mind is not built for Western science but for mastering local environments, forming concepts that find "clumps in the correlational texture of the world," and wielding intuitive theories of objects, animate things, natural kinds, artifacts, minds, and social bonds plus inferential tools like logic, arithmetic, and probability (file page 205). Drawing on Ray Jackendoff, he shows spatial language extended to possession, change, and time: "the inheritance went to Fred," "the light went from green to red," "the meeting went from 3:00 to 4:00" (file pages 205–206). The point is not word-borrowing but borrowing inferential machinery: from "X went to Y" we infer X was not at Y before but is now, and the same inference transfers to ownership, though imperfectly, since inheritance is instantaneous while travel is gradual (file page 206). Concepts must therefore remain distinct while sharing rules. Grammatical constructions are co-opted too: the double-object construction for giving extends to telling, asking, writing (file pages 206–207). Leonard Talmy's force metaphors show sentences differing in felt dynamics: "John doesn't go out" versus "John can't go out," where desire and restraint are modeled as opposing forces (file pages 206–207). Pinker and other cognitive scientists infer that a handful of concepts—places, paths, motions, agency, causation—underlie tens of thousands of words across studied languages, forming the vocabulary and syntax of mentalese, a combinatorial language of thought vindicating Leibniz's "alphabet of human thoughts" (file page 207).

**Evolutionary proposal and evidence.** Pinker speculates that ancestral circuits for space and force were copied, severed from eyes and muscles, and bleached of physical reference, becoming scaffolds whose slots accept symbols for states, possessions, ideas, and desires, retaining computational abilities like tracking one state at a time or stronger overcoming weaker (file page 208). Evidence offered: chimpanzees manipulate objects, use symbols for going and placing, and David Premack showed they select the object causing a change, a knife or eraser, from before-and-after pictures (file page 208). Melissa Bowerman found preschoolers coin novel metaphors equating space with possession, time, and causation, such as "Friday is covering Saturday and Sunday," which they could not have inherited (file pages 208–209). Pinker argues these are not dead or literary metaphors; meetings as points in motion provoke no incongruity, suggesting models of space and force are "closer to the medium of thought itself" (file page 209). He suspects modules for time, animate beings, minds, and social relations were copied from intuitive physics (file page 209). The section closes with George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's conceptual metaphors: ARGUMENT IS WAR, VIRTUE IS UP, LOVE IS A PATIENT (file page 209).

**Engineering implications, kept separate.** For a thinking machine, this suggests representational primitives of location, path, force, agency, and causation, with distinct domains sharing inference rules rather than merging; a compositional combinator able to bind slots; frequency-based and reference-class-aware uncertainty handling with explicit stability assumptions; and analogical transfer that copies computational structure while blocking invalid inferences, such as gradual versus instantaneous transfer. Pinker's evolutionary story is a hypothesis about human origins, not a demonstration that any architecture suffices; the text supplies no learning algorithm, no proof that metaphor yields grounded semantics, and no account of memory, emotion, or social cognition beyond brief mentions of minds and social bonds as domains (file page 205). Child language data are narrow evidence about metaphor coinage, not universal learning laws. Nothing here establishes consciousness or general intelligence in a machine.
