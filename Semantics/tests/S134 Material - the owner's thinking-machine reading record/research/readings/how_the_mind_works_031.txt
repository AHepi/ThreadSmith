**Subject and central claims.** This section opens Chapter Five, "Good Ideas," on human reasoning. Steven Pinker frames it around Alfred Russel Wallace's paradox: human intelligence seems overbuilt for ancestral foraging life, so Wallace inferred guided development rather than natural selection (file page 175). Pinker's stated aim is to demote this from a worldview-shaking mystery to an ordinary research problem (file page 176). He agrees with Stephen Jay Gould that brains were exapted for novelties like calculus and chess, but criticizes Gould's factory-computer analogy: a payroll computer cannot play tic-tac-toe unless someone reprograms it, so co-option still demands an explanation of who elaborates what and why the original structure was suited to it (file page 176). Pinker argues Wallace erred empirically: foraging is harder than chess, all peoples have abstract words, foresight, and general reasoning, and everyone is an intuitive physicist, biologist, engineer, psychologist, and mathematician from the cradle (file page 176). Yet intuitive science diverges from institutional science — children say styrofoam weighs nothing, adults expect a ball leaving a spiral tube to keep spiraling (file page 176).

**Ecological intelligence.** Jean Piaget's "little scientists" analogy holds partly: children investigate and generalize (the boy concluding "Babies don't have penises" after watching a bath, file page 177). But selection shaped us to master local environments, not to publish. Michael Cole's Kpelle subjects, asked whether Yakpalo drinks rum when Flumo does, answer with concrete narrative ("Yakpalo went to his farm"), which Ulric Neisser calls sensible but not an answer to the formal question (file page 177). Schooling trains content-free inference — long division, logic, statistics transferable across domains — and students must feign ignorance, as with the geometry student forbidden to measure the triangle (file pages 177–178). Outside school, ignoring what you know is irrational. Ancestral problems (object recognition, toolmaking, language, mate-finding, navigation, animal prediction) recur; moon landings and Fermat's last theorem do not. Domain-specific "ecological rationality" (Leda Cosmides and John Tooby's term) exploits old regularities: slant-luminance cues serve shape, tone-of-voice cues serve mate fidelity, neither crosses over (file page 178). Knowledge is also costly: Pinker's bread experiment confounded water, yeast, and temperature; a factorial design would need eight days or more (file page 178). Finally, brains serve fitness, not truth; reliance on experts (Hilary Putnam cannot tell elm from beech yet knows they differ) invites manipulation by shamans and quacks, mitigated by peer review and open criticism (file pages 178–179). Donald Brown's survey found hereditary-caste civilizations produced myth but no accurate history, political science, or realistic portraiture (file page 199). Good science is pedantic, expensive, subversive — an unlikely selection pressure (file page 179).

**Categories for inference.** Pinker rejects textbook reasons for categorization: memory is not scarce (though combinatorial spaces are), and organization for its own sake is useless (Borges's absurd animal list, file page 179). Categories exist for inference: observe some properties, assign a category, predict unobserved ones (Mopsy the rabbit eats carrots; a cottontail also spreads tularemia, file page 180). Specificity trades off against identification cost, yielding Eleanor Rosch's basic-level categories ("rabbit," "car," "chair"), children's first labels (file page 180). Categories are not arbitrary conventions (contra deconstructionism): the world is "lumpy" because physics, natural selection, genetics, and anatomy cluster features — no finned cottontails (file page 180). Two kinds exist: fuzzy, stereotype-based categories (games, vegetables) fall out of pattern-associator neural networks recording correlations; rule-defined categories (odd numbers, females) ferret out the laws producing the clusters (file pages 180). "Grandmother" gets both treatments.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** A thinking machine needs both a correlational store (similarity-based generalization) and a rule system that models causal structure, plus a cost-bounded experimenter that decides when to isolate variables. It must support context-sensitive premise handling (Kpelle-style relevance) alongside formal modes, and an expert-consultation channel with credibility checks. Pinker's text does not supply algorithms, memory formats, or temporal representation; those remain open. The pattern-associator versus rules contrast is stated, not demonstrated here. No claims about consciousness are warranted.
