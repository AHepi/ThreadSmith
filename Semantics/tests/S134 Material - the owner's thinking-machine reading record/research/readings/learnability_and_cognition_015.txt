This section, from Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition*, continues Chapter 4's analysis of how verb meanings constrain grammatical rules, focusing on the dative alternation and opening the locative alternation. The printed page labels run 137 to 145.

**Source claims: the dative's thematic core.** Pinker argues that the double-object form retains a general tie to change of possession, extended metaphorically to benefaction and malefaction (file page 158). Evidence includes idioms like "Cry me a river" and "do you a favor," the benefactive/malefactive readings of "I had my leg broken," and Japanese give/receive benefactives (file page 158). He claims this "Thematic Relations Hypothesis" outperforms rivals: a purely benefactive core cannot explain why to-datives and most for-datives involve possession, while a purely literal-possession core cannot cover symbolic or reflexive benefactives (file page 159). He adds a cognitive motivation for which subclasses dativize: verbs like *throw* and *ask* inherently involve the recipient in defining the action, unlike *pull* or *shout*, so the receiver is more naturally a patient (file pages 159–160). He explicitly qualifies this: the learnability argument does not hinge on it, and he does not insist the distinction is perfectly predictive across dialects (file page 160).

**The morphological constraint.** A separate constraint restricts some dativizable subclasses to native stems: *tell*/*explain him the story*, *give*/*donate* (file page 160). Pinker's key claim is that the constraint is psychologically real but applies only to certain subclasses—giving, sending, instantaneous causation of motion, communication, creation, obtaining—while future-having (*bequeath*), malefactive (*envy*), and instrument-of-communication (*radio*) verbs ignore it (file pages 160–161). Apparent counterexamples thus reflect scope, not invalidity. He notes nondativizable classes like *shout* and *pull* remain cognitively compatible with possession, so semantics alone is not sufficient (file page 161).

**Why morphology matters.** The native/Latinate split governs many English processes: irregular past tenses, *in-* prefixation, comparatives, *-ion*, *-ity*, velar softening, particle *up* (file pages 161–162). Pinker proposes that argument-structure rules count as morphological rules even without overt affixes, so the dative can be sensitive to morphological class; other languages mark datives with affixes (file pages 163–164). He offers a learnability twist: in child-directed speech, prepositional datives were almost entirely native-stemmed (only *explain* appeared), so children would infer a morphological constraint even if English lacked one (file page 164). This is a narrow corpus finding about English input, not a universal learning law. Denominal verbs from names or neologisms (*fax*, *xerox*) escape the constraint, explaining mixed questionnaire results (file page 165). Pinker tentatively suggests Latinate verbs connote less direct action on a recipient (*donate* vs. *give*), but explicitly declines to push this (file pages 165–166).

**Locativizable verbs.** A verb must specify both motion and end state to alternate (*fill*, *pour* fail), but Pinker stresses this is necessary, not sufficient: *drip* vs. *raise* the question of why meanings lack components (file page 166). Only 34 of 142 English locative verbs alternate; finer-grained criteria are needed (file page 166).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests representing verb meaning as structured thematic roles plus permitted extensions (literal possession to metaphorical benefaction), with separate layers for semantic compatibility and morphological form class. A learner should treat a rule's scope as defeasible and class-indexed rather than absolute, and should model input statistics as evidence that can generate apparent constraints. The section leaves unsolved how meanings are grounded perceptually, how narrow classes are induced from experience, and how such lexical machinery composes into general reasoning, memory, or goal-directed action. Nothing here establishes general learning laws or non-neural sufficiency.
