**Subject and central claims.** This section of Chapter 6 concerns how children learn verb meanings, specifically the role of syntax-guided learning alongside conceptually driven mechanisms. Steven Pinker argues that universal linking rules can be applied backward: an object NP suggests an open patient slot in a verb's semantic structure, and recognizing argument positions triggers candidate semantic content (file page 325). For "She covered the bed with a blanket," the direct object licenses a patient slot, a known state change becomes an embedded effect, and the oblique "a blanket" can occupy a means substructure; transparent prepositions can supply places or paths (file pages 325–326).

**Conflation classes.** Pinker proposes a two-step procedure: reverse-linking first acquires conflation classes (incomplete semantic structures defining possible verb meanings in a language), which then guide learning of individual verbs (file page 326). A child with the English GO-THING-PATH-MANNER class hearing "The ball glipped into the room" can hypothesize that glip encodes manner of motion. Multiple classes may match one argument structure, so hypothesis testing and the Principle of Contrast must eliminate spurious ones (file page 326).

**Circularity and its brake.** Pinker explicitly flags a circularity: conflation classes presuppose correctly learned verb meanings. He calls it non-vicious if some verbs are learned via Hypothesis Testing or Event-Category Labeling, then classes are derived and reused (file pages 326–327). A Spanish child mislearning entrar as "float along a path" could propagate error to subir and bajar, but varied evidence would refute it (file page 327). Hypothesis testing is "the final arbiter," pruning semantic branches against the conceptual world; syntax merely guides and preselects hypotheses (file page 327).

**Syntactic bootstrapping.** Landau and Gleitman (1985) propose examining the full set of subcategorization frames for a stem; their blind subject learned see and look readily (file pages 327–328). Their example: see takes sentential complements (cognitive verb), directional phrases (abstract motion), but no imperative or pseudo-cleft (stative, nonagentive) (file page 328). Pinker finds this interesting but disputes its foundational status (file page 329).

**Pinker's disagreement.** He argues reverse linking and set-based inference are "mutually incompatible": components predictable from an argument structure are those *not* shared across a verb's forms (file pages 329–330). "Into" in "float into" adds movement, predictable from into, not from float (file page 330). Sewing illustrates the problem: dative implies transfer, "out of" implies creation, "onto" attachment, bare transitive affecting, bare intransitive activity — yet nothing linguistic reveals what unifies them as sewing (file page 330). In the limit, only the arbitrary lexical nugget remains (file pages 330–331).

**Residual value.** Two usable sources remain: cognitive riddle-solving across premises (e.g., "what activity… results in creation… for a beneficiary?" — sewing), which Pinker says he has no evidence children do, and which fails for single-frame verbs like die or devour (file page 331); and backward application of narrow-range lexical rules, though these presuppose independently learned verb meanings, so syntactic bootstrapping cannot be the "required" mechanism (file pages 331–332). Pinker concludes it is either cognitive problem solving or an adjunct (file page 332).

**Broad conflation classes.** Section 6.3 introduces thematic cores — semantic structures shared by semantically related words mapped by linking rules — and broad-range lexical rules mapping one core to another (file pages 332–333). These are constrained by linking rules and by attested verb meanings, contain only argument-linked material, and are built by "Conflation Class Skeleton-Building Through Reverse Linking," e.g., deriving dative cores from prepositional and double-object frames (file page 333). Multiple candidate skeletons are usually needed (file page 333).

**Engineering implications (ours, not Pinker's).** A thinking machine could implement: slot creation from argument positions; candidate semantic substructures (effect, means, path) attached to predicate frames; conflation-class induction from verified entries; hypothesis pruning against perceptual evidence; and contrast-based disambiguation. The permitted scope of a rule (e.g., which alternations a class licenses) must be distinguished from memorized verb entries; innate linking constraints are separate from acquired class definitions. Pinker's caution that syntax cannot ground meaning without prior conceptually grounded learning argues for a perceptually anchored semantic core before linguistic inference. Nothing here establishes general learning laws, consciousness, or non-neural sufficiency; the material is narrowly about verb semantics. No garbled or visually dependent content noted.
