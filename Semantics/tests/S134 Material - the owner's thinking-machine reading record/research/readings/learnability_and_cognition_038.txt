**Subject and central claims.** This section of Chapter 7, "Development," tests whether children's argument-structure overgeneralizations come from the same mechanism as adults' occasional novel forms ("Haigspeak"). Steven Pinker argues for a "parallel-tracks" account: an unchanged broad-range rule mapping semantic structures, plus narrow-range rules copied from individual verbs' lexicosemantic entries and refined as word meanings are refined. He rejects any process that would progressively split and narrow one broad rule into adult narrow rules, saying there is "no way to make sense of such a process" (file page 395, printed 374).

**Semantic constraints on errors.** Across four alternations, semantic constraints on children's rules are measurable at every age tested, show no age trend in effect size (one minor locative exception), and bound spontaneous errors within defined semantic limits "very close to what I have proposed for broad-range rules for adults" (file page 394, printed 373). Errors are not mere phrase-structure manipulations. Pinker treats this as a pillar of his solution to Baker's paradox: the alternations are inherently lexicosemantic, and the error-producing mechanism persists with little developmental change (file page 394). He notes the findings contradict many developmental psycholinguists' belief that generalizations arise from distributional analysis of surface input regularities (file page 394).

**Passives as a boundary case.** Pinker, Lebeaux, and Frost's data show children's passives are not all after-the-fact state descriptions, refuting stronger Horgan-Borer-Wexler claims; but every passive in Adam's sample involves a verb with a specific effect (an "effect" link to a GO substructure), while passives of actional verbs like slap, hit, touch, kiss, feel and of perception verbs are absent (file page 393, printed 372). Eve and Sarah's passives were all ambiguous between stative adjectives and verbal passives. Children are "nowhere near pushing the outside of the envelope" of adult passivizable verbs (file page 393).

**Conservatism dominates.** The theory permits only circumscribed productivity: broad-range rules as form-predicting constraints (except affix-adding rules) and extension of conservatively acquired argument-structure pairs to similar verbs in narrow conflation classes, against a "pervasive background tendency of conservatism" (file pages 395–396, printed 374–375). Teaching experiments show form-specific persistence: children reused modeled dative and transitive/intransitive forms more than alternates (file page 396). In spontaneous speech, productive double-object datives occurred about once per 4,111 sentences across five children; about 95% of double-object tokens and 86% of types could have been acquired conservatively from adult speech (file pages 397–398, printed 376–377). Locative errors were four clear cases in 86,000 sentences (file pages 398–399). Causative overgeneralizations were likewise rare, though Christy temporarily replaced bring, keep, leave with come, stay transitively (file pages 399–400). Passives are the exception: 25% of Adam's and 22% of Sarah's passives were not directly traceable to parental speech, though often only morphology, not argument structure, was clearly productive (file page 400, printed 379). Pinker concludes children are "wired to be very sensitive" to heard verb–argument-structure combinations and mostly stick to them (file page 400).

**Children's ambivalence.** If errors come from property-predicting but not existence-predicting rules, children should find their own errors odd, as adults do Haigspeak (file pages 400–401). Christy corrected and judged her own and others' causatives ill-formed from age 3;8 onward, yet 57% of her 62 novel causatives occurred during or after that awareness, through age 7;11 (file pages 401–402, printed 380–381). Hochberg's puppet judgment task found 78% of 3;4–3;10 and 92% of 4;1–5;5 children preferred correct lexical causatives, while errors persisted in that range (file page 402). Pinker concedes he cannot show all errors lack the grammar's "full seal of approval," since preschool metalinguistic ability is limited and errors blend into discourse (file page 401).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests separating a permissive generative mapping over structured event representations from item-specific learned licenses, with a default bias toward stored exemplars and a graded confidence or acceptability signal marking rule-generated outputs as provisional. Semantic role structure (theme, goal, effect, causation) should constrain generalization scope rather than surface word order. The persistence of errors despite explicit rejection is a useful design lesson: evaluation and generation can be dissociated channels. None of this demonstrates human-level intelligence or consciousness, and these are narrow language-acquisition findings, not universal learning laws.
