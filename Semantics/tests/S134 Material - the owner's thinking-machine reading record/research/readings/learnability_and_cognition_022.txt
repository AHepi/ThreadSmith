This section is Chapter 5 of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition*, pages 204–215 of the printed labels (file pages 225–236), continuing a taxonomy of verb meanings and then proposing a decompositional semantic representation designed to solve Baker's paradox: how children learn argument structure without direct evidence of what is ungrammatical.

**Source claims.** The section first inventories semantic dimensions verbs encode: manner of action or motion (punch versus slap, bounce versus roll), properties of actors or themes (drink requires liquid; German *essen* versus *fressen* distinguishes human from animal eaters), temporal distribution or aspect (hit as pointlike, run as boundariless, beat as iterative; Vendler's states, activities, accomplishments, achievements), purpose (chase, or "to knock the apple off the tree"), coreferentiality (comb another's hair versus one's own), and truth value or factivity (think versus doubt, be versus seem, witnessed versus hearsay) (file pages 225–226).

Pinker then sketches a theory close to Jackendoff's and Carter's, borrowing from Talmy, while explicitly criticizing alternatives: Generative Semantics wrongly treated meanings as syntactically transformed; psychological and computational theories such as Schank's target inference rather than linguistic data; Talmy and Rappaport and Levin are too informal for a learning theory; Jackendoff fails to separate broad property-predicting regularities from narrow existence-predicting ones, making it both too weak and too strong (file page 227). He concedes the framework has "rough edges."

The machinery: basic ontological categories (Thing, Event, State, Action, Place, Path, Property, Amount) combine through formation rules such as GO(THING, PATH) (file pages 227–228). Paths are defined by a reference object plus a path-function (to, from, toward, away-from, via); places by an object plus a place-function (in, on, under, near). English prepositions are only inexact mnemonics for these functions (file page 228). The Lexical Variable Principle requires that open arguments be complete conceptual constituents, ruling out verbs whose arguments are bare place-functions or partial motion structures (file page 229). Linking rules map themes to direct internal arguments and goals or locations to indirect ones; categorial correspondence rules map conceptual categories to phrasal categories (NPs usually Things, PPs Places and Paths, S and VP Events and States), yielding compositional sentence representations such as that for "John went into the room" (file pages 230–231).

Crucially, verbs like *enter* and *put* encode path and place information internally, so they need not list permitted prepositions; incompatible prepositions fail to "fuse" with the verb's structure (file pages 231–232). This predicts children's errors: they confuse semantically related prepositions (*scrunch from somebody*, *stay at the puppy*) but never omit the marker or use a semantically unrelated one, suggesting knowledge of argument types abstracted from specific words (file page 233). Idiosyncratic selections like *rely on* can be handled by specialized preposition entries rather than literal lists, avoiding "harmful and unnecessary descriptive power" (file page 233). Pinker stresses that open arguments as pointers to syntax, instantiated by fusion, is "a strong theoretical claim, not mere notation" (file page 233).

Manner is a daughter node of the event; its particular topography (rolling) is opaque to grammar, a "black box," while the existence of manner matters syntactically (file pages 233–234). Manner without path permits motion in place (the penguin spun in place); GO implies whole-object translation; combining them synthesizes local and global reference frames, as in "the bottle floated into the cave," which requires the cave to be on water (file pages 234–235). States use BE; GO can be stativized ("this road goes from Toledo to Columbus") and BE eventivized (STAY) (file page 235). Incorporated arguments (*berth*, *splash*) and selection restrictions arise from the same fusion machinery: conflicting conceptual information yields semantic anomaly (*the sand splashed*, *John elapsed*) (file page 236).

**Engineering implications.** For a thinking machine, this offers implementable primitives: typed conceptual constituents, formation rules, open-argument pointers, linking and correspondence rules, and a fusion operation that rejects inconsistent combinations. Such a system could in principle distinguish circles from squares only if perception supplies typed objects and properties; the text says nothing about perception, memory of past observations, temporal experience, causation beyond verb semantics, goals, reasoning, analogy, creativity, emotion, or social cognition. The "black box" for manner explicitly leaves perceptual representation unsolved. These are narrow language-learning proposals, not established universal learning laws, and nothing here demonstrates general intelligence or consciousness. Innate versus acquired status is left open: the categories and rules are presented as a theory of linguistic representation, not proven innate endowment.
