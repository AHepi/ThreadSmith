**Subject and central claims.** This section continues Steven Pinker’s account of intuitive physics and then extends it to animacy, natural kinds, and artifacts. He argues that infants as young as three to four months perceive enduring objects and expect continuity, cohesion, and contact, so the infant world is not William James’s “blooming, buzzing confusion” (file page 187). This early competence cannot be credited to manipulation, walking, or language, since infants barely reach or orient; instead it makes later learning possible (file pages 187–188). Yet infant physics is limited: babies tolerate hovering objects after slight contact, objects resting in midair, balls crossing holes, and wrong-corner outcomes, showing weak grasp of gravity and inertia (file page 187).

Adults are also non-Newtonian. College students, including physics students, often predict curved continuation after a ball leaves a curved tube or a tether is cut, invoking an “impetus” that dissipates; Leonard Talmy finds such conceptions in language, as when a ridge is said to keep a pencil on the table (file page 187). Perception fails further for spinning tops, rolling wheels, collisions, and displacement, where even professors err without equations (file pages 187–188). Pinker explains this by friction masking idealized laws and by perceptual systems handling roughly one variable at a time (file pages 187–188). Learning remains essential: sand, glue, balloons, boomerangs, and remotes supply idiosyncratic properties, but only because infants already carve the world into enduring objects with lawful motion (file page 188).

**Agents and minds.** Heider and Simmel’s three-dot film is seen as protagonist, hinderer, and helper pursuing a goal (file page 188). People treat self-propelled, goal-directed movers as animate agents with renewable “oomph,” distinct from intuitive physics, although scientifically animals obey physical laws (file page 188). Infants distinguish still faces from stopping objects, use pushing for objects and noise for people, expect collision-driven object motion but self-initiated human motion, and by twelve months interpret dots as goal-seekers (file pages 188–189).

**Natural kinds.** Folk biology classifies animals, plants, and minerals differently from artifacts and rule-defined kinds (file page 189). “Lion” survives changes of habitat, ferocity, appearance, and even family assignment, suggesting an unknown shared essence passed to offspring; gold’s atomic number is likewise not a definition (file pages 189–190). Essences explain form, growth, and vegetative processes, and folk taxonomies worldwide resemble Linnaean trees, overriding appearance to group frogs with tadpoles and reasoning about breeding (file pages 189–190). Darwin used common descent to explain such hierarchies but opposed extreme essentialism, since species are variable populations that can shade into intermediate forms (file page 190). Pinker objects to “essentialism” as a term of abuse, arguing that seeking what makes things work underlies chemistry, physiology, and genetics (file page 190). Children show essentialist intuitions: seven-year-olds judge a bleached, maned tiger still a tiger while five-year-olds say lion, yet preschoolers resist cross-domain transformations, privilege innards over appearance, and expect inherited traits (file page 190). Category membership guides inference: children told a flamingo is a bird infer that a blackbird feeds babies mashed food despite bat-like appearance (file page 191). They also attribute biological parts to function while understanding involuntary processes (file page 191). Parental speech rarely mentions innards or essences, so this is not simple instruction (file page 191).

**Artifacts.** Tool fascination appears around one year; by eighteen months children attend to contact, rigidity, and shape, and brain damage can dissociate naming of natural objects and artifacts (file page 191). An artifact is defined by suitability for an intended end, not shape or constitution: diverse chairs count as chairs because designed to support people, and a stump becomes one through use (file page 191). Artifact concepts mix mechanics and psychology, producing strange objects such as a book that is both ideas and physical mass, a rebuilt house counted as the same house, or a city treated as an object (file page 191).

**Engineering implications.** A thinking machine would need separate but interacting representational regimes: object files enforcing continuity, cohesion, and contact; an agent detector keyed to self-propelled, goal-directed motion; essentialist category nodes carrying hidden, inheritable properties; and artifact representations defined by intended function. Learning should attach material properties to these stable pegs rather than begin from undifferentiated traces. Inference should permit category-based projection, as in the blackbird example, while keeping appearance defeasible. The source does not specify algorithms, data structures, or how innate capacities arise, and its developmental findings are not universal learning laws. Nothing here demonstrates that an inspectable non-neural machine suffices for human intelligence or consciousness.
