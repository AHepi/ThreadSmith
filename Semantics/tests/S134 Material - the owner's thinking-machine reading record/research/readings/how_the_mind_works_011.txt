**Subject and central claims.** This section continues Steven Pinker’s account of how mental machinery could be grounded in neural hardware. It opens by dissolving the regress of explicit rules: no inference system follows rules “all the way down”; at some point a rule must simply be executed by brute-force operation, and a machine at that point obeys physics rather than interpreting further rules (file page 64). Pinker then builds upward from Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts’s toy neurons, which sum weighted inputs and compare the sum to a threshold (file page 64). Wired appropriately, these implement AND, OR, and NOT gates; wired together with extendable memory, such a network becomes a Turing machine, a full-powered computer (file page 64).

**Representation and compositionality.** Pinker argues that hard-wiring one unit per concept or proposition is “utterly impractical,” since every concept would need its own unit; instead concepts should be patterns over sets of units, as a byte represents a character (file pages 64–65). He then makes the code non-arbitrary: individual bits can stand for real features such as greenness, leafiness, crunchiness, with positive weights to a vegetable unit and negative weights for features vegetables lack (file page 65). Units can carry graded values rather than all-or-none states, so category membership becomes a matter of degree — celery near 1.0, garlic around 0.4, ketchup 0.1, a rock 0 (file page 65). Graded units also let a network accumulate evidence statistically, with a conclusion unit either thresholding (“beyond a reasonable doubt”) or rising gradually and leveling off (file page 65).

**Perception, memory, and constraint satisfaction.** Connecting every unit to every other yields auto-associators, which Pinker credits with five properties (file pages 65–68). First, they are content-addressable, reconstructive memories: partial cues such as greenness and crunchiness complete the rest of an item’s pattern, and one network stores many items in overlapping weights (file page 66). Second, they degrade gracefully, correcting “pritn” to “print” and “HELF” to “HELP,” unlike brittle address-based memory (file page 66). Third, they perform constraint satisfaction in parallel: ambiguous speech sounds are resolved by the interpretation violating the fewest constraints, and a Necker cube flips globally, dragging local interpretations with it (file pages 66–67).

**Generalization and its scope.** Fourth, a pattern associator generalizes: weights encode that feathered things tend to be warm-blooded, so facts about parrots transfer to budgies without any unit for “parrot” (file pages 67–68). Subsets of units implicitly define classes of varying inclusiveness — six features pick out dogs, three pick out mammals, two pick out animals (file page 68). This is generalization by shared features, not memorization of instances.

**Learning and its limits.** Fifth, weights can be learned from examples by a teacher-supplied correction rule that nudges weights and thresholds up or down (file page 68). But Pinker stresses a hard limit: perceptrons, which merely sum weighted inputs, cannot solve exclusive-or, parity, symmetry, or simple addition (file page 68). The remedy is an internal layer of hidden units computing intermediate products, so the output unit only has to combine them (file page 68). The excerpt cuts off mid-sentence here.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker’s).** For a thinking machine, the section supports feature-based distributed representations, graded activation, content-addressable recall, parallel constraint satisfaction, and hidden-layer recoding as mechanisms for perception, memory, and generalization. It does not supply semantics, temporal memory, causation, goals, analogy, creativity, emotion, or social cognition; those remain open. Pinker’s account is descriptive of brains and models, not a proof that an inspectable non-neural machine suffices. The exclusive-or result concerns a narrow architecture, not a universal law of learning.
