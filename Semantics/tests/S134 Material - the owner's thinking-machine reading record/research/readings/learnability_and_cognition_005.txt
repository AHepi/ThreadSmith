This section of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition* continues Chapter 1's treatment of Baker's paradox: how children productively generalize verb alternations without overgeneralizing, given that they receive little direct correction. The section's subject is Solution #3, the attempt to use syntactic representations of argument structure as criteria delimiting which verbs a lexical rule may apply to (file pages 55–56).

Pinker's central move is to reject strict lexical conservatism, the hypothesis that children merely record which verbs appear in which argument structures and stick to attested combinations, because studies show children do produce productive forms (file page 55, table 1.20). He also rejects solutions appealing to negative evidence and those denying productivity. The remaining option is rejecting arbitrariness: if a verb's possible argument structures can be predicted from other information in its lexical entry, learners could generalize an alternation to verbs meeting the criteria while withholding it from others (file pages 55–56). Any tier of information—syntactic, morphological, semantic, phonological, pragmatic—might serve as the criterion; this section tests syntax, deferring morphology and semantics to later chapters (file page 56).

Pinker reviews how argument structures are represented. Classical notation lists phrasal categories accompanying the verb, but is treated as a mnemonic rather than a mental-representation hypothesis (file pages 56–57). Lexical Functional Grammar notation pairs grammatical functions like subject, object, and oblique with thematic roles such as agent, theme, and location (file page 57). The Thematic Relations Hypothesis extends these roles metaphorically to abstract change and possession, motivated by parallelisms like "John went from Chicago to Boston" versus "John went from being sick to being well" (file pages 57–58). Government-Binding theory instead locates arguments in abstract phrase-structure positions, distinguishing external from internal arguments and, further, direct from indirect internal arguments (file pages 58–59). Pinker notes these frameworks are largely intertranslatable, with the main substantive difference being the Government-Binding distinction between external subjects and moved internal subjects (file pages 60–61).

The critical argument is that abstract syntactic representations cannot by themselves solve the learning problem. Saying one verb alternates because it has representation A while another does not because it has representation B merely pushes the question back: how does the child learn which verbs have which representation (file page 62)? Without an answer, such accounts are no better than stipulating a feature like plus-or-minus dativizable. Two answers are possible. If a morphological, phonological, or semantic property predicts the representation, the account reduces to the non-syntactic solutions Pinker examines later, and the configurations play no direct role in solving the paradox (file pages 62–63). Alternatively, the representations could have independent syntactic effects—for instance, all verbs alternating between structures X and Y also appearing in structure Z. This is logically capable of resolving the paradox, especially if Z predicts non-alternation, but Pinker finds it implausible that the factors blocking X-to-Y would vanish entirely for X-to-Z (file page 63).

He then tests Janet Randall's proposal that dativizable verbs have two obligatory internal arguments while nondativizable verbs have an optional goal. On this account, hearing a simple transitive use like "Connie reported the news" would let the child deduce non-dativizability from positive evidence alone (file pages 63–64). Pinker shows the correlation fails: "Bill told a story," "Sam asked a question," "Irv wrote a letter," and "John threw the ball" are grammatical yet dativizable, while nondativizable verbs like "entrust" and "credit" have obligatory to-phrases (file page 65). Locative alternation data likewise show all combinations of optional and obligatory arguments among alternating and nonalternating verbs (file page 65).

For a thinking machine, the engineering relevance is a design principle: a rule's permitted scope must be grounded in independently detectable properties of representations, not stipulated class labels, and the learner must be able to acquire those properties from available input. Pinker's argument that abstract structure alone defers rather than solves the problem cautions against architectures that encode competence without a learning path. However, the section leaves unsolved which property—likely semantic, as later chapters argue—actually delimits generalization, and it addresses narrow language-learning phenomena, not universal learning laws. Topics like memory, time, causation, goals, analogy, creativity, emotions, and social cognition are not developed here.
