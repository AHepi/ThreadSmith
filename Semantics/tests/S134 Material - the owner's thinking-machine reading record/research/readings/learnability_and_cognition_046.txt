No new theory is argued here, but the notes qualify earlier claims.

**Representation and semantics.** Suppressed arguments carry typed content: "X ate" means X ate something intended for eating, parallel to unspecified-object deletion, and is encoded by appending a property to the suppressed object substructure via a "for/to" operator next to a replica of the action structure (file page 472). ACTs in the efficacy field are ambiguous between a state asserting that one entity is responsible for another's circumstance and an event in which one entity exerts causal efficacy to produce a state change; this yields the ambiguity of "Snow covered the ground" (file page 472). The same field is proposed for whole–part relations ("Canada has ten provinces"), whose verbs resist passivization (file page 472).

**Compositionality and permitted scope.** Rules operate over semantic structures, not strings: broad-range and narrow-range rules are assumed to include aspectual or time-line representations, perhaps only a coarse point/region distinction at the broad level (file page 472). Where two verb forms are linked by cognitive inference rather than shared symbols — inserting contents against resistance versus the container being overfull — rule creation might have to include the premises needed to derive the second form, not just coindexed parts (file page 473). This is flagged as unresolved: it is unclear whether common symbols suffice (file page 473).

**Perception and learning.** Many closed-class morphemes, such as gender or declension markers, have no perceptual correlate, so their acquisition needs a separate account (file page 472). Verbs like "butter" make idiosyncratic reference properties morphologically transparent via similarity to a related noun (file page 472). A statistical worry: children who hear both forms of an alternation may sample non-randomly, and a clustering procedure could wrongly exclude unattested verbs, blocking productive extension (file page 472).

**Constraints on generalization.** Object properties of locative verbs are argued to attach to the theme in the GO structure, not the patient in the ACT structure, because shape and material interlock with path and destination (loading, spraying) (file pages 472–473). This is a motivated constraint, not a memorized list. Children's overgeneralization of the broad-range rule to BE and HAVE is attributed to indeterminacy in Reverse Linking, later narrowed by Lexical Abstraction (file page 474) — an explicit case of a rule's scope being revised toward the adult grammar. Iterated causation is not obviously blocked by syntax; proposed filters (no third object in LFG, case problems in GB) are called ad hoc and not clearly extendable (file page 474).

**Causation, time, goals.** Causative experiments are qualified: permissive-causation pictures were never chosen for "shake," and for "spin" only 42–46%, plausibly because a globe keeps spinning after the cause ends (file page 473). Novel intransitives challenge unidirectional causativization but are orthogonal to whether rules add a "cause" superstructure or an external argument (file page 474). Aspect is treated as the interface between lexical semantics and syntax, consistent with Tenny (file page 473).

**Memory and evidence.** Diary estimates run to about 1.4 million utterances for one child and 0.96 million for another (file pages 474–475); errors were rarely corrected (file page 475), and one child's prepositional dative usage mirrored adult input at 79% type overlap (file page 474). A child's "Are we buying money now?" shows analogy from observed transactions (file page 475). "Make" and "give" are not interchanged, supporting location as the base domain extended to possession and state (file page 475).

**Engineering implications (ours, not Pinker's).** A machine could implement typed substructures with suppressed-argument defaults, state/event disambiguation tags, and theme-anchored property attachment; rule induction should record inferential premises and permit scope revision. None of this is shown sufficient for general intelligence, and the notes concern English verb learning only.
