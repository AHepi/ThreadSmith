**Subject and central argument.** This section defends the adaptationist program in biology and then turns to why nervous systems evolved. Steven Pinker argues that organized complexity in organisms is best explained as serving survival and reproduction, and that even nonadaptive by-products are found only during a search for adaptations (file page 101, printed label 167). He concedes real constraints: natural selection lacks foresight, is limited by ancestry and by what protein machinery can grow, and birds could not evolve propellers (file page 102, printed label 168). But he rejects the stronger claim that animals are badly designed, citing cheetah biomechanics, bat sonar, spider silk, and DNA repair, and correcting the folklore that penguins' wings are useless — they fly underwater (file page 101).

**Constraints versus selection.** Pinker calls "selection versus constraints" a phony dichotomy, like innateness versus learning (file page 102, printed label 169). Developmental constraints rule out broad classes of options but cannot force a functioning organ into existence; without selection, growing tissue would tend toward scar tissue or amorphous mass rather than working organs. He insists the question "why this organ?" is meaningless without a "compared-to-what": wings rather than propellers reflect growability limits; wings rather than forelegs reflect selection for flight (file page 102).

**Change of function.** A changed function does not refute selection. Darwin's examples include fish pectoral fins becoming whale flippers and bird wings; mammalian middle-ear bones began as reptile jaw hinges that also transmitted ground vibrations, later specializing (file pages 102–103, printed labels 169–170). Partial eyes give partial sight. Kingsolver and Koehl's insect-wing work is presented as a triumph of adaptationism: proto-wings too small for flight worked as adjustable heat exchangers, with diminishing returns at sizes where flight usefulness begins, so selection could favor enlargement continuously (file page 103, printed label 171). Pinker notes paleontologists disagree about which wing intermediate the fossil record best supports (file page 103). He treats Gould's "exaptation" as relabeling an old organ's new use, not a replacement for selection, and argues a complex organ is rarely usable "out of the box" for a comparably difficult new job without retrofitting (file page 103, printed label 172).

**What counts as design.** Answering Noam Chomsky's charge of circularity (file pages 103–104), Pinker says selection explains *improbable* usefulness, not mere usefulness: positive mass is cheap, image-forming eyes are rare. He offers explicit criteria for design: heterogeneous parts plus unity of function, where the function can be stated more economically than the structure (lens, diaphragm, photopigment all serve image formation) (file page 104, printed label 174). The hypothesis is falsifiable: designed things must descend from replicators and serve reproduction — Darwin said evolved horse saddles would refute the theory (file page 104). He recounts a spider exhibition where he and a woman agreed on the facts needing explanation but disagreed on the explanation (file page 104).

**Transition to mind.** Pinker concludes that organisms are interactions of adaptations, by-products, and noise, distinguished by reverse engineering (file page 105, printed label 175). He extends this to human intelligence: mental faculties show selection's handiwork, but not every aspect — neural sluggishness, art, music, religion, dreams — is an adaptation. Understanding the mind requires meshing with how it evolved.

**The value of information.** Under "The Blind Programmer," Pinker argues brains evolved because information has economic value: life is choosing among gambles. His lottery example shows information about which ticket pays better is worth up to four cents per play (file page 105, printed label 176). Organisms should "pay" for information in tissue, energy, and time when the expected payoff in food, safety, or mating exceeds the cost, valuated ultimately in surviving offspring. More information often helps up to diminishing returns (treasure-hunt coordinates, combination locks), explaining more complex nervous systems. Crucially, selection cannot endow an organism directly with environmental information or with representations, modules, or mental organs; it selects genes, and genes build brains whose assembly processes differ (file page 105).

**Engineering implications (ours, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this section supplies a design *rationale* — process information to guide choices under uncertainty, and treat representations as structures whose heterogeneous parts serve a joint function — but no representational format, learning algorithm, or composition mechanism. The "compared-to-what" discipline and the distinction between permitted scope (constraints) and favored design (selection) suggest separating a system's hypothesis space from the criterion that ranks hypotheses. The lottery analysis motivates explicit value-of-information computations. The text says nothing here about perception, memory, time, causation, analogy, or social cognition; those remain unsolved.
