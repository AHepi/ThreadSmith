**Subject and central claims.** This section of Chapter 7, "Development," continues Steven Pinker's explanation of why children's speech differs from adults' in verb argument structure. File page 403 (printed 382) reports that older children preferred the grammatical periphrastic causative 68% of the time for verbs like *sing* and *dance*, while younger children performed at chance (52%). Pinker attributes part of this to a parsing confound: periphrastic causatives embed a clause, which English 3- and 4-year-olds comprehend only about 70% of the time, whereas single-clause causatives in other languages are acted out nearly perfectly by age 4 (file page 403). His summary argues that children's errors are rare, occur at ages when children can judge such sentences ungrammatical, and resemble adults' occasional conscious bending of language — evidence that children share adults' simple broad-range rule rather than needing a useless rule-complication procedure (file pages 403–404, printed 382–383).

**Errors from meanings, not rules.** Section 7.5 argues that some errors cannot be one-shot innovations, since ungrammatical forms displaced correct ones (Christy's *come*/*stay* for *bring*/*leave*; wrong objects for *touch* and *fill*), and Bowerman's example shows overextension in comprehension: "Touch your toes" → "To what?" (file page 404, printed 383). Pinker's thematic core theory holds that lexical rules manipulate a verb's lexicosemantic structure, with alternations licensed by cognitive compatibility (broad-range rules) or detailed semantic correspondence (narrow-range rules). If a child's rule is correct but the verb meaning is wrong — say *fill* structured like *pour* — errors look like bad rules but are really "malapropisms with syntactic consequences," requiring no rule change (file pages 404–405, printed 383–384).

**Acquired meanings, innate cores.** Slobin's cross-linguistic "scenes" (manipulative activity; figure-ground) match the theory's ACT-THING-THING and GO-THING-PATH substructures; children respect agency, motion/change, and the point/region time distinction early (file pages 405–406, printed 384–385). But specific conflations must be learned: Gentner's noun–verb asymmetry (verbs later and slower, not explained by frequency or other confounds) follows because verb meanings are perceptually arbitrary and language-variable, requiring Semantic Structure Hypothesis Testing over many situations (file pages 406–407, printed 385–386). Gentner's transfer verbs show underspecification: success tracked semantic complexity (*give*/*take* best, then *pay*/*trade*, then *buy*/*sell*/*spend*), with children omitting countertransfer or money restrictions — *buy* acted as *take*, *sell* as *give* (file page 407). Ammon's picture tasks show insensitivity to proximal versus ballistic motion (*hand*/*throw*) and to object-type restrictions (file pages 407–408). Overspecification also occurs: early *put* and *take* were tied to narrow contexts like donning or removing clothing (file page 408, printed 387).

**Biases and substitutions.** Gentner found children encode manner of motion but neglect end-state: 5-to-7-year-olds matched manner verbs 97% correctly but applied *mix* indiscriminately (48% versus 46% appropriate), though they knew *mix* as an action verb by age 3½ (file page 409, printed 388). Manner is conflated with path in errors like "laughed all the way down the hill" and Spanish *correr abajo* (file page 410, printed 389). Menyuk and Bowerman document substitutions (*put* for *give*, *let* for *make*, *take* for *bring*, etc.), flouting distinctions of transfer type, causation type, and deictic path (file pages 410–412, printed 389–391). Pinker stresses threefold indeterminacy: an error may reflect a distorted meaning with a correct rule, a correct meaning with an overly broad rule, or both (file page 412).

**Limits.** The section concedes younger children's chance performance resists the picture, and admits many error mechanisms cannot be uniquely identified (file pages 403, 412). These are narrow English- and cross-linguistic acquisition findings, not proven universal learning laws.

**Engineering implications (ours, not Pinker's).** A thinking machine could separate a fixed compositional semantic algebra (agent, patient, path, time tiers) from learned lexical entries accumulated across situations, with permitted alternation scope checked against semantic compatibility rather than memorized frames. Representations should support partial meanings (missing or extra components) so errors are diagnosable as semantic rather than procedural. Perception must supply event gestalts (manipulation, figure-ground, punctual versus ongoing) as primitives; memory should retain episodic contexts to constrain generalization; a parser must handle embedded clauses before judging grammaticality. None of this demonstrates human-level intelligence or consciousness.
