This section of *Learnability and Cognition* (file pages 383–392, printed pages 362–371) continues Chapter 7's argument that children's errors in argument-structure alternations are constrained by thematic semantics rather than by surface syntax alone. The material is not bibliography or index.

**Source claims: thematic cores and causatives.** Steven Pinker argues that argument-structure rules map among "thematic cores," which have independent identity in the lexicon as definitions of possible verb meanings (file page 383). Children's productive double-object errors involve only verbs whose adult class denotes literal or metaphorical change of possession, and few could arise by dativization (file page 383). Cross-linguistic evidence from Nedyalkov and Silnitsky (1973) shows that residual causative ditransitives cluster around psychological verbs and giving-for-consumption, matching English children's overgeneralizations; Pinker treats this convergence as support for language acquisition as hypothesis testing, noting the children entertain a hypothesis correct in other languages but not English (file pages 383–384).

**Datives.** In Gropen et al. (1989), children produced double-object forms most often when the recipient was themselves (52%), less for a toy animal (37%), least for an inanimate place (32%), with significant differences; the pattern held across ages 5;8–7;6 and 7;6–8;11 with no age increase in the effect (file page 384). Spontaneous errors (file pages 384–386) involve literal possession change (including communication of a message), benefactive uses (brush, fix, open, etc.) treated as metaphorical prospective possession, and malefactive uses ("You ate me my cracker") as prospective loss. Pinker anticipates the objection that possession can be "shoehorned" anywhere and answers it with counterexamples (7.11–7.12) that a purely syntactic rule would license but children never produce (file pages 386–388). A rare misanalysis ("That's you some tea") shows errors are possible but not of the predicted syntactic type (file pages 387–388).

**Locatives.** Gropen, Pinker, and Goldberg tested a novel verb ("pilking") where the goal becomes surface object only if it changes state; distinctive manner instead makes the moving thing the object (file page 388). Even 3-year-olds showed sensitivity (effect 23.5 points), and ages 5, 7, and adults showed 47, 42, and 45.5 points with no consistent age trend (file page 389). Bowerman found locative errors only for physical figure–ground motion, never for syntactically parallel communication, perception, or instrumental relations (file pages 389–390), and Pinker lists further impossible alternations (7.13–7.14).

**Passives.** Pinker, Lebeaux, and Frost (1987) used a Relative Passivizability Index (a difference between differences) to separate passive difficulty from generalization difficulty (file pages 390–391). Across five datasets, verb semantics affected productive passivizability in the predicted direction in the younger group in all cases, with mixed age trends (file page 392). Children may respect the passive's broad constraints even more than adults, possibly requiring a concrete change of state; Horgan (1978) describes child passives as "after-the-fact" observations, and Borer and Wexler (1987) argue only adjectival passives exist for preschoolers (file page 392).

**Limits.** The evidence concerns argument-structure alternations in English and a few languages, not universal learning laws. Pinker's hypothesis-testing framing is a proposal, not proof of innate cores; the text distinguishes adult broad-range rules (permitted scope) from children's memorized forms but does not fully resolve innateness.

**Engineering implications (ours, not Pinker's).** A thinking machine could implement thematic cores as typed semantic frames (possession, causation, motion, state change) that constrain which argument mappings a rule may license, with a permitted-scope check that rejects surface-syntactic analogies lacking the right thematic structure. Novel-verb experiments suggest learning mechanisms that bind observed event features (manner, end state, affected entity) to argument roles, while counterexample sets (7.11–7.14) provide negative constraints. This is a design sketch, not evidence of human-like intelligence or consciousness.
