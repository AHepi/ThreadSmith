**Subject and central claims.** This section of chapter 4, "Possible and Actual Forms," addresses Baker's paradox: how children learn which verbs may appear in which argument structures without hearing every verb in every structure. Steven Pinker argues that semantic criteria derived from argument-structure alternations are only necessary, not sufficient, conditions for a verb to alternate (file page 139). He rejects the idea that a verb's licensing meaning can be recovered from full sentences, since that would collapse learning back into conservatism — the child would have to hear each verb in each structure first (file page 138). He also rejects pure semantic "compatibility" as a solution: semantic intuitions are weaker than grammaticality judgments, and dialect differences (Georgia Green's grammatical "shout him the answer" and "carry him the box") show that knowing a verb's meaning does not fix its syntax (file page 139).

**The proposed solution: narrow conflation classes.** Language must map flexible cognition onto rigid syntax. Perception permits many construals of one event — a stick hitting a wall can be analyzed as affecting the wall or the stick; pouring water can affect water or glass (file page 140). Left to speakers, such ambiguity would defeat linking rules. So grammar "guards" verb meanings: reconstrual is permitted only for narrow, semi-arbitrary classes defined by independent semantic features (file page 141). Instantaneous imparting of force causing ballistic motion (throw, toss, kick, slide, roll, bounce) licenses the double-object dative ("She threw him the puck"), while continuous guided motion (carry, pull, push, schlep) does not, despite being cognitively construable as change of possession (file pages 141–142). Broad-range rules define broad conflation classes (necessary conditions); narrow-range rules pick out subclasses whose membership is sufficient for alternation (file page 142).

**Evidence from transitive action verbs.** Following Beth Levin (1985) and Laughren, Levin, and Rappaport (1986), Pinker shows that even simple action verbs split into subclasses with distinct syntactic privileges (file page 143). The conative alternation ("cut at the bread") requires motion resulting in contact: cutting and hitting verbs participate; touching and breaking verbs do not (file pages 143–144). Part-possessor ascension ("cut Brian on the arm") requires contact (file page 144). The contact locative ("hit the bat against the wall") requires motion plus contact without a specific effect (file pages 144–145). The middle ("This bread cuts easily") requires an effect (file page 145). The anticausative ("The glass broke") requires an effect with no specified cause, contact, or motion (file page 145). A summary table maps hit, cut, break, and touch to semantic elements (motion, contact, effect) (file page 146).

**Key representational insight.** Class membership depends not on characteristic features of real events but on which aspects a verb's semantic structure constrains (file page 146). Verb meanings are not scripts, frames, stereotypes, or similarity clusters; those capture typical features, whereas verb semantics impose necessary conditions and are mute about the rest (file pages 146–147). Typicality is irrelevant: kissing usually involves approach and contact, but "kiss" does not require prior motion; "cut" requires motion causing the effect, so magically splitting bread by heating is not cutting (file page 147).

**Limits and disagreements.** Pinker concedes the semantic-compatibility story has some truth but cannot solve the paradox (file page 138). He notes the analysis is provisional: narrow classes may be defined by grammatically relevant semantic subsets and "perhaps" morphological divisions (file page 142). The text ends mid-sentence on "break" (file page 147), so the argument continues beyond this excerpt.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests separating constrained semantic features (what a representation obligatorily specifies) from default or inferred event knowledge (what is typical), and gating compositional operations on the former. Permitted scope of a rule should be a narrow feature class, not memorized verb lists or holistic similarity. Innate machinery would be the linking rules and feature vocabulary; acquired knowledge would be each verb's feature decomposition. This is a language-learning result, not a universal learning law. Topics like memory, time, causation, goals, analogy, creativity, emotion, and social cognition are largely absent here beyond brief causal examples.
