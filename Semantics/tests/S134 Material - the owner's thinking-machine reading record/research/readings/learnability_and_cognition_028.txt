This section of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition* continues Chapter 5's program of representing verb meanings as structured semantic fields built from primitives such as ACT, BE, GO, PATH, PLACE, PROPERTY, and THING, linked by causal and force-dynamic relations. The subject is how verb semantics encode spatial configurations, changes of state, and causal structure, and how these representations explain syntactic alternations like the locative and passive.

**Source claims: representation and compositionality.** Pinker represents verb meanings compositionally from reusable parts. For *pile*, the theme must be an aggregate with one-dimensional extension and a vertical GO path, yielding the prediction that *Irma piled books on the table* can describe placing prearranged piles (file page 294). For *cover*, the location object changes state to have a two-dimensional object coextensive with its surface; the place-function "on" here means surface coextensiveness, not top-of, and the 2D property permits construal rather than literal dimensionality (file pages 294–295). No specific path, manner, or even translation of the object is required—one can cover a bed with already-strewn sheets (file page 295). Classes like *fill*, *line*, *saturate*, and *riddle* are differentiated by dimensionalities of content and container plus place-functions, with idiosyncratic properties distinguishing individual verbs (file page 296).

**Goals and causation.** *Load*, *pack*, and *stock* encode a container's intended capacity and purposive state: loading a gun requires the designed receptacle, and a drug dealer slipping cocaine into a suitcase is not *packing* it (file page 296). Pinker captures this with a "for/to" property operator and an opaque "as designed" manner specification (file page 297). The *into* form derived from the *with* form drops the change-of-state as an asserted effect, so *Bob loaded hay into the wagon* need not entail completion (file page 297).

**Explicit limits.** The *stuff* class (*cram*, *crowd*, *jam*) exposes a gap: "overfull" is not a place or path, and no English preposition expresses it; the theme's quantity relative to container capacity must be represented, which Jackendoff's formalism cannot do (file pages 297–298). Pinker proposes an extension—CONFIGURATION constituents with functions like "over-"—but marks this as a natural extension, not established machinery (file page 298). The derivation between "despite" (force resistance) and "over-" configurations would require conceptual inference, not mechanical rearrangement (file pages 299–300). For *adorn*-type verbs, whether an "eval" property is needed is explicitly unclear (file page 300).

**Passive and reasoning scope.** Passivization is a broad-range rule applying to any verb with a dyadic ACT, which explains why action verbs, psych-verbs, and efficacy verbs like *cut* and *cover* passivize while BE/GO-based verbs do not (file pages 302–304). Agentless passives still entail an agent, licensing *deliberately* (file page 302).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** These representations suggest a machine architecture with typed semantic frames, dimensional and force-dynamic primitives, and linking rules mapping semantics to syntax. The permitted scope of a rule (any dyadic ACT) is distinguished from memorized verb lists. However, Pinker addresses linguistic representation, not perception, memory over time, learning algorithms, or general intelligence; innate versus acquired status is not resolved here. The diagrams (5.60–5.68) are visually structured and partially garbled in text form, so exact topology is uncertain. Narrow verb-class results should not be read as universal learning laws.
