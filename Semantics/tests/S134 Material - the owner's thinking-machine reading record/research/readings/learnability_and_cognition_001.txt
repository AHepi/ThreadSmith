Subject and scope. Pinker calls the book "a technical book on how people learn, understand, and use verbs in sentences" (file page 14). Its motivating problem is how children master contrasting verbs such as fill and pour, give and carry, which forced him to consider "the human concepts of causation, space, time, matter, and purpose"; syntax interacts with "the stuff of thought" (file page 14). The contents confirm the architecture: a learnability paradox, constraints on lexical rules, argument structure, possible versus actual forms, representation, learning, development, and conclusions (file pages 8–10).

Central claims visible here. The book's "major claim" is that "every argument structure chooses its verbs from a set of narrow-range classes, each with a meaning that is conceptually compatible with it (if only metaphorically)" (file page 17). A "thematic core" delineates the "conflation class" of verbs compatible with an argument structure, close to construction grammar's "construction meaning" (file page 16). Pinker argues lexical rules are still needed beyond meaning-to-construction mappings, because they govern whether a morphological root may be reused intact across related meanings, must be modified, or is ineligible: Hand me the book versus Hand the book to me; He will hand the book over to me; She killed/*died the spider (file page 16). This is a rule's permitted scope, distinct from memorized forms.

Representation and semantics. The contents promise a theory of lexicosemantic representation, a cross-linguistic inventory of components of verb meaning, and explicit representations of lexical rules and structures (file page 9). Pinker treats conceptual structure as the solution to a verb-learning paradox and defends it elsewhere against Fodor's atomic innate meanings, radical pragmatics, and linguistic determinism (file page 15). He stresses the autonomy of semantic representation and spatial schemas for abstract thought (file page 10).

Learning, development, innate versus acquired. The series foregrounds "the nature and limits of learning and maturation," initial structures, and "domain-specific constraints on development" (file page 12). Contents sections cover linking rules, learning mechanisms, developmental sequences, an unlearning problem, and children's semantically conditioned errors (file page 9). Pinker claims priority for documenting children's mostly conservative verb use (section 7.4.1), while noting it is often attributed to Michael Tomasello (file page 17). Acknowledged disagreements: Melissa Bowerman doubts constraints resolve the paradox; Lila Gleitman questions how much meaning children infer from situations; Jane Grimshaw and Janet Randall warn about grammatical constraints; Janet Fodor doubts rule productivity; Kenneth Wexler questions fixed acquisition mechanisms (file pages 20–21). Pinker concedes each "is right about something" but rejects "anything can happen" (file page 21).

Explicit limits. The preface is retrospective, not a research update (file page 15). Pinker remained agnostic about grammatical formalisms because Government and Binding was discarded; his diluted Lexical Functional Grammar aims at upward compatibility (file pages 15–16). He does not claim these narrow language results are universal learning laws; section 3.4 is titled "On Universality" but its content is absent (file page 9).

Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's). A thinking machine could implement: typed semantic structures with roles like cause, motion, contact, effect; narrow conflation classes as permitted scopes for argument-structure rules; morphological licensing flags on roots; and conservative default generalization revised by counterexamples. The sex-verb analysis (file pages 17–18) shows syntax can expose competing mental models — joint voluntary action versus forceful impingement — useful for social cognition, though speculative. Nothing here addresses perception, memory traces, temporal experience, analogy, creativity, or emotion beyond these hints, and nothing demonstrates general intelligence or consciousness.
