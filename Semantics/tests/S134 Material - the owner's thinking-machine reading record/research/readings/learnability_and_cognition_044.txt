**Subject and central claims.** This section closes Chapter 8 of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition* (printed pages 433–440, file pages 454–460), followed by endnotes to Chapters 1 and 2 (file pages 462–463). Its subject is how speakers decide that a linguistic form "exists" or is grammatical, and what this reveals about rule-governed generalization and abstract thought.

Pinker argues that rule-generated forms divide into three statuses (file page 456, printed 435): forms licensed automatically by existence-predicting rules (most syntax, regular past tense, passive participle); forms acquired conservatively and listed; and forms produced by property-predicting rules that become acceptable only through structure-sensitive analogy to listed forms. The analogy is not ordinary conceptual similarity: it requires identical configurations of "grammaticizable semantic elements" in the relevant argument positions while tolerating arbitrary variation elsewhere — his "Color-Blind Conservatism" (file page 454, printed 433).

**Evidence and reasoning.** The English regular past tense is existence-predicting: *fleeced* sounds natural even for a novel, meaningless stem *fleech* (file page 454, printed 433). Irregular subregularities (*hit/hit*, *bear/bore*, *sting/stung*) are not: *?*forwent* remains unacceptable despite perfect fit (file page 455, printed 434). Yet irregulars are not a mere list: the roughly 180 forms cluster into about a dozen families, historically absorbed new words (*quit*, *cost*), and license nonce forms like *skring → skrung* by similarity to *spring/sprung*, *string/strung* (file page 455, printed 434). Crucially, the similarity dimensions are linguistic — alliteration, rhyme, monosyllabicity — not semantic or raw phonological (file page 456, printed 435). This is the key distinction between permitted scope and memorization: a rule defines possible forms; actualness comes from analogy to listed exemplars along structurally privileged dimensions.

**Open questions.** Pinker explicitly labels his account of why some operations are productive as speculative (file pages 456–457, printed 435–436). He proposes that additive operations (adding *-ing*, passivization) are existence-predicting, while operations with nonadditive "mental chemistry" (causativization, *-t/d* and ablaut) become semiproductive, because some events are more causable than others and some affixes disrupt phonology. He calls further exploration "critical" and offers only "vague speculative examples."

**Spatial schemas and abstraction.** Section 8.6 claims that abstract relations in language map onto a small number of schemas of space, force, and time, grasped by children by age two (file page 457, printed 436). Verb meanings combine a non-Euclidean geometry of things, places, paths with a non-Newtonian physics of force and temporal intervals (same page). Pinker frames this as a possible answer to the puzzle of how brains evolved capacities for mathematics and science without direct selection pressure: old structures (motor programs, perceptual analyzers) may be co-opted for isomorphic abstract tasks (file pages 458–459, printed 437–438). He stresses this is testable against syntactic, historical, developmental, and perceptual data, not just a just-so story (file page 459, printed 438).

**Representation and semantics.** Lexical semantic structure is an autonomous level: grammatical choice uses sparse, cross-classifying idealizations, not rich scripts. *He poured water into the glass* profiles mass motion into a cavity; *He filled the glass* profiles a state change of a dimensionless point (file page 460, printed 439). Unprofiled knowledge is simply not entertained. Pinker suspects this selective abstraction underlies science, law, and mathematics, against "massive networks of probabilistic bits" (same page) — a stated disagreement with connectionist-style knowledge bases.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** A thinking machine could implement three separate mechanisms: existence-predicting operators with transparent output composition; a conservative store of exemplars; and an analogical matcher whose similarity metric is computed over designated structural roles rather than global feature overlap. The *pour/fill* contrast suggests a representation layer that can re-profile one event under competing sparse schemas, with unprofiled detail suppressed rather than deleted. The past-tense cases suggest graded confidence: possible, then actual as structural match tightens. None of this is presented as a universal learning law; the results are narrow English morphological and lexical-semantic findings. Pinker does not address perception, memory persistence, planning, emotion, or social cognition here, and does not claim any architecture reproduces human intelligence or consciousness.
