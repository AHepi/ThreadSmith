What the notes do reveal is the book's citation architecture. Steven Pinker's notes attach empirical sources to topics including status and display, reciprocity and the Banker's Paradox, warfare and ethnocentrism, art and visual aesthetics, music and auditory scene analysis, fiction and goal-directed plots, humor and incongruity resolution, religion, and philosophical puzzlement about consciousness and meaning (file pages 352 through 356). Several entries are worth flagging for a thinking-machine design because they name mechanisms rather than conclusions: auditory scene analysis is credited to Albert Bregman and Steven Pinker's own 1978 work on auditory streaming (file page 354); fiction is described in the note as "experiment" and plots as "goal-seeking," citing Jerry Hobbs, with the further note that the goals in fiction are the goals of natural selection, citing Joseph Carroll (file page 354); case-based reasoning is credited to Roger Schank (file page 355); and philosophical bewilderment is attributed to limits of human conceptual equipment, citing Noam Chomsky and Colin McGinn, with a final note on a mismatch between "the combinatorial mind" and philosophical problems (file pages 355 through 356). These are note headings, not argued theses, and the reasoning behind them is not present here.

The References list from file page 357 onward is alphabetical and runs from Edward Adelson through David Chalmers and beyond. It includes entries relevant to representation and perception (shading and reflectance, stereopsis, computer vision, visual object recognition, mental imagery, unilateral neglect of representational space), to learning and its constraints (Susan Carey on conceptual change and causal understanding, Elizabeth Spelke on domain-specific knowledge, Alyson Brown on domain-specific principles affecting transfer), to memory and time (flashbulb memories, working memory, spatial and verbal components of recall), to causation and reasoning (Cheng and Holyoak on pragmatic reasoning schemas, Chomsky on language and knowledge), and to social cognition (Simon Baron-Cohen on mindblindness and theory of mind, Andrew Whiten and Richard Byrne on Machiavellian intelligence, Dorothy Cheney and Robert Seyfarth). Engineering implications, kept separate: the citation set suggests a machine would need distinct subsystems for perceptual organization, domain-constrained learning, episodic memory, causal and pragmatic inference, goal-directed simulation, and agent modeling. Nothing here demonstrates that any such subsystem works, nor that a non-neural implementation suffices. The notes distinguish innate endowment from acquired knowledge only implicitly, through citations to domain-specific learning; no universal learning law is established.
