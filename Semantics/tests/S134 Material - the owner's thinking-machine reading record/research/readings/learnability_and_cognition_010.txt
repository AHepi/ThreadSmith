**Subject and central claims.** This section of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition* (file pages 110–118; printed labels 89–97 are visible) develops a theory of thematic roles, linking rules, and lexical rules as the machinery behind verb argument structure, applied in detail to the locative alternation and briefly to the dative alternation. Pinker argues against the Fillmore-style tradition, in which thematic roles are atomic labels from a fixed hierarchy, each argument bears exactly one role, and linking preserves rank order (file page 110). He endorses instead the view of Jackendoff, Levin, and Rappaport: thematic roles are positions in structured semantic representations, arguments can bear several roles at once by appearing in several semantic substructures, and linking rules apply in any semantic field, not only physical location (file pages 110–111). The advantage claimed is that semantic constraints lose their arbitrariness and pair nonaccidentally with particular lexical rules (file page 111).

**Lexical rules and the locative alternation.** Conflation classes built on thematic cores cannot by themselves generate new forms, because a new word needs a sound; lexical rules supply this by reusing an existing verb's sound with a new related meaning (file page 111). In the locative alternation, the *into/onto* core is "X moves Y into/onto Z," where Y is the theme and hence object (file page 111). Verbs built on this core specify manner of causation or manner of motion — spilling, injecting, ladling, pouring, spraying — but not necessarily the container's resulting state: pouring water into a glass requires a cohesive stream, yet the glass may end up full, partly full, or empty (file page 112). The *with* core is "X causes Y to change state by means of moving Z to Y": filling a glass with water requires the interior be occupied, regardless of how the water arrived (file page 112). Linking rules map the affected state-change theme to object and the moved "state-changer" to the oblique *with* phrase (file page 113).

**Holism and geometry.** The apparent holism requirement (the whole wagon loaded, the whole wall painted) is argued to be an epiphenomenon of state change: a statue sprayed with a single dab of paint still counts as changed, and a wagon loaded with one box counts if one box is the standard load (file page 113). Talmy's geometric insight is invoked: themes are construed as pointlike and undifferentiated, so promoting the wall to theme yields a holistic reading (file pages 113–114). The alternation is thus a gestalt shift — loading hay into a wagon is something that happens to hay; loading a wagon with hay is something that happens to the wagon (file pages 114–115).

**Constraints on generalization.** Alternating verbs must specify motion that predictably causes a characteristic effect on the goal; this excludes pure manner-of-motion verbs (*pour*), force verbs (*push, drag*), and positioning verbs (*put*), and excludes pure-effect verbs like *fill* from the *into* form (file page 115). Adding *full* to *pour* licenses the alternation (*I poured the glass full with water*), supporting the effect requirement (file page 115). Pinker explicitly limits the claim: the principle explains why criteria exist and take these forms, but does not exactly predict the boundary (e.g., *spray* versus *dribble*), deferred to later sections (file page 116). A parallel removal alternation (*empty garbage from the bag / empty the bag of garbage*) shows only effect-of-removal verbs take the *of* form, again remediable by particles (*shoveled the walk clear of snow*) (file pages 116–117).

**Summary of explanatory gains.** The theory answers why meaning changes with argument structure (the rule alters which argument is theme), why the two meanings are related (motion is embedded in the state change), why object and oblique link as they do (general linking rules plus preposition entries), and why lexical rules are choosy (the required state change must be predictable from verb meaning) (file pages 117–118). The dative alternation is introduced as a parallel shift from "X causes Y to go to Z" to "X causes Z to have Y," predicting that the possessor in the double-object form is affected (*teaching the students French* implies success) (file page 118).

**Limits and status.** These are claims about English verb semantics and learnability, not universal learning laws; Pinker presents the account as a departure from prior theories and defers precise criteria to later sections (file pages 110, 116). No claims about perception, memory, emotion, or social cognition appear here.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests representing events as structured semantic substructures with typed roles rather than flat labels, allowing one entity to occupy multiple roles across substructures (cause, motion, state change). A rule's permitted scope should be licensed by compositional semantic features — such as a predictable effect on a goal — rather than memorized verb lists, mirroring the contrast between productive alternation and rote exceptions. Linking rules that map semantic roles to output positions could generalize across domains (spatial, possession, state), and "gestalt shift" operators could re-construe one event structure into another while preserving shared substructure, a candidate mechanism for analogy and flexible re-description. The source does not address how such structures are grounded perceptually, learned from experience, or how innate versus acquired components divide; those remain open design problems.
