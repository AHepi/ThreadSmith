**Subject and central argument.** This section is Steven Pinker’s defense of natural selection as the only nonmiraculous, forward-causal explanation of adaptive complexity in organisms, presented as groundwork for reverse-engineering the human mind (file page 100, printed pages 158–166). He introduces Darwin’s theory “in an unorthodox way” to stress its core contribution: explaining the appearance of design without a designer (file page 96, printed page 158). The full story begins with a replicator produced by physics and chemistry, not selection, lest one face infinite regress (file page 96, printed page 158). Because the world is finite, replicators compete; copying errors arise; most are harmful, but by chance some improve replication, and descendants accumulate further improvements, including bodies with covers, supports, manipulators, and catalysts (file page 96, printed page 158).

**Rejected alternatives.** Pinker dismisses an unexplained “urge” toward complexity as magic (file page 96, printed page 158). Lamarckian use-and-disuse and inheritance of acquired characteristics fail both empirically and in principle: use does not improve an organ (photons do not wash a lens), adaptive responses to use are themselves evolved designs needing explanation, and most acquired traits are damage; how a wound’s shape could be encoded into germ-cell DNA is mysterious (file pages 96–97, printed pages 158–159). Macromutation is astronomically improbable; Darwin instead reconstructed gradual eye evolution from light-sensitive skin to opaque tissue, cup, hollow, cover, and lens, each step a small improvement (file page 97, printed page 160). Random genetic drift can fix simple or neutral traits but cannot build organs requiring hundreds or thousands of coordinated parts (file page 97, printed page 160). Directed or adaptive mutation is rejected: chemistry has “no sense of niceness”; mutations are indifferent to benefit in general, and claimed adaptive mutations are laboratory curiosities or artifacts (file page 97, printed page 160).

**Complexity theory.** Pinker grants that complexity theory might explain the origin of replicators, bodily cohesion, and tolerable mutation ranges, constraining selection as physics and mathematics do (file page 97, printed page 160). But he accuses readers of a pun: biological “complexity” is functional, adaptive design—digestive tracts as factory lines—not mere order or stability. General equations cannot explain why teeth are in mouths, not ears; matter has no innate tendency to organize into wombats. Selection alone explains adaptive complexity because how well something works causally shapes how it came to be (file page 98, printed page 162). Kauffman and Gell-Mann themselves reject the extrapolation that selection is obsolete (file page 98, printed page 161).

**Evidence.** Pinker cites artificial selection (dog breeds from wolves in thousands of years), the peppered moth tracking industrial soot, and the Grants’ measurements of Galápagos finch beaks evolving with seed availability—“frame-by-frame” selection Darwin could only imagine (file pages 98–99, printed pages 162–163). Variation and deep time exist: three billion years for life (file page 99, printed page 163). Population genetics shows a 1 percent reproductive advantage can drive a variant from 0.1 to 99.9 percent in just over four thousand generations (file page 99, printed page 164). Nilsson and Pelger’s Artificial Life simulation, mutating a three-layer light-sensitive slab and retaining only improvements in image resolution, evolved a fish-like eye with cup, cornea, and lens in four hundred thousand generations under pessimistic assumptions—no master plan, every retained change an improvement (file page 99, printed page 164).

**Limits and disputes.** Pinker lists academic objections—circularity, “half an eye,” insufficient time, Gould, emergent complexity, future physics—and attributes hostility partly to threatened theories of mind (file page 100, printed page 165). Contra Lewontin’s “adaptationism,” a sane evolutionist holds complex organs to be adaptations while allowing drift and by-products (blood’s redness) (file page 100, printed page 166). Phylogenetic baggage exists (the looping seminal duct), yet that does not make the whole eye useless baggage; physics still constrains what evolves (no turning into King Kong) (file page 100, printed page 166). He defends reverse-engineering against the charge of after-the-fact storytelling, quoting Mayr that asking an organ’s function has driven physiology for centuries (file page 100, printed page 166).

**Engineering implications (ours, not Pinker’s).** For a thinking machine, the section models how cumulative, locally evaluated improvements under resource constraints can yield integrated functional design without foresight—relevant to incremental construction of representations and mechanisms that serve goals. It also warns that general self-organization is not a substitute for goal-directed functional organization. The text does not address machine representation, compositionality, perception, learning, memory, time, causation, reasoning, analogy, creativity, emotion, or social cognition; those remain open design problems. Nothing here demonstrates human-level intelligence or consciousness in any architecture.
