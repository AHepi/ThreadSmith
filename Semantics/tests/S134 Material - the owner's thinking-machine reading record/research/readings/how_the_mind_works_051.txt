**Subject and scope.** This section continues Steven Pinker's evolutionary psychology of human social motives, moving from sexuality and marriage into a subsection titled "RIVALS" (file page 286). The central question is posed explicitly: "Is this any way to build an intelligent organism? Where do these powerful motives come from?" (file page 287). The answer offered is that costly, seemingly pointless displays and rituals are signals shaped by the problem of advertising potency that others cannot directly inspect.

**Marriage as property and control.** File page 285 argues that marriage in most societies transfers ownership of a woman from father to husband, citing bride-prices in ninety-six percent of the seventy percent of societies where someone pays, and contrasting dowries, which go to the newlyweds. Pinker links this to control of women's sexuality through chaperones, veils, confinement, and similar practices, noting only fertile women were so controlled. He reads adultery law, the double standard, and the treatment of rape and elopement as property violations against husbands or fathers (file page 285). Common law's three mitigations of murder to manslaughter — self-defense, defense of close relatives, and sexual contact with one's wife — are attributed to Wilson and Daly as threats to Darwinian fitness (file page 285).

**Limits and disagreements.** Pinker explicitly defends evolutionary psychology against the charge of antifeminism (file pages 285–286). He states that documented sex differences concern reproductive psychology, not economic or political worth, and should heighten deterrence of rape, harassment, and discriminatory law (file page 286). What he challenges is a modern orthodoxy: that people act for class or sex interests rather than their own beliefs and desires, that minds are formed by parents, language, and media, and that natural inclinations are good (file page 286). He names the "nature is nice" premise as bad biology, bad psychology, and bad ethics (file page 286).

**Status signals.** The key idea of "RIVALS" is that creatures cannot probe each other's genes, strength, or ferocity directly, so potent creatures advertise with signals; less potent ones counterfeit; and third parties sharpen discrimination (file page 287). Like paper currency, the displays are intrinsically worthless but valuable because everyone treats them so. Pinker separates dominance (who can hurt you) from status (who can help you) (file page 287).

**Dominance.** He rejects the early ethological idea that surrender gestures evolved to preserve the species as a group-benefit fallacy, favoring Maynard Smith and Parker's game modeling: fighting to the death is costly to both sides, so animals size each other up, exaggerate size (ruffs, manes, low bellows), or settle by arbitrary conventions like arrival order (file page 287). Round-robin outcomes yield pecking orders that track win probabilities and can shift with age, injury, or coalition — chimpanzee dominance depends on political acumen (file page 288). Humans lack rigid pecking orders but recognize rank; taller men are hired, paid, and elected more often (file page 288).

**Reputation and honor.** Language adds reputation as a way to propagate dominance information (file page 288). Drawing on Daly and Wilson, Pinker explains "senseless" homicides over insults as defense of a credible threat of violence; a hothead who would stop at nothing is unextortable (file pages 288–289). Cultures of honor arise where the state is absent and wealth is portable — herders more than crop-growers (file page 289). Maleness is the largest risk factor (men kill men twenty-six times more often across thirty-five samples), compounded by youth, poverty, and hopelessness, which steepen future discounting (file page 289). Academics duel verbally through intimidation, authority, and insult in reviews and panels (file page 289).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this section models social cognition as inference over unobservable attributes from costly signals, with adversaries who counterfeit and observers who discriminate — a mechanism for grounded representations of other agents. The dominance analysis offers a template for resource-bounded conflict resolution: assess, concede, escalate only when stakes justify cost. Reputation requires memory of past encounters and temporal updating as probabilities change (file page 288). Goal reasoning here is instrumental and competitive, not normative. The section says nothing about perception, compositionality, learning rules, analogy, or creativity; those design problems remain unsolved by this text. Pinker's claims concern evolved human motives, not architectures; nothing here demonstrates that a non-neural machine could implement them, nor addresses consciousness.
