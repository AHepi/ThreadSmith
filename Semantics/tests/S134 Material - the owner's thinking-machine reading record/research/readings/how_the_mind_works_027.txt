**Subject and central claims.** This section continues Steven Pinker's account of vision, moving from the "experts" that recover depth to the format their output takes and how it becomes usable. Pinker argues that vision does not deliver a full three-dimensional scale model of the world. Phenomenology counts against it: we see only what is before us and lose graphic detail when we move our eyes (file page 152); we see surfaces, not volumes, so an object hidden in a box is known but not visually detailed (file page 152); perspective persists despite depth cues (file page 152); by Poincaré's division test the visual field is two-dimensional, since a line can split it (file page 152); and vision does not naturally deliver "objects," as David Marr's questions about nose, head, and rider-plus-horse show (file page 152). Instead the product is Marr's "2½-D sketch," or visible surface representation (file page 153).

**Representation.** The sketch is a mosaic of cells, one per line of sight from a cyclopean vantage point, finer in the center. Each cell carries either surface information (depth, slant, tilt, color, and a label for which surface it belongs to) or edge information (boundary type, orientation, and which side "owns" the boundary versus backdrop) (file page 153). Depth is only "half" a dimension because it is a value stored in the medium, not a coordinate of the medium, illustrated by the pin-mold toy (file page 153). The forms are explanatory, not literal; the brain presumably uses neural clusters, possibly distributed maps accessed in register (file pages 153–154). Perception is bottom-up here: surfaces are seen involuntarily, "we do not see what we expect to see" (file page 153).

**Why not a full model.** Storage cost and change answer this: a composite model would be obsolete as soon as anything moved, so the brain lets the world store what falls outside a glance and reloads a fresh sketch on each look (file page 154). Depth is viewer-relative (distance, slant, tilt, occlusion), not world coordinates; the rest of the brain must use it (file page 154).

**Frames of reference.** As delivered the sketch is "useless": its coordinates are retinal, so eye, head, body, or object motion invalidates them (file page 154). Location always requires a named frame (file page 154). Proposed mechanisms: a movable crosshair or cursor-like frame; a head-stabilized frame shifted by a copy of the eye-muscle command, demonstrated by the eye-poking illusion where the world jumps (file page 155); frames compensating for head and body motion via efference copies or visual-slippage tracking (file page 155); a world-aligned trapezoidal grid giving equal ground extents, matching J. J. Gibson's "visual field" versus "visual world" attitudes (file page 155); and a gravity frame from the vestibular canals, only partly applied to vision (file pages 155–156).

**Causation and limits.** Mismatch between visual and vestibular signals causes motion sickness; Michel Treisman's proposed but explicitly unproven explanation treats gravity as the most stable cue, so disagreement signals poisoning and triggers vomiting (file page 156). Shape description is frame-relative: rotated Africa is hard to recognize because form is encoded relative to the up-down axis (file page 156).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** A thinking machine needs addressable, viewer-relative surface records with ownership labels, plus transformable reference frames (retinal, body, world, gravity) and efference-copy updates, rather than a monolithic voxel world. This supports stable action and memory across viewpoints. The section leaves unsolved how surfaces become objects, how the sketch feeds language, memory over time, goals, reasoning, analogy, or social thought; narrow perceptual results are not universal learning laws. Innate machinery (experts, frames) is distinguished from acquired content (what is seen). The final sentence is cut off mid-word ("what's"), and the Africa figure is visually dependent.
