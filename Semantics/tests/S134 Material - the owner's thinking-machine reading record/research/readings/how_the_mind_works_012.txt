**Subject and central claims.** This section, headed "Connectoplasm," continues Steven Pinker's argument about how mental software might be implemented in neural hardware. He first describes error back-propagation: when a teacher supplies correct outputs, output units send hidden units a surrogate error signal, allowing weights to be adjusted iteratively through any number of layers (file page 69). A hidden-layer network can thus compose propositions into complex logical functions, with continuous values standing for degrees or probabilities of truth, and can generalize from one input to similar inputs "as long as the problem is one in which similar inputs yield similar outputs" (file page 69). Pinker presents this as a "rickety" bridge from conceptual psychology through rules and representations to neurons, and suggests neural modelers may supply a parts catalogue of reusable components such as content-addressable memory or a generalizing pattern associator (file page 69).

**The dispute.** Pinker frames a boundary dispute: at high levels cognition resembles a production system with symbolic inscriptions and demons, while lower levels resemble pattern-associating networks, but where the boundary lies is contested (file pages 69–70). Connectionism, led by David Rumelhart and James McClelland, claims simple networks can explain most intelligence, with rules and symbols as mere approximations (file page 70). Pinker favors the opposing view: networks must be structured into programs manipulating symbols, especially for language and reasoning that interacts with it; he cites his own work arguing that English past-tense formation is too computationally sophisticated for a single network (file page 70). He connects this to associationism (Locke, Hume, Berkeley, Hartley, Mill), whose laws of contiguity and resemblance map onto network training and distributed representations, making connectionism a testable version of the blank-slate doctrine (file pages 70–71).

**Limits and red herrings.** Pinker stresses that connectionism is a variety of the computational theory of mind, not an alternative; that no one holds the straw-man view of a serial error-free CPU cranking through logic-textbook rules; and that these networks are not realistic brain models, since their weights can switch sign and information flows both ways along "axons," which is anatomically impossible (file page 71). The real question is whether they perform the right computations.

**Individuals.** The first of five feats of everyday thinking that "raw connectoplasm" cannot handle is representing individuals. Property-pattern representations cannot distinguish two entities with identical properties; doubling activation confuses number with confidence or degree (file page 71). Class–subclass inheritance (animal to horse) resembles subclass–individual inheritance (horse to Mr. Ed), tempting modelers to treat individuals as ultra-specific subclasses, echoing Berkeley's "congeries of sensible impressions" (file pages 71–72). But two identical chairs swapped in a room are understood as a changed room, and two identical circles overlapping remain distinct, so spatiotemporal stamping is not our definition of individuality (file page 72). The fix is to dedicate units or serial-number-like codes to identity independent of properties — arbitrary labels like computer memory slots (file page 72). This matters for social cognition: a spouse feels no attraction to an identical twin, and justice in the Blick twin ear-biting case demands identifying which individual acted (file pages 72–73). Pinker speculates this capacity evolved because each person houses a unique history of memories and desires, and notes analogous tracking problems for mothers, predators, and possibly zebra stripes, while conceding we do not know whether animals possess the concept of an individual (file page 73).

**Compositionality.** The second problem is compositionality: meanings built from parts and their arrangement, as in "The baby ate the slug" versus "The slug ate the baby," enabling novel thoughts assembled on the fly (file page 73). Simply activating units for "baby," "eats," and "slug" leaves the thinker "in a fog" about who did what to whom (file page 73).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this argues for identity tokens separate from property bundles, and for structured, role-labeled representations rather than undifferentiated activation patterns. The back-propagation description is a source claim about learning mechanisms; whether an inspectable non-neural machine needs it is our design question, not settled here.
