**Subject and central claims.** This section closes an argument that rational thought bottoms out in unanalyzed "reasoning instincts": no rule can consult another rule all the way down without infinite regress, so at some point a thinker simply executes a step as "the human way" (file page 111). These instincts link into complex programs for rational analysis, but they are fallible — seducible by sophistry and paradox (Zeno, sentience, free will) (file page 111). The section then turns to "The Cognitive Niche," arguing that humans are unique less in body than in behavior: goal-directed chains assembled on the spot, planned with causal models of the world, learned within lifetimes, transmitted by language, and accumulated across generations (file page 111).

**The cognitive niche.** Steven Pinker endorses John Tooby and Irven DeVore's proposal: in an evolutionary arms race where prey defenses change only across generations, humans exploit a "cognitive niche" by learning which manipulations achieve which goals — the surprise attack (file page 112). Crucially, human knowledge is not confined to concrete recipes like "how to catch a rabbit." People analyze the world through intuitive theories of objects, forces, paths, places, manners, states, substances, hidden essences, and others' beliefs and desires, and compose new plans by mentally playing out combinatorial interactions among these laws "in the mind's eye" (file page 112). Foragers exemplify this: vocabularies for space, time, motion, mental states, tools, flora, fauna, weather, plus logical connectives (not, and, same, opposite, part-whole, general-particular), combined into sentences used to reason about invisible entities (diseases, weather forces, absent animals); mental maps of thousands of sites and nested calendars of seasons and life cycles (file pages 112–113). The !Kung tracking example shows inference from trampling, freshness, straight-line travel, and repeated footprints to conclude the wildebeest is resting in shade nearby (file page 113). Tools, fire, poisons, nets, and traps let humans "crack the safes" of other organisms, driving Ice Age extinctions (file page 113).

**Why us.** Pinker's own guess (explicitly a guess; the event happened once, "no one knows") is that four traits primed causal reasoning (file page 114). First, primate vision: stereoscopic depth and color yielded a three-dimensional space of movable solid objects, splitting the visual stream into "what" and "where" systems; abstract thought co-opts this format — we say John "went from" sick to well without moving, and scientists plot relations as graphs (file page 114). Olfactory mammals, by contrast, live in a "two-dimensional flatland" explored through a "zero-dimensional peephole," lacking models of 3-D mechanical relations (file page 114). Second, group living multiplies the value of information — the one commodity given away yet kept — and poses social challenges (theft, deception, coalitions), plausibly driving "Machiavellian intelligence"; but Pinker doubts social competition alone sufficed, since costs of large brains would damp the loop, and humans are also mechanically and biologically intelligent (file pages 114–115). Third, the grasping hand, refined in arboreal primates and used by apes for tool-like acts (file page 115). A fourth trait is cut off by the excerpt's end.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests representational primitives for objects, forces, paths, substances, and agents' goals, composed combinatorially to simulate candidate plans; a "what/where" split; knowledge that generalizes beyond memorized instances via intuitive theories; cheap copying of knowledge across a community; and long apprenticeship-like learning. It does not supply algorithms, prove an inspectable non-neural machine suffices, or address consciousness. Topics like analogy, creativity, and emotion are largely absent here; social cognition appears only as deception and coalition tracking.
