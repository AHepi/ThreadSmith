**Subject and central claims.** This section of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition* develops a decompositional semantic representation for verbs, centered on the primitive ACT relation and a family of causal "subordinating relations" that link events within a verb's meaning. The ACT relation is dyadic and asymmetric: its first argument is causally responsible, its second is the patient, and any explicitly represented change is caused by the interaction with the patient as theme. ACT also hosts a MANNER specification, and the patient "measures out" the event's temporal delimitation — hitting is complete only when the patient has received the blow (file page 248). A feature system classifies the four basic predicates along <±dynamic> and <±control>: GO and ACT are events, HAVE and BE states; ACT and HAVE have controlling human first arguments, GO and BE have will-less points (file pages 249–250).

**Inter-event structure.** Pinker rejects Jackendoff's "tier" proposal, arguing that verbs routinely combine several acts and several changes of location, and that verb-internal events always stand in quasi-causal relations — there is no verb meaning "simultaneously John yawned and the cat fell off the roof" (file page 249). He supports this with "sniglets" — culturally salient but linguistically impossible meanings such as *wattbobble* (alternating turning a hot bulb and cooling fingers) and *purpitation* (replacing a rejected item in the wrong aisle) — showing that many conceivable event sequences are not lexicalizable (file pages 250–251). The formal constraint is that lexicosematic representations are single-rooted connected graphs; the substantive constraint is that multiple events in a verb must stand in a causally conceived relation (file page 251).

**Representational machinery.** Coreference among arguments is marked by indexes, with square brackets identifying the single position that triggers a linking rule (file pages 251–252). Examples: *run* conflates manner with motion, represented as an ACT with an effect of GO along a path; *break* is an unspecified direct ACT with an effect identifying the patient as theme of a "broken" property; *cut* embeds a means-substructure — acting on a knife causing it to go against and through the pear — beneath an effect of the pear becoming cut (file pages 252–254). The choice of which event is subordinate is constrained by parsimony and by syntax: since the patient, not the instrument, is the direct object of *cut*, the cut is the effect and the instrument-motion the means (file page 253). Oblique arguments are handled by linking rules keyed to the <±control> feature, with a lexical entry for *with* selecting a patient inside a means-substructure (file pages 254–255).

**Causal family.** Using features of focus (cause or effect), potency (antagonist succeeds or fails), and occurrence of cause and effect events, Pinker derives six subordinators: effect, cause, despite, but, let, prevent (file pages 256–258). Extended causation is a stative ACT, as in *support* (file page 256). Intent or purpose adds a <purposive> feature, distinguishing *kill* from *murder*, *chase* from *follow*; *chase* is glossed as acting and going toward the mouse in order to be at the mouse (file page 258). Obligation adds a <deontic> feature, yielding "obligates" and "fulfills" for *buy*/*sell* versus *reward*/*repay* (file page 259). The feature system reduces ten putative primitives to six binary features, predicting up to sixty-four possible subordinating relations across languages (file page 259; the passage ends mid-sentence, so the qualification "if no further" constraints apply is truncated).

**Limits and disagreements.** Pinker explicitly disputes tiered representations (file page 249) and notes intuitive uncertainty about event embedding in *run* and *cut*, resolved by syntactic prediction (file pages 252–253). He cites disagreement over atomic versus decomposed causal representations (file page 257). The claims concern lexical semantics of English and cross-linguistic verb structure, not universal learning laws; children's confusion of causing and letting is flagged as a chapter 7 topic, not established here (file page 257).

**Engineering implications (ours, not Pinker's).** The single-rooted connected graph with typed causal links offers a compositional format for grounded event representations: primitive predicates (ACT, GO, BE, HAVE) plus feature-valued subordinators could let a machine build structured descriptions of perceived episodes, distinguish means from effects, and encode goals and obligations as explicit nodes. The coreference-by-index mechanism is a workable recipe for binding arguments across sub-events, relevant to tracking objects through time and to analogical mapping between event schemas. The sniglet argument suggests a principled filter on which generalizations a learner should attempt: not every conceivable event conjunction is a candidate lexical unit. However, the section supplies no learning algorithm, no account of how perceptual input is segmented into ACT/GO/BE primitives, no memory architecture, and no treatment of emotion or social cognition beyond goal and obligation features. It is a representational proposal, not a demonstrated thinking machine, and nothing here establishes that an inspectable non-neural implementation would suffice for human-like intelligence.
