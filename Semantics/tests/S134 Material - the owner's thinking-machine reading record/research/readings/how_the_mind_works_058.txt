Steven Pinker's subject here is the psychology of religion and the limits of human cognition, culminating in the hypothesis of "cognitive closure." He argues religion is not reducible to ethical or spiritual yearnings, citing violent religious history, and treats religious customs as cultural instruments serving promulgators' interests (file page 321). Following Ruth Benedict, he calls religion "a technique for success": people invoke gods when stakes are high and ordinary causal techniques fail (file page 321).

Pinker's central representational claim, drawing on Pascal Boyer and Dan Sperber, is that religious concepts are ordinary module-supplied constructs with a property deleted or added. A spirit keeps standard person or animal defaults—seeing, hearing, memory, beliefs, desires, decisions, threats—while being exempted from selected laws of biology, physics, or psychology (file pages 321–322). Teachers need not spell out these defaults because pupils' tacit psychology supplies them; believers also avoid deriving the strange logical consequences of piecemeal revisions (file page 322). This is compositionality with local emendation, not wholesale invention. Animistic beliefs, following Edward Tylor, are hypotheses explaining baffling data: dreams, trances, shadows, reflections, and death (file page 322).

The section then turns to philosophical puzzles Pinker says remain unsolved: sentience, the self, free will, meaning, knowledge, and morality (file pages 322–323). He reviews four failed solution types—mysticism, declaring the entities irreducible, denying the problem as linguistic confusion, and "domesticating" it by equating consciousness with cortical activity or morality with kin selection—each of which, he argues, leaves the main question intact (file pages 323–324). Pinker favors Colin McGinn's proposal, indebted to Noam Chomsky, Gunther Stent, and David Hume: human minds evolved for ancestral survival, not for grasping every truth, so some problems may be "cognitively closed" to us (file page 324). He stresses this is a claim about one species' organ, not a paradox of self-reference, not a limit on all knowers, and not a license for religion; computational, neurological, and evolutionary aspects of consciousness remain tractable (file page 325). He closes with the analogy that humanly thinkable thoughts are closed under our cognitive faculties yet possibly infinite, and praises the mind's "syntactic, compositional, combinatorial abilities" that build complex ideas from simpler ones via relations like part-of, example-of, actor-exerting-force, cause-of-effect, and mind-holding-belief, extended through analogy and metaphor into science and mathematics (file page 325).

Engineering implications, kept separate: the emendation-plus-defaults model suggests representing concepts as structured bundles of properties with explicit override slots, where inference engines fill unstated defaults from typed ontologies (person, object, artifact) rather than memorizing whole scripts. The scope of a rule matters: spirits inherit most person-model inferences but are exempted from named physical or biological laws, so a reasoner must track permitted scope rather than treat the concept as an unrelated memorized entry. Pinker's discussion of dreams, souls, and meaning illustrates how anomalies generate hypotheses; a machine would need anomaly detection plus candidate explanation generation, not just pattern matching. His puzzles about knowledge (the "grue" problem, brain-in-a-vat) mark unsolved constraints on generalization that no architecture here resolves. Nothing in this section demonstrates that a non-neural machine suffices, nor does it address learning algorithms, memory indexing, or temporal representation directly.
