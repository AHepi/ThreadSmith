This section of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition* (file pages 343–353; printed pages 322–331) proposes how a learner could form narrow-range lexical rules governing verb alternations, and then summarizes the chapter's learning mechanisms.

**Source claims: narrow-range rule formation.** Pinker's central proposal is "Narrow Conflation Class Formation by Parameterization of Idiosyncratic Lexical Information" (file page 343). The learner takes a verb's semantic structure, replaces idiosyncratic details—manner, or object properties outside a distinguished set—with parameters, yielding a class of verbs sharing grammatically relevant structure while nonlinguistic details vary. Possessing such a class lets a speaker coin or understand a new verb while recognizing it as a coinage (file page 343). A narrow-range rule then takes an existing verb form and creates a full morphological and semantic specification for a new form perceived as an existing member of the language; the generalization is "if verb X alternates, other verbs with the same grammatically relevant semantic structure alternate, too" (file page 343).

Crucially, narrow-range rules require prior broad-range rules, which abstract over sets of verbs showing the same alternation. This blocks accidental single-verb alternations: *blame the accident on John / blame John for the accident* is unique to *blame*, and *rip off* uniquely alternates between loot-object and victim-object forms among possession-deprivation verbs (file pages 343–344). Pinker leaves open whether type frequency directly strengthens narrow-range rules, suspecting the one-pair versus many-pair distinction may suffice (file page 344). This is a permitted scope for generalization, not memorization of individual forms.

**Variable ranges.** Simple copying would make every rule too narrow. Pinker proposes "Preservation of Argument-Relevant Parameterized Lexicosemantic Structure": a narrow-range rule preserves nodes dominated by the same nodes as the remapped open arguments, or coindexed with them, while deeper substructures are parameterized wholesale (file pages 345–346). Middle formation touches agent and patient/theme arguments, so it ignores embedded path details; locativization assigns new roles to theme and location, so it must inspect the entire semantic tree, explaining its very narrow rules (file pages 344–345). Advantages: rule complexity mirrors verb-meaning complexity, class sizes vary naturally, and semantic "noncoplanarity" (Talmy 1983) explains redundant differences between classes (file pages 346–347).

**Color-Blind Conservatism.** Grammar is maximally conservative but "blind" to distinctions outside its privileged semantic vocabulary: it can distinguish *smearing* from *pouring* but not *smearing* from *smudging* (file page 347). Pinker derives this from conservatism, a grammatically relevant semantic subvocabulary, and independence of morphology from lexical listing (di Sciullo and Williams 1987), noting languages cannot be itemwise conservative (file page 347).

**Qualifications.** Pinker weakens this hypothesis. Verbs within classes differ in grammatically relevant detail (*sell* and *pay* specify countertransfer; *hand* specifies means), potentially blocking generalization (file page 348). Three relaxations follow: classes may be narrower than assumed, with broad-range rules licensing coinages felt as initially strange (file pages 348–349); matching may tolerate one difference ("myopic"); and matching may ignore certain less crucial differences while respecting aspectual, event-type, and field distinctions ("astigmatic") (file pages 349–350). A continuum of acceptability may track deviation (file page 350).

**Summary of mechanisms (file pages 351–352).** Linking rules are not learned, though ergative languages may require adjustment. Lexicosemantic structures arise via Event-Category Labeling under semantic biases, discourse attention, and the Principle of Contrast, shaped by Syntactic Cueing and possibly syntactic bootstrapping. Broad conflation classes come from reverse linking and lexical abstraction; broad-range rules map classes via semantic and morphological operations. Narrow classes and rules follow the parameterization and preservation procedures above.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests representing meanings as structured trees with privileged vocabularies, parameters for idiosyncratic content, and scope-sensitive abstraction keyed to argument roles—mechanisms for constrained generalization rather than rote storage. The tolerance metrics offer graded novelty handling. However, the text addresses verb alternation only; it does not establish universal learning laws, nor does it cover perception, memory, time, causation, goals, reasoning, analogy, creativity, emotions, or social cognition.
