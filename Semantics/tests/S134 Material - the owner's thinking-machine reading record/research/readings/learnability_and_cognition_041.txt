**Subject and central claims.** This section of Chapter 7 (printed pages 401–411; file pages 422–432) argues that children's argument-structure errors arise from errors in verb meaning, not from syntactic overgeneralization alone. Steven Pinker proposes that argument structures are projections of lexicosemantic structure: a verb's thematic core determines which participant becomes the direct object (file page 422, printed 401). The thematic core of the into/onto locative is "X causing Y to move to Z," with manner attached to causation or motion; if a child misreads *touch* as "cause X to move into contact with Y" or *fill* as "pour X into Y," the locative form wrongly fits (file page 422).

**Experimental evidence.** Pinker and Jess Gropen test whether Gentner's manner-over-end-state bias explains persistent "fill the water" errors. In a picture-sequence task, children chose which before-and-after pair matched a verb (file pages 423–424, printed 402–403). All age groups (2;6–5;5) correctly identified manner-specific *pour* and *dump*, but none reliably identified end-state-specific *fill* and *empty*; half the oldest group chose pouring-with-empty-glass for *fill* (file page 424). In a second task, children produced content objects for *fill* about as often as container objects, unlike adults (file pages 424–425). A contingency analysis showed a trend linking manner-biased interpretation to syntactic errors (nine children consistent versus three converse; file pages 425–426).

**Causal manipulation.** Gropen taught novel verbs in argumentless gerund form ("This is pilking") with actions varying whether manner of motion or end-state change was distinctive (file pages 426–427). When the goal changed state (cloth sagging or changing color), children and adults encoded the goal as object; when the theme moved distinctively (hopping, zigzag), they encoded the theme as object (file pages 427–428). A manner bias persisted in the pennies task, attributed to confounded downward motion (file page 428). Pinker concludes that verb meanings constrain argument structure: theme/patient status maps to object, and which entity counts as theme/patient depends on distinctive manner versus state change, superimposed on a manner bias (file pages 428–429).

**Narrow-range rules and limits.** Pinker notes difficulty testing narrow-range rules in children, since correct use is indistinguishable from memorization and children know few verbs per subclass (file page 429). He predicts that as semantic representations refine, children will generalize argument structures only to semantically similar verbs (file pages 429–430). Pilot dative experiments failed until pretraining demonstrated that manner-of-causation verbs can dativize; children then generalized to novel verbs (file pages 430–431). A speculative maturation hypothesis suggests adult narrow-class rules freeze at a critical point near puberty (file page 431).

**Engineering implications (distinct from source).** For a thinking machine, this suggests representing events as structured thematic roles (agent, theme, goal, patient) with slots for manner and end state, and letting argument selection follow from which participant undergoes the most distinctive change. Learning should bias toward manner but permit salience-driven reassignment. Generalization requires tracking semantic similarity within subclasses, not blanket rules; a system should record which exemplars license a construction and restrict extension accordingly. Temporal before/after representations and causal attribution (manner of causation versus result) are essential. The source does not address perception, memory implementation, emotion, analogy, or social cognition; those remain open design problems. Narrow language results should not be treated as universal learning laws.
