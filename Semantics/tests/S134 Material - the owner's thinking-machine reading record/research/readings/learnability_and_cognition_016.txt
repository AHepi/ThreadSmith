This section, spanning file pages 167 to 176 (printed pages 146 to 155), continues Chapter 4's examination of "Possible and Actual Forms," specifically the locative and causative verb alternations. Steven Pinker's central claim is that these alternations are not governed by broad semantic domains but by narrow, semantically cohesive subclasses, supporting his "narrow-class" theory over purely statistical or memorization-based accounts.

**Locative alternation.** Pinker analyzes 142 verbs taking "into/onto" or "with" forms, deriving directionality from argument obligatoriness (file page 167). He finds roughly seven subclasses per direction, defined by thematic roles, force dynamics (citing Talmy 1988), dimensional geometry (Talmy 1983; Jackendoff 1987c), and a count/mass distinction for matter (file page 168). Content-oriented classes include forceful contact ("smear"), vertical arrangement ("pile"), ballistic motion ("splash"), and widespread distribution ("scatter"); container-oriented classes include capacity-filling ("stuff"), function-enabling ("load"), covering ("inundate"), qualitative change ("adorn"), coextensive saturation ("soak"), impediment ("clog"), and surface distribution ("stud") (file pages 168–170). Two unique verbs, "string" and "wrap," occupy one-word classes (file page 170).

**Motivation.** Alternation correlates with two factors: the ease of reconstruing a goal as a patient, and "interpredictability"—alternating content verbs must specify a particular resulting state of the goal (e.g., "smear" predicts a smear shape), while alternating container verbs must specify what or how something moves (e.g., "stuff" implies excess relative to capacity) (file pages 171–172). Pinker notes this motivation may be psychological or merely historical, an open question (file page 172). Crucially, subclasses may contain any number of verbs, even one, suggesting generalizations are licensed by semantic regions rather than statistical averaging (file page 172). Related alternations (image-impression, emptying) follow the same pattern; possession-removal verbs like "steal" and "rob" virtually never alternate (Talmy 1985), a fortunate gap given their near-synonymy (file pages 172–173).

**Causative alternation.** Only three intransitive classes causativize: extrinsic physical change ("The box opened"), contained motion ("The log slid"), and locomotion, which shifts meaning to coercion ("I walked the horse") or enabling ("Captain Mars flew her") (file pages 173–174). Noncausativizable classes include directed motion ("*I went my son"), volitional action ("*Bert ate Sally"), existence ("*Catherine died Bobby"), emotional expression ("*Irv smiled his audience"), and emission ("*Barbara glowed the light") (file pages 174–175). Conversely, transitive verbs of directed motion, killing/creating, and inducing behavior resist anticausativization (file pages 175–176). Pinker distinguishes pure inchoatives (specific events) from middles (properties, e.g., "Bureaucrats bribe easily") and notes motion-contact-effect verbs like "cut" resist anticausativization despite inherent causation (file page 176). Motivations include lexical gaps (no verb means "cause to rejoice") and semantic fit (file page 176).

**Limits.** Pinker concedes subjectivity in judging argument asymmetry (file page 167) and leaves the psychological status of interpredictability open (file page 172). These are narrow language-learning results, not universal learning laws.

**Engineering implications.** For a thinking machine, this suggests representing events with structured roles (theme, goal, force, geometry, mass/count) and permitting transformations only when semantic constraints—such as interpredictability or reconstrual ease—are satisfied. A rule's permitted scope should derive from feature-based subclass membership, not memorized verb lists. However, the source addresses linguistic generalization, not grounded perception, memory, or action; those remain unsolved. Innate capacities (e.g., thematic role structure) versus acquired subclass knowledge are not fully disentangled here.
