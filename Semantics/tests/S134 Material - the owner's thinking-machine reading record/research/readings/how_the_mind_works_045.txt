**Subject and central claims.** This section continues Steven Pinker's evolutionary psychology of kinship, moving from fictive kin and marriage alliances into parent–offspring conflict. His core method is to derive emotional dispositions from genetic interests and then test the derivation against ethnographic and biological evidence.

**Fictive kin and marriage.** Pinker reports Richard Alexander's argument that faithful spouses with shared children and lifelong unions have identical genetic interests, so marital love "should be stronger than any other kind" (file page 255). In reality, uncertainty about fidelity, desertion, and death means people fine-tune emotions to particulars, which is why in-laws, infidelity, and stepchildren cause strife (file page 255). Marriages link in-laws genetically through grandchildren and commercially through dowries and bride-prices; cross-cousin marriage consummates exchange with a proven partner clan, whereas parallel-cousin marriage does not (file page 255). Pinker rejects two "myths": that people in traditional societies have no voice in marriage, and that kinship is unrelated to biology. Romantic love is real and children lobby fiercely for it, as in *Tevye's Daughters* (file pages 255–256). Fictive kin labels are face-saving workarounds or solidarity tactics; privately, people distinguish real from fake kin and side with blood relatives in disputes (file page 256).

**Politics and the family.** Parental love produces the "fundamental paradox of politics": fairness plus freedom plus inherited wealth cannot coexist with equality (file page 256). Pinker, citing Ferdinand Mount, calls the family "subversive" because it rivals the loyalties of church and state, which is why totalitarian and religious movements demand higher loyalties (file pages 256–257, quoting Matthew 10:34–37). Citing Nancy Thornhill and Laura Betzig, he argues incest and marriage laws often target wealth concentration in rival families rather than brother-sister marriage itself (file page 257).

**Parent–offspring conflict.** Robert Trivers's theory: parents should shift investment from an older child to a younger when the benefit to the younger exceeds the cost to the older, but each child, being fully related to itself and only half to siblings, wants investment continued until the benefit exceeds twice the cost (file pages 257–258). This predicts weaning conflict, sibling rivalry, and partial rather than total selfishness in children (file page 258). David Haig's prenatal conflict: the fetal placenta secretes hormones that raise maternal blood sugar and pressure, provoking maternal countermeasures (file pages 258–259). Daly and Wilson's finding: infanticide tracks actuarial risk (deformity, twinning, fatherlessness, maternal youth, poverty) across cultures, and postpartum depression may be an adaptive pause for a costly decision rather than mere hormonal delirium (file page 259). Bonding is gradual, not a critical-window imprint, and infants advertise viability through cuteness and responsiveness (file page 259).

**Limits and disagreements.** Pinker disputes romantic-love-as-modern-invention and biology-free kinship theories (file page 256), and the hormonal-delirium account of postpartum depression (file page 259). He notes exceptions: some species do have lethal siblicide (file page 258). The text ends mid-sentence on Konrad Lorenz's baby geometry (file page 259); the continuation is not supplied.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this section models emotions as *goal-sensitive valuation functions* over agents with partially shared and partially opposed interests, updated by evidence about reliability, relatedness, and future options. Useful mechanisms: explicit agent models with attributed interests; commitment and betrayal tracking; discounting of others' welfare by relatedness and reciprocity; and decision thresholds under uncertainty (the infanticide and bonding material shows graded commitment rather than binary attachment). The cross-cousin analysis shows how *rule scope* (permitted marriage classes) can be distinguished from mere case memorization by deriving the rule from an exchange model. However, Pinker supplies no computational architecture, no account of how such representations are composed or grounded perceptually, and no theory of learning these dispositions; innate emotional biases versus culturally acquired rules are asserted, not mechanistically separated. Nothing here demonstrates general intelligence or consciousness.
