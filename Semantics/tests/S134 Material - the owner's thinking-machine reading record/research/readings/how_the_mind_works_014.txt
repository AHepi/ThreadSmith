**Subject and central claims.** This section continues Steven Pinker's argument in *How the Mind Works* that human thought is better explained by crisp, compositional, rule-governed representations than by associationist "connectoplasm." The onion-loving gorilla example (file page 79) shows that generalization is not fixed by surface similarity: knowing that chimpanzees and gorillas are both great apes suggests one answer, but knowing that chimpanzees are omnivores while gorillas are herbivores with specialized digestion suggests the opposite. Depending on which explanatory system is invoked, the two animals are either near-identical category-mates or "as different as people and cows" (file page 79).

**Representation and generalization.** Pinker argues that in associationism, representing an object as a bundle of properties automatically commits the system to a particular generalization unless contrary examples are trained in. His alternative is that humans mentally symbolize kinds and refer to those symbols in multiple rule systems, which he links to explanation-based generalization in artificial intelligence, versus similarity-based generalization in connectionism (file page 79). Rule systems express knowledge as "compositional, quantified, recursive propositions" interlocking into modules or intuitive theories of domains such as kinship, intuitive science, intuitive psychology, number, language, and law (file page 79). This is the key distinction between a rule's permitted scope and mere memorization: a rule licenses conclusions about cases never encountered.

**Why crisp categories matter.** Socially, sharp boundaries let rites of passage, licenses, and legal categories settle disputes and block "salami tactics" over borderline cases (file page 79). In the natural world, rules let us "get beneath the surface" of similarity to hidden laws, and their digital precision supports long chains of reasoning. Pinker's sorites example (all ravens are crows, all crows are birds, and so on to "all animals need oxygen") lets a thinker conclude ravens need oxygen without any observation, even on expert testimony alone; fuzzy or probabilistic links would accumulate noise like a degraded tape or a broken-telephone whisper (file pages 79–80).

**Limits and disagreements.** Pinker rejects the framing of the connectionism debate as innateness versus learning. His complaint is that raw connectoplasm is underpowered, forcing the "worst combination: too much innate structure combined with too much environmental input" (file page 80). Hinton's family-relationship network, intended as a demonstration but treated by others as psychological theory, hardwires a quiz format (name plus relationship in, related name out); its knowledge is smeared across weights rather than stored in a reusable database, so it cannot answer rephrased questions such as how two people are related (file page 80). It required training on 100 of 104 possible pairs, each presented 1,500 times (150,000 lessons), to generalize to the remaining four — unlike children (file page 80). Networks interpolate rather than cut to solutions by rules, so every substantially new kind of example must be in the training set or the network interpolates spuriously (the duck-hunt joke, file page 80). Pinker nonetheless insists neural-network modeling is important and sometimes surprising; his complaint is overselling. What makes a system smart, he says, is the choice of input representation, number of networks, wiring diagrams, data pathways, and control structures, not generic connectoplasm (file pages 80–81).

**Consciousness.** The section then turns to consciousness, which the computational theory "even with complete neural underpinnings, offers no clear answer" to (file page 81), quoting Johnson, Huxley, and McGinn on the apparent gap between matter and experience. Pinker distinguishes, following Ray Jackendoff and Ned Block, several senses: intelligence; self-knowledge; access to information; and sentience (file pages 82–83). Self-knowledge is ordinary perception and memory — a self-entry in a people database, a mirror as a tool; "any beginning programmer" can write self-examining software (file page 83). Access-consciousness is the pool of information available to verbal report, rational thought, and deliberate decision, versus inaccessible processing (digestion, visual computations, syntax, motor sequences), with traffic between pools as skills become automatic (file page 83). Machines have analogues: a computer "knows" a printer is unresponsive but not why; the printer's chip knows why (file page 83). Sentience — "what it is like" — remains the miracle (file page 83).

**Mechanisms and engineering specs.** Crick and Koch propose lab criteria: awake versus anesthetized brain structures (lower cortical layers as candidates), binding of distributed object information possibly by synchronized firing entrained by cortex-thalamus loops, and frontal-lobe fiber tracts for planned behavior (file pages 83–84). Computationally, access-consciousness resembles the production system's communal short-term workspace or bulletin board, versus long-term memory copied into it; conscious information is "inferentially promiscuous," usable by many agents (seeing a ruler, describing it, reaching, deducing uses) (file page 84). Newell and Simon simulated problem-solving from think-aloud reports matching bulletin-board contents step by step (file page 84). Access must be limited because information costs space: combinatorial explosion rules out memorizing all chess games (about 10^120 versus 10^70 particles), sentences, stories, or melodies; a processor needs rules or algorithms computing answers on demand (file page 84).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For an inspectable non-neural thinking machine, this supports explicit typed symbols, multiple interlocking theory modules, and rule-based deduction with quantifiers and recursion, plus a bounded global workspace for broadcast. The kinship critique implies knowledge should live in a queryable database rather than in weights tied to one question format. The sorites argument motivates exact symbolic copying across inference steps. Pinker's claims concern human cognition; none of this demonstrates that a symbolic machine achieves human intelligence or consciousness, and the sentience problem is explicitly left unsolved.
