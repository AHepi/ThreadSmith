**Subject and central claims.** This section moves from neurological and twin evidence to Steven Pinker's program of "reverse-engineering the psyche." Brain injuries reveal that vision is not one faculty: patients may see positions without seeing motion, copy a bird yet call it a tree stump, or recognize a face without recognizing the person (file page 16). Such syndromes arise from damage to one or more of roughly thirty primate visual areas specialized for color and form, location, identity, and movement (file page 16). The lesson for a seeing robot is that unified vision rests on many hidden layers of apparatus (file page 16).

Twin studies, Pinker argues, show genes shape fine-grained talents, opinions, and habits, even in separated identical twins; he stresses these findings measure variation among people, not the design shared by all normal minds, and warns against imagining single genes for traits or denying experience (file page 16). The mind's complexity, he concludes, is the book's subject.

**Reverse engineering.** Pinker's key idea: the mind is a system of organs of computation, designed by natural selection for ancestral foraging problems—understanding and outmaneuvering objects, animals, plants, and people (file page 16). Unpacked: the mind is what the brain does; thinking is computation; modules are specialized experts whose basic logic is genetically specified and shaped by selection for ancestral problems, ultimately serving gene replication (file page 17). Psychology is reverse engineering: infer what a machine was designed to do, as with an antique olive-pitter whose ring, lever, and blade suddenly make sense (file page 17). Harvey's discovery of venous valves and Darwin's account of complex organs ground this method; Darwin predicted psychology would rest on a new foundation, yet Pinker judges the social and cognitive sciences still mostly "Darwin-free," treating evolution as irrelevant or speculative (file page 17). Evolutionary psychology, named by John Tooby and Leda Cosmides, joins cognitive science's account of how thought is possible with evolutionary biology's account of why we have this mind (file pages 17–18).

**Qualifications.** Pinker lists premises often misread: computation does not make the commercial computer a good metaphor; modules are not encapsulated boxes or brain patches; genetic organization does not mean a gene per trait or that learning is unimportant; adaptation does not make every act adaptive; human minds differ from apes'; and gene propagation is not people's ultimate goal (file page 18). He declines to dwell on neurons and hormones because mind is information processing, not metabolism (file page 18).

**Computation and meaning.** Information and computation are patterns and logical relations independent of medium: a telephone message survives changes of physical form, and a program runs on tubes, switches, or pigeons (file page 18). This computational theory of mind, credited to Alan Turing, Alan Newell, Herbert Simon, Marvin Minsky, Hilary Putnam, and Jerry Fodor, lets beliefs and desires be configurations of symbols—physical states triggered by the world through sense organs and by each other—so meaning can cause and be caused (file pages 18–19). Bill boards the bus because he wants to visit his grandmother and knows the route; change either and the behavior changes (file page 18).

Uniform-looking cortex does not imply uniform programs: content lies in connection and activity patterns, as books lie in ink marks (file page 19). Tooby and Cosmides' list of species-specific feats (star navigation, echolocation, speech, farming) illustrates that neural tissue can embody many distinct programs (file page 19). Yet substrate matters: silicon and neural circuits differ in speed and pattern capacity, so psychology must meet neurobiology partway (file page 19). The computer metaphor is rejected; brains are parallel, slow, noisy, self-assembling (file page 19).

**Modules and vision.** Without computation, flexible human behavior seems to escape evolution; but a program of tests, branches, loops, and subroutines can be subtle, and biology's imperative is "If… then… else" (file page 20). Pinker claims the mind is many organs, not one; general intelligence, culture, and multipurpose learning will fade like protoplasm (file page 20). Specialization runs deep, as heart and lung tissues differ (file page 20). Vision illustrates the need: inverse optics—inferring objects and materials from the retinal image—is ill-posed, with no unique solution (file page 20).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For an inspectable non-neural machine, the text supports layered perceptual subsystems (form, location, identity, motion), symbol structures whose causal roles give meaning, and specialized procedures rather than one general learner. It does not supply algorithms for grounding, composition, memory, time, causation, goals, analogy, creativity, emotion, or social cognition; those remain open design problems. Innate capacities here mean genetically specified module logic, distinct from acquired knowledge; the section gives no learning laws.
