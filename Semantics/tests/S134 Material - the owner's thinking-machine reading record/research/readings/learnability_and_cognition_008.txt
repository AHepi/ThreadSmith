**Subject and central claims.** This section spans the end of Chapter 2 and the opening of Chapter 3 of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition*, on constraints governing verb argument-structure alternations (passive, dative, causative, locative). Pinker argues that children's productive rules are constrained by semantic criteria — a gradient of passivizability, for instance, with agent-subject actional verbs most passivizable and perception verbs intermediate — and that such criteria are psychologically real in children and adults, though applied less consistently by children (file page 87). The criteria-governed productivity theory, he says, fits three facts: alternations are neither across-the-board nor arbitrary but criterion-governed; experiments show the criteria constrain generalization; and the theory offers a route out of Baker's paradox (the puzzle of how children learn restrictions never explicitly taught) (file page 87).

**Three problems.** First, exceptions abound. Positive exceptions (verbs that alternate despite failing the criterion, e.g. "xeroxed me a copy," "The audience was bored by the movie") can be tolerated if learned conservatively, verb by verb from positive evidence, and if rare enough not to discredit the criteria — but Pinker calls these "escape hatches" whose truth is hard to assess (file pages 87–88). Negative exceptions (verbs that should alternate but do not, e.g. "*John pulled Bill the box," "*I poured the glass with water") are fatal in his view: they require negative evidence and so "bring Baker's paradox back in full force," since even one such verb demands a novel mechanism (file pages 88–90). Post hoc re-description of a verb's meaning to save a criterion would defeat the criterion's purpose, which is to let the child predict alternability from form or meaning alone (file page 89). Second, Fodor (1985) notes an unconstrained rule is simpler, easier to learn, and more expressive (Pinker's example: "He donated it that priceless Vermeer…"), so why children and languages retain constraints is unexplained (file pages 90–91). Third, the criteria are a "motley collection" (metrical feet, prospective possession, directness, holism, thematic hierarchy); the candidate list must be finite and small for learning to succeed, yet no explicit list is forthcoming, and why particular criteria pair with particular rules is unexplained (file page 91).

**The proposed resolution.** Pinker argues criteria are epiphenomena of general principles of argument structure, to be derived from answers to what makes a possible verb, how verbs link to syntax, when two verbs share a root, and when a verb may enter a language (file pages 91–92). In Chapter 3 he reframes lexical rules as semantic operations: a rule alters a verb's semantic structure, and linking rules automatically map the new meaning to a new argument structure (file pages 94–95). Dativization converts "cause X to go to Y" into "cause Y to have X"; hence "drive Chicago the car" fails because driving cannot cause possession (file page 96). Causativization adds "cause"; locativization converts motion into a state change of container or surface; passivization recasts "X acts on Y" as "Y is in the circumstance of X acting on it" (file pages 96–97). Semantic incompatibility, not a stipulated filter, blocks application. Which verbs admit a construal is fixed by lexical subclasses sharing a semantic plan: ballistic verbs like *throw* permit "cause to have" and dativize, continuous-force verbs like *pull* do not (file page 97). Evidence that constraints attach to argument structures themselves, not just alternations, comes from non-alternating verbs obeying the same conditions: double-object verbs like *bet* and *cost* require a possessor first object, lexical causatives like *kill* entail direct causation, and *with*-locatives like *fill* entail complete affectedness (file pages 98–99).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests representing meanings as structured predicate frames with typed roles (cause, theme, goal, possessor, location), and implementing "rules" as meaning transformations whose outputs are checked for semantic coherence rather than matched against memorized exception lists. Permitted scope would follow from compositional constraints on role types (e.g. only entities capable of possession can be goals of a "have" relation), distinguishing genuine productivity from rote storage. The subclass idea (ballistic versus continuous force) models how perception of manner of causation could ground abstract categories. However, the text addresses only verb morphology-syntax learning; it supplies no account of perception, memory over time, analogy, creativity, emotion, or social cognition, and does not establish universal learning laws.
