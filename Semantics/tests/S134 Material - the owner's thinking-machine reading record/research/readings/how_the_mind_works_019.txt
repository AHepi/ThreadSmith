**Subject and central claims.** This section, spanning file pages 106 to 110, continues Steven Pinker's argument that natural selection can build sophisticated information-processing brains, then launches the subsection "Instinct and Intelligence." Pinker lists developmental mechanisms by which genes could shape neural hardware: numbers of proliferative units and cortical areas, axon guidance via chemical trails, molecular recognition between neurons, programmed cell death, and activity-dependent wiring, all interacting in cascades (file page 106). Selection judges only the resulting algorithms for perception, thought, and action, not elegance of construction (file page 106).

**Genetic algorithms and the Baldwin effect.** Pinker answers the objection that random variants would merely crash systems by citing genetic algorithms: populations of programs are copied with mutation and sexual recombination, and the fittest reproduce, sometimes exceeding human designs (file page 106). Applied to neural networks in simulated environments, evolving innate connection weights can beat learning alone, especially with multiple hidden layers, because back-propagated teaching signals are diluted while mutation reprograms hidden layers directly (file page 106). Evolution and learning can also be combined, evolving architecture, initial weights, and learning rates (file page 106). Geoffrey Hinton and Steven Nowlan's simulation shows learning guiding evolution: with twenty connections all needing correct settings, purely innate evolution fails, but making connections innately on, off, or learnable lets animals stumble onto correct settings, reproduce earlier, and drive more connections toward innateness, though never complete innateness, since selection pressure tapers off (file pages 106 to 107). This is the Baldwin effect, proposed by James Mark Baldwin and now demonstrated computationally; learning turns a needle-in-a-haystack search into guided search (file page 107). Pinker notes learning is ancient, present in fruit flies and sea slugs, so it could scaffold specialized circuits (file page 107).

**Against the associationist ladder.** Pinker rejects the view that intelligence climbs from reflexes through association to human idea-linking (file pages 107 to 108). The Tunisian desert ant, studied by Rudiger Wehner, computes path integration—integrating velocity over time to recover nest direction and distance, as Randy Gallistel explains with a marine navigation analogy (file pages 108 to 109). Such computation is cheap and unconscious, surfacing only as a feeling of home direction (file page 108). Migratory birds learn the celestial pole by watching star rotation in the nest, because precession would obsolete innate knowledge (file page 108). Honeybees communicate direction and distance relative to the sun, compensating for solar motion and using polarized light when cloudy (file pages 108 to 109). Other cases include optimal foraging, sun ephemeris learning, barn owl sound localization, and Clark's Nutcracker caching ten thousand seeds; even Pavlovian and operant conditioning are complex time-series prediction, not generic association (file page 109).

**Specialization and human uniqueness.** Brains are precision instruments tailored to lifestyles; species form a bush, not a ladder, so there is no generic animal intelligence to scale up (file page 109). Mammalian brains share a plan but differ in cortical areas—twenty or fewer in rats, fifty or more in humans—and in specialized expansions: visual areas in monkeys, sonar areas in bats, hippocampus in caching mice (file page 109). Human brains are three times too large for body size, with prolonged fetal growth, shrunken olfactory and primary visual areas, expanded complex-form, language, and prefrontal regions, marked asymmetry, and functional takeovers such as Broca's area (file page 110). Yet gross shape is not the point; connection patterns matter, and human microcircuitry is largely unknown (file page 110). Pinker concludes that human flexibility comes from more instincts, not fewer—Darwin's "instinct to acquire an art"—with William James's examples of human instinctive preferences (file page 110).

**Limits and uncertainty.** The text is argumentative, not a design spec; simulations are illustrative, and human neural code is explicitly unknown (file page 110). **Engineering implications (distinct from Pinker).** For a thinking machine, the section suggests: developmental cascades and selective pruning as buildable assembly procedures; genetic algorithms plus learning as a search strategy for architecture and parameters; explicit path-integration-style accumulators rather than associative links; specialized, inspectable modules for navigation, timing, caching, and prediction; and innate scaffolding that biases learning without hard-coding everything. It does not supply grounded semantics, compositionality, memory indexing, causal models, goal management, analogy, or creativity mechanisms, nor does it justify a non-neural implementation or claim consciousness. Narrow animal and language-learning results should not be universalized into learning laws.
