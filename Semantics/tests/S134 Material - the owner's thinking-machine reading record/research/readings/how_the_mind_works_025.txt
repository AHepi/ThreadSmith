**Subject and central claims.** This section, from Steven Pinker's *How the Mind Works*, explains binocular ("cyclopean") stereo vision as a paradigm of modular mental computation, then turns to shading and shape. The correspondence problem is a chicken-and-egg riddle: disparity cannot be measured before marks are matched, yet knowing distance would make matching trivial (file page 138, printed 234). David Marr's rescue is to restrict matching to physically plausible pairings using assumptions about the evolved world (file page 138).

**The three constraints.** First, each world mark occupies one position on one surface, so a black dot matches a black dot, not a white one; autostereograms violate this by repeating splotches (file page 138). Second, one dot matches at most one dot per eye, though Pinker notes the subtlety: transparent lake bottoms are permitted; only exact coincidental alignment of two identical visible splotches is ruled out (file page 138). Third, matter is cohesive and smooth, so neighboring lines of sight hit nearby depths; object edges violate this but occupy little of the visual field. The assumption excludes dust storms, gnats, fine wires, deep crevasses, and beds of nails viewed point-on (file page 138).

**Constraint satisfaction.** Because parts cannot be solved one at a time, the mind keeps several guesses per part and retains mutually consistent ones, like a crossword solved with pencil and eraser (file page 138, printed 235). Marr and Tomaso Poggio built a constraint network: input units carry dot color, an array holds all n × n possible matchups, and a lit unit guesses a splotch at a depth (file pages 138–139). Matching colors enforce assumption one; units along one line of sight inhibit each other (assumption two); neighboring points at nearby depths excite each other (assumption three). Activations reverberate until a depth contour stabilizes (file page 139). Pinker reads this as consistent with parallel interconnected processors and with the gradual emergence of the hidden figure in a random-dot stereogram, noting dozens of iterations run beneath consciousness (file page 139).

**Explicit limits.** The model is only a flavor of the real circuitry. In artificial worlds violating uniqueness and smoothness, people see better than the model predicts, so the brain must use more information (file page 139). Real scenes contain diagonals, T's, zigzags, and inkblots that sharply reduce false matches. Leonardo's occlusion clue also matters: a pen clip visible to one eye marks a boundary. Ken Nakayama and Shinsuke Shimojo showed people see a floating square defined by dots present in only one eye's view, and they with Martin Anderson propose neurons detecting such occlusions as three-dimensional boundary detectors (file page 139, printed 237).

**Innate capacities versus acquired knowledge.** Stereo is not free with two eyes: about two percent of people are stereoblind despite normal monocular vision, and Whitman Richards's three disparity pools (at fixation, beyond, nearer) have counterparts in monkey and cat neurons, with deficits apparently genetic (file page 140, printed 238). Yet stereo is absent at birth and can be ruined by early cataracts or patches. Pinker reframes this as project scheduling: assembly continues after birth and requires information genes cannot predict (file page 140). Infants suddenly take to stereograms near three to four months, when convergence and rivalry appear. Richard Held's explanation is that newborn cortical neurons sum both eyes' inputs; only when neurons pick a favorite eye can disparity be computed, and downstream stereo circuitry is already installed (file page 140, printed 239).

**Critical periods.** David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel found that covering one eye in kittens or monkeys during a critical period permanently shifts cortex to the open eye, while covering both causes far less damage—evidence of competition for cortical real estate, sharpened even by internally generated activity, not simple "learning" (file page 141, printed 240). Strabismus or unequal refraction causes amblyopia (brain suppression, not a lazy eye); early surgery matters because alignment sensitivity fades near one to two years (file page 141). The critical period tracks growing eye separation that genes cannot specify; precocious rabbits are wired at birth (file page 141, printed 241). Pinker generalizes: learning may be an innate adaptation to self-assembly, turning on information-gathering when needed, and he links this to language critical periods in *The Language Instinct* (file pages 141–142). He cautions that narrow language results are not universal learning laws.

**Modularity and semantics.** Stereo is a module: it works without object recognition, the rest of the mind works without it, it demands specific wiring, and it embodies binocular parallax geometry. It solves unsolvable problems by tacit assumptions true in our evolutionary past but not always now (file page 142, printed 242).

**Shading and shape.** Edward Adelson's drawings show identical ink read as folded white cardboard with gray stripes under different lighting; identical borders look like paint boundaries or shadows depending on zig versus zag (file pages 142–143, printed 243). Three image-making laws each need a mental "expert." Perspective makes any projection compatible with infinitely many objects, so the shape analyzer plays odds (file page 143). Bayes' theorem combines prior probability of a hypothesis with likelihood of the evidence; the analyzer favors objects likely to produce the lines and likely in scenes, assuming the world "subtle but not malicious" (file page 143, printed 244). It assumes generic viewpoints: a penny usually projects an ellipse, not an edge-on line (file page 143).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** A thinking machine could implement explicit constraint networks with excitatory and inhibitory links, plus Bayesian priors over generative scene models, to build grounded surface representations from ambiguous input. Occlusion detectors and multi-cue experts (stereo, shading, perspective) suggest modular perceptual front ends feeding shared world models. Developmental scheduling suggests staged acquisition of tunable parameters with bounded, task-specific calibration windows rather than undifferentiated learning. None of this demonstrates general intelligence, memory, time, causation, goals, reasoning, analogy, creativity, emotion, or social cognition—absent here. The section is not bibliography or index.
