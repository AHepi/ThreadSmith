**Subject and central claims.** The section first argues that abstract thought is built by adapting concrete mental forms. Metaphors such as "ideas are food" are ubiquitous; Steven Pinker claims the mind does not natively think in arbitrary logical variables but inherits "a pad of forms" capturing encounters among objects and forces, then blanks out contents and refills them with new symbols for abstract domains (file page 210). Some adaptations are evolved—ownership, time, and will derived from intuitive physics—while others arise during a life of learning (file page 210). Even scientific reasoning reuses "down-home mental metaphors": biology borrows artifact understanding, chemistry treats essences as tiny bouncy sticky objects, mathematics treats functions as shapes and operations as mental doodling (file page 210). Children's beliefs that a rice grain weighs nothing or that endlessly divided steel vanishes show that mature matter concepts come from merging object intuitions with number intuitions (file pages 210–211).

**Compositionality.** Educated understanding is "an enormous contraption of parts within parts": basic models are copied, bleached of content, connected, and packaged recursively without limit, so combinatorial and recursive thought explores vast knowledge with a finite toolkit (file page 211). This is the section's clearest statement about representation and composition.

**Creativity.** Against the Romantic image of the genius struck by unconscious bolts, Pinker reports that historical study of diaries and manuscripts finds geniuses are "wonks": typically ten years of apprenticeship, tens of thousands of absorbed problems and solutions, competition-watching, hard work, much subgenius output, rest rather than unconscious incubation, "creative worrying," and endless revision of earlier attempts (file pages 211–212). Genius may also involve genetic luck, but creators are not alien minds; combinatorial adapted minds produce good ideas generally (file page 212).

**Emotions as cognitive and universal.** The Dunblane massacre and the Malay "amok" syndrome illustrate that rampage is triggered by an idea—a coldly logical calculus of lost face and exchange of worthless life for others'—not a stimulus or random chemistry (file page 213). Pinker argues emotions are universal across cultures: Darwin's worldwide questionnaire and Paul Ekman's photo studies, including with isolated Fore foragers, found recognition of happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise, corroborated by blind-deaf children's expressions (file pages 214–215). Controversies remain over which expressions are universal, needed context, and reflex strength (file page 215). Claims of emotionless cultures rest on untrustworthy language and opinion: vocabulary reflects translation skill and lexical history, not feeling; foreign emotion words like Schadenfreude are instantly grasped (file page 215). Apparent exotic triggers reduce to shared categories—contaminant versus nutrient, disrespect—yielding disgust or anger (file pages 215–216). Native informants' denials are contradicted by behavior (Utku-Inuit, Samoan evidence against Mead) (file page 216). Cultures differ mainly in public display rules: Japanese students masked horror in front of an experimenter but showed it alone (file page 216). The section closes by setting up a critique of the Romantic split between hot emotion and cool intellect (file page 216), but the text cuts off mid-sentence; the "Feeling Machines" argument is incomplete.

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** A thinking machine could implement metaphor as structure-mapping: store schemas of force, containment, possession; erase and rebind slots to new domains; compose recursively. Learning would merge schemas (objects plus number) and revise them. Emotion modules could tag goals, losses, and social standing, driving planning and action selection, with separate display policies. Creativity suggests search over accumulated solution repertoires with incremental revision, not a mysterious oracle. None of this demonstrates human-level intelligence or consciousness; the source leaves perception, memory indexing, temporal representation, and constraint mechanisms underspecified.
