This section is the conclusion of Chapter 8 of Steven Pinker's *Learnability and Cognition*, covering argument structure, semantic bootstrapping, and lexical conservatism. It is not bibliography or index material.

**Source claims: semantics and conceptual structure.** Pinker argues that the semantic representations of words and sentences are distinct from cognitive or conceptual representations of categories, events, scenes, and propositions, though they may draw on overlapping primitive vocabularies (file page 445). Evidence comes from verb classes governing alternations like the dative: the cuts that English makes (for example, grouping *telling* with *quoting* and *writing* but not *saying* or *shouting*) are semantic, since they track situations where verbs can be used, yet they do not follow psychologically natural kinds of events (file pages 444–445). Sentence meaning is therefore a "highly schematic construal," an austere idealization built from foundational notions such as causation, motion, and change, not a rich model of a particular event (file page 445). The same situation must be mapped onto one of several possible idealizations before it can be worded: *spraying* can construe causing water to move or causing a wall to be wet (file page 445). Pinker holds that language does not determine conceptualization generally (against strong Whorfian claims) but does constrain how reality must be construed for speech (file page 445).

**Semantic bootstrapping revised.** Children are proposed to use innate expectations linking semantics to syntax—thing-word to noun, predicate-argument to head-phrase, agent to subject—to make a first syntactic analysis, then learn exceptions like passives distributionally (file pages 445–446). Pinker lists problems: accurate encoding of adult meaning from context, non-universal syntax-semantics correlations, and probabilistic correlations requiring filtering (file page 446). His revised three-stage model inserts conceptual structure between perception and linguistic semantics: children construe situations conceptually, parents choose words whose semantic representations match the child's construal, and formal linking rules (possibly universal, near-exceptionless) map semantics to syntax (file pages 447–450). This trades one extra link for simpler assumptions, shifting burden from filtered input to parent-child construal alignment (file pages 448–450). Gropen and colleagues' experiments support predictable child construals of events as motion versus state change (file pages 447, 450).

**Conservatism and listedness.** A verb-argument pairing heard in input is listed and sounds natural; an unattested pairing formed by a broad-range rule sounds merely possible; a pairing licensed by a narrow-range rule because the verb meaning closely matches listed verbs sounds actual (file page 450). Grammars generate possible forms but accept them "grudgingly," reflecting the conventional, memorized nature of sound-meaning pairings (file pages 450–451). Following Di Sciullo and Williams, the lexicon lists unpredictable objects of any type; morphology and syntax are productive rule systems (file pages 451–452). Pinker dissents: unlisted rule-made morphological forms (*graciosity*, *golder*) feel ungrammatical while novel syntactic forms feel fine, and Di Sciullo and Williams' degree hierarchy leaves actuality unexplained (file pages 452–453). Root inventories are learned conservatively; nonce roots like *pilk* are easy to invent but children overwhelmingly adopt adult roots, and historical neologisms are rare (file page 453).

**Engineering implications (mine, not Pinker's).** For a thinking machine, this suggests separating a schematic, compositional semantic layer (causation, motion, change, argument roles) from richer world models, with explicit linking rules between them. Generalization should be scope-limited: a rule licenses a construction only within a defined semantic class, and attested forms should be marked as memorized versus merely generable, echoing the possible/actual distinction. The bootstrapping chain implies staged grounding: perceptual construal, then language-specific encoding, then structural inference—each an inspectable module. Innate versus acquired: Pinker posits innate linking expectations and possibly universal linking principles, while lexical roots and language-specific idealizations are learned. None of this demonstrates human-level intelligence or consciousness; it is a theory of language acquisition, and narrow verb-alternation results should not be generalized into universal learning laws. Topics like memory over time, goals, analogy, creativity, emotion, and social cognition are not treated here.
