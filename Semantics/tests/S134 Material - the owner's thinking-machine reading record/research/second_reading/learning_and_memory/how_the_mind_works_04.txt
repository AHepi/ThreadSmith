## Engineering proposals, clearly marked as ours

The following are my proposals, not claims by Pinker, and they are extensions of what earlier sections of this reading project established rather than findings from the present pages.

First, the machine should implement a memory architecture that separates episodic records from generalized knowledge while allowing controlled transfer between them. Each episodic record should bind participants, roles, spatial locations, temporal order, and outcome into a single retrievable unit. Generalized knowledge should be stored as typed relational structures that can be instantiated by new episodes and revised when episodes repeatedly contradict them. The transfer operation should be explicit and inspectable: when a set of episodes shares a common structure, the system should extract that structure into a reusable schema, retaining pointers back to the source episodes so that the schema can be audited and retracted.

Second, the machine should implement a rule-plus-exception architecture for classification and productivity. A general rule should be stored as a productive operation over structured inputs, and individual exceptions should be stored as specific entries that override the rule when matched. This allows the system to handle both regular generalization and item-specific knowledge without collapsing one into the other. For the circle-versus-square demonstration, the system should learn a geometric criterion from positive and negative examples, store that criterion as a reusable rule, and also store specific borderline cases as exceptions that can trigger rule revision when they accumulate.

Third, the machine should implement a temporal indexing scheme that preserves the order and duration of observed events. Each observation should carry a timestamp or sequence position, and the system should support queries that retrieve events by relative order, by participant, and by outcome. This is necessary for combining partial observations across time: if a shape is seen from one angle at one moment and from another angle at a later moment, the system must be able to bind both observations to the same object hypothesis and use them jointly.

Fourth, the machine should implement a hypothesis revision mechanism that treats absence of failure as weak evidence rather than as confirmation. When a rule has been applied and no counterexample has appeared, the system should record the number of applications and the diversity of contexts in which the rule was tested, and should lower its confidence only when a genuine counterexample is found. An unseen failure is not evidence of correctness; it is evidence only that the failure has not yet been observed.

## Mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is to treat the index or reference list as if it were argument. Doing so would attribute claims to Pinker that the present pages do not contain.

The second mistake is to extrapolate from narrow grammatical findings, such as the regular-and-irregular morphology of verb inflection, to a universal law of learning. The reference to Marcus and Pinker on German inflection at file page 385 indicates that such work was cited, but morphological paradigms are a specific domain with specific structural properties. A learning mechanism that works for verb forms does not automatically generalize to spatial categories, causal reasoning, or social knowledge.

The third mistake is to treat the absence of a counterexample as proof that a generalization is correct. A machine that has classified many circles and squares without error may simply not yet have encountered a rounded square or a polygon with many sides. Confidence should be calibrated to the diversity and volume of tested cases, not to the mere fact of no observed failure.

The fourth mistake is to equate a proposed architecture with demonstrated human intelligence or consciousness. The design proposals here concern control structures for learning and memory. They do not establish sentience, understanding, or phenomenal experience.

The fifth mistake is to assume that the non-neural, inspectable implementation constraint is something Pinker argued for. He discussed computational and connectionist models as scientific hypotheses about human cognition. Our choice to build an inspectable symbolic machine is an engineering decision, not a conclusion drawn from his text.

## Unresolved questions

The present section supplies no new theoretical content, so the unresolved questions remain those identified in earlier readings, now sharpened by the apparatus.

The reference list indicates that explanation-based learning and causal pattern learning were part of the conversation (file page 391), but the body text that would specify how Pinker used these ideas is not in the assigned pages. A reader with access to the "Good Ideas" chapter should determine whether Pinker endorsed explanation-based learning as a model of human induction or merely cited it as a contrast case.

The reference to Sherry and Schacter on multiple memory systems (file page 398) suggests that the episodic-semantic distinction was discussed, but the mechanism of consolidation from one store to the other is not specified in the present pages. The next reader should look for any account of how repeated episodes become generalized knowledge and how generalized knowledge constrains the encoding of new episodes.

The reference to Elman on finding structure in time (file page 369) indicates that sequential learning was in scope, but the format of temporal representation is not given here. The next reader should determine whether Pinker specified how order, duration, and simultaneity are encoded, or whether he left this to the cited models.

The index entry for "Time, concept of, 352–354" at file page 449 points to a discussion of temporal metaphors in the chapter on good ideas, but the present pages do not contain that discussion. The next reader should determine whether temporal reasoning is treated as a metaphorical mapping from spatial primitives or as an independent representational domain.

Finally, the question of how a learner selects among competing generalization formats remains open. The index shows entries for categorization, fuzzy categories, essentialism, and natural kinds, indicating that multiple classification schemes were discussed, but the selection criterion is not recoverable from the apparatus alone.
