# Research Memorandum: Learning, Generalization, and Temporal Memory

**Reader role:** Learning, Generalization, and Temporal Memory
**Source:** Steven Pinker, *Learnability and Cognition: The Acquisition of Argument Structure*, file pages 164 through 318
**Scope note:** This section continues chapter 4 from the middle of the discussion of the morphological constraint on the English dative, covers the locative, causative, and passive alternations in detail, closes chapter 4 with the distinction between property-predicting and existence-predicting rules, presents chapter 5 on semantic representation in full, and begins chapter 6 on learning at file page 312, stopping in the middle of the discussion of syntactic ergativity at file page 318. The section therefore contains the most explicit representational machinery in the book so far and the opening moves of the learning theory, but it does not contain the core of chapter 6, where Pinker addresses how children actually acquire narrow-range rules and verb meanings. I report what is specified here and flag what remains deferred.

---

## What the learner must acquire, stated precisely

Pinker opens chapter 6 by distinguishing two problems. Once a speaker possesses correct verb definitions, narrow-range lexical rules, and linking rules, productive extension to new argument structures is no longer paradoxical (file page 312). The unsolved problem is how children arrive at those structures. He lists what must be learned: linking rules, conflation class definitions at both broad and narrow levels, broad-range lexical rules, narrow-range lexical rules, and the semantic structures of individual verbs (file page 312). He identifies the last two as crucial, because the fine-grained selectivity that produces Baker's paradox is localized there.

He proposes a hierarchy of mutual constraint (file pages 312 through 313). Linking rules sit at the top and are essentially innate. Conceptual structures for particular kinds of events and states sit at the bottom and are formed by perception of the situations in which verbs are used. Between them, narrow conflation classes are generalizations over individual verb entries; narrow-range rules map one narrow class onto another and are specific cases of broad-range rules; broad-range rules are generalizations over sets of narrow-range rules or alternating entries and can also be motivated by running linking rules backward from input sentences. This is the closest thing in the section to a learning architecture, and it is genuinely informative: it tells us where learning starts (top and bottom), what constrains what, and which structures are the hard cases.

## Innate structure and the circularity problem

Pinker argues that linking rules can be innate because they are simple and circumscribed (file page 313). Most variation in how thematic roles map onto syntactic arguments is localized in semantic structures rather than in the linking rules themselves. He acknowledges two problems. First, linking rules are a consistency-enforcing link between two kinds of structures that must each be learned, so vicious circles are possible: phrase structure might be acquired by applying linking rules forward from lexical entries, while lexical entries are acquired by applying linking rules backward from independently identified phrase structures (file pages 314 through 315). He notes the danger is not perpetual confusion but stable, mutually compatible, incorrect structures.

He offers two ways to break the circle (file pages 315 through 316). The first is sequencing: early input may consist of verbs whose semantics match the child's cognitive representation of the event, allowing correct phrase structure assignment first, with later noncanonical verbs restructured to fit. The second is constraint satisfaction: the child combines multiple partially predictive cues to grammatical function, including linear order, position in phrase structure, agreement marking, coreference patterns, and prosody, with satisfaction of universal linking rules as one cue among several. He mentions a symbol-passing networklike architecture as a possible implementation (file page 316) but does not develop it here.

The second problem is that linking rules are not fully universal. Syntactically ergative languages link patients to subject role (file page 317). Pinker notes these are rare, under five percent of languages, and offers a partial account in terms of the mapping problem from four thematic distinctions to two case distinctions (file pages 317 through 318), but the discussion is cut off at the section boundary. This is an important qualification: the innate end of the hierarchy is not exceptionless.

## The representational machinery that learning must target

Chapter 5 is the most substantial contribution of this section to the design problem. Pinker contrasts two hypotheses about lexical semantics (file pages 214 through 216). On the Unrestricted Conceptual Representation hypothesis, verb meanings are ordinary cognitive categories and any culturally salient distinction could define similarity classes relevant to syntax. On the Grammatically Relevant Subsystem hypothesis, verb meanings are organized around a much smaller set of semantic elements, and linguistic processes attend only to those. He argues the second is more satisfying because it makes the learning problem tractable: characterizing the learner's possible hypotheses is the first step to characterizing learning mechanisms, and a constrained hypothesis space is a better explanation of learning success (file page 216).

He then assembles converging evidence for the subsystem hypothesis (file pages 218 through 223): the semantic distinctions that govern syntactic behavior are subtle and do not correspond to ordinary cognitive similarity classes; certain elements like motion, causation, and contact recur across languages and across alternations; these elements appear in closed-class morphemes; verb meanings vary across languages in ways suggesting composition rather than unanalyzed labeling; verbs show statistical and psycholinguistic profiles intermediate between nouns and closed-class items; and neurolinguistic evidence suggests verb meanings share machinery with grammar.

The inventory itself (file pages 223 through 226 and summarized at file pages 264 through 266) includes conceptual constituents such as event, state, thing, path, place, property, and manner; functions expanding these, including the predicates defined by the dynamic and control features; place-functions and path-functions; features of subordinating causal relations including cause-focus versus effect-focus, success versus failure, occurrence versus nonoccurrence, purposive, and deontic; object properties including animacy, humanness, dimensionality, count versus mass, rigidity, substance versus aggregate, and liquid versus semisolid; temporal objects including a time-line with points and regions; and mechanisms including open arguments, coindexing, semantic field annotation, and quoted constants for manners and idiosyncratic properties.

This is directly relevant to the thinking-machine problem. It is a concrete proposal for what a structured, inspectable representation of event meaning could look like, with a small vocabulary of reusable parts and explicit slots for idiosyncratic content. It is not a claim that this is the only possible format, and Pinker is explicit that the system has indeterminacies and is not a formal theory (file page 310).

## Temporal representation and role binding

This section contains more on temporal structure than the previous one, though still less than the design problem requires. Pinker adopts a time-line representation as a separate tier in semantic structure (file page 262). States and processes are regions with no distinct boundaries; instantaneous events are points; accomplishments are regions bounded at their ends by points; achievements are points bounding the end of a region. Events must precede or be simultaneous with their effect and but-subordinate events, and follow their cause and despite-subordinate events (file page 263). Purposive substructures are interpreted as goals that precede the events they are subordinate to.

He notes that the temporal representation is partly redundant with the causal and spatial structure, and that Tenny has proposed the temporal representation is the true interface with argument structure (file page 263). Pinker declines to eliminate either, noting that some verb pairs contrast in alternation behavior without obvious aspectual differences. This is a real open question: how much of event structure is temporal and how much is causal or spatial.

Role binding is handled by coindexing within semantic structures (file pages 251 through 252). An argument can occupy multiple thematic positions, but only one is the primary position marked for linking to syntax; other positions are coindexed. This is a static structural mechanism, not a temporal one, but it is directly relevant to a machine that must bind participants to roles across partial observations.

## Learning updates and generalization

The most concrete learning procedure remains the four-step outline from the previous section, and this section does not supersede it. What it adds is the target representation. Pinker notes that lexical rules are probably composed of elementary operations on parts of semantic structures, such as adding or deleting a cause argument, making a patient a theme of a predication, embedding an act as the means of an effect, adding or deleting path or purpose or instrument arguments, assigning patient role to an embedded argument, and suppressing nonspecific arguments (file page 267). He explicitly says that laying out a precise set of operations and decomposing lexical rules into them is beyond the scope of the investigation. This is a significant gap: we have the input and output structures but not the elementary operations that transform one into the other.

On generalization, Pinker makes an important claim about the status of narrow classes. He notes that conflation subclasses can contain any number of verbs, perhaps even one, and suggests this means subclass-defined generalizations are not licensed by statistical averaging over large numbers of similarly behaving verbs, but that each alternating verb defines a generalizable region in semantic space around it (file page 171). This is a striking proposal: productivity is not a threshold phenomenon driven by frequency, but a structural property of the representation. He defers the exploration to chapters 5 and 6.

The distinction between property-predicting and existence-predicting rules (file pages 208 through 213) is central to the learning problem. Broad-range rules are psychologically real but only property-predicting: they tell you what a form would have to be like if it existed, but do not license adding it to the language. Only narrow-range rules are existence-predicting. Pinker argues this reflects a deep conservatism in the lexicon: the default is not to add new entries freely, and this default is abandoned only for words minimally different from existing ones (file page 210). He calls this classwise conservatism, as opposed to the itemwise conservatism he rejected in chapter 1. For a learning machine, this is a direct design constraint: generalization should be conservative by default, with productivity licensed only within well-defined narrow classes.

## Positive and negative evidence

This section does not revisit the negative evidence problem in detail, but it sharpens the picture. The Haigspeak examples (file pages 199 through 208) show that adults occasionally produce forms that obey broad-range rules but violate narrow-range ones. These are not random errors; they are perceived as possible but nonactual English. Pinker argues this shows broad-range rules are part of adult competence, but that only narrow-range rules license automatic extension (file page 209). For a learning machine, this means the absence of a form in the input is not by itself evidence of ungrammaticality, but the machine should distinguish between forms it has licensed productively and forms it can recognize as structurally possible but unattested.

## Engineering proposals, clearly marked as ours

The following are my proposals, not claims by Pinker.

First, the machine should implement the hierarchy of mutual constraint described at file pages 312 through 313 as an actual dependency structure. When a new observation is encoded, the machine should attempt to fit it at each level: conceptual structure from perception, semantic structure of the individual item, membership in narrow and broad classes, and consistency with linking rules. Failure at one level should propagate as a constraint on the others, not as a silent error.

Second, the machine should implement the time-line representation as a separate tier in its event records. Each event record should bind participants to roles, specify causal subordination relations among subevents, and locate each subevent as a point or region on a temporal axis. This extends Pinker's semantic machinery to the domain of episodic memory, which the source does not address.

Third, the machine should distinguish required features from typical features in its representations, following the autonomy of lexical semantics discussed in the previous section and reinforced here by the contrast between cognitive similarity and linguistic similarity (file page 219). For the circle-versus-square test, this means representing the geometric conditions as required, not merely typical, and checking borderline cases against those conditions rather than against a similarity metric.

Fourth, the machine should implement classwise conservatism. When a new item is encountered, it should be checked against existing narrow class definitions. If it matches, it inherits the syntactic privileges of that class. If it does not match, it should be stored item by item without productive extension, until enough evidence accumulates to motivate a new narrow class or a revision of an existing one.

Fifth, the machine should represent generalizations with pointers back to the source observations, so that a generalization can be audited and retracted when contradicted. This is an extension of Pinker's constraint revision procedure to a persistent memory system.

## Mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is to treat the semantic inventory as a complete theory of meaning. Pinker is explicit that quoted constants carry idiosyncratic conceptual content that is opaque to grammatical rules (file pages 237 through 238), and that the system has many indeterminacies (file page 310). A machine that treats the inventory as exhaustive will fail on the very distinctions it is meant to capture.

The second mistake is to extrapolate from the English passive to a universal law of learning. Pinker argues the passive is unusual in having broad-range conditions that are both necessary and sufficient, and attributes this partly to affixation and partly to historical and functional pressures specific to English (file pages 193 through 198). This is a language-particular fact, not a general principle.

The third mistake is to treat the absence of a counterexample as confirmation. The Haigspeak data show that speakers produce forms violating narrow-range rules even when the broad-range rule is satisfied (file pages 208 through 209). A machine that has not encountered a negative exception has not proven its rule correct.

The fourth mistake is to assume that the elementary operations underlying lexical rules are specified. Pinker lists candidate operations (file page 267) but explicitly defers the decomposition. Any machine implementation of lexical rules is therefore an engineering hypothesis, not a claim grounded in this source.

The fifth mistake is to equate this architecture with human intelligence or consciousness. The proposals concern representational formats and learning procedures for linguistic generalization. They do not establish understanding, sentience, or general intelligence.

## Unresolved questions

The most important gap is that the core of chapter 6 is not in this section. We have the hierarchy of mutual constraint and the claim that linking rules are innate and conceptual structures are perceptually given, but not the mechanism by which children acquire narrow-range rules or verb semantic structures. The next reader should determine whether Pinker specifies how the learner detects shared properties, chooses among candidate constraints, and represents the semantic primitives as grounded.

The second gap is grounding. The semantic primitives are defined structurally and by their grammatical behavior, not by perceptual features. File page 313 says conceptual structures are formed by perception of the situations in which verbs are used, but does not say how. This is the central problem for a thinking machine and remains open.

The third gap is temporal memory. The time-line representation is a tier within a single verb's semantic structure, not a mechanism for encoding sequences of experience or combining partial observations across time. How episodic memory relates to generalized knowledge is not addressed.

The fourth gap is the elementary operations of lexical rules. Without these, we have input and output structures but no executable transformation. This is a concrete implementable problem that the source leaves open.

The fifth gap is the selection problem among candidate narrow classes. Pinker notes that each alternating verb defines a generalizable region in semantic space (file page 171), but does not specify how the boundaries of that region are determined or how competing generalizations are adjudicated.

The sixth gap is the status of syntactic ergativity and other violations of the proposed innate linking rules. The discussion is cut off at file page 318. The next reader should determine how Pinker handles languages that do not conform to the unmarked linking pattern, and what this implies for the innateness claim.
