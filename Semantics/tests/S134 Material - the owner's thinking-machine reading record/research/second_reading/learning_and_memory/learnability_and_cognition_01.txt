# Research Memorandum: Learning, Generalization, and Temporal Memory

**Reader role:** Learning, Generalization, and Temporal Memory
**Source:** Steven Pinker, *Learnability and Cognition: The Acquisition of Argument Structure*, file pages 1 through 163
**Scope note:** This section covers the front matter, the preface to the new edition, and chapters 1 through 4 up to the middle of section 4.4.1.1. It contains the full statement of Baker's paradox, the attempted solutions, the criteria-governed productivity proposal and its problems, the theory of thematic cores and linking rules, and the beginning of the narrow conflation class analysis. It does not contain chapter 6 on learning mechanisms or chapter 7 on development, which are the sections where Pinker most directly addresses acquisition procedures. I therefore report what is specified here and flag what is deferred.

---

## The learning problem stated precisely

Pinker frames acquisition as a logical problem, not merely a psychological one. A child hears a finite sample of sentences but must generalize to an open-ended language (file page 26). Infinitely many hypotheses fit any finite sample while differing from the target. The standard solution is that constraints on the learner restrict the hypothesis space so that the correct hypothesis is included and many incorrect ones are excluded, after which positive evidence can reject the remaining wrong guesses (file page 26). The critical asymmetry is this: if the child's hypothesis generates a superset of the target language, no amount of positive evidence can falsify it. Only negative evidence, information about which strings are ungrammatical, can do that (file page 27).

Pinker then assembles three facts into what he calls Baker's paradox. First, negative evidence is not reliably available. Brown and Hanlon found that parental approval does not track grammaticality (file page 27). Later studies found slight statistical differences in repetition or recasting, but these are noisy, inconsistent across children, decline sharply after age two, and are not diagnostic of whether the error is syntactic, phonological, semantic, or pragmatic (file pages 32 through 34). Pinker's own fieldwork documented children producing ungrammatical double-object sentences with no parental reaction at all (file page 33). Second, children are productive: they use verbs in argument structures they have never heard, as the extensive spontaneous-speech and elicitation evidence shows (file pages 42 through 55). Third, the distribution of verbs across argument structures appears arbitrary: near-synonyms like give and donate, or load and pour, differ in whether they alternate (file page 31).

The three factors together make acquisition seem impossible. Any solution must deny at least one of them. Pinker rejects subtle negative evidence, indirect negative evidence from nonoccurrence, and the Uniqueness postulate as insufficient (file pages 32 through 40). He rejects strict lexical conservatism, the hypothesis that children only use verbs in heard structures, on the basis of both spontaneous errors and controlled experiments with novel verbs (file pages 40 through 55). The remaining option is to reject arbitrariness: the verbs that alternate must be distinguishable by properties the learner can detect.

## What is specified about learning updates

The most concrete learning procedure in this section appears at file pages 81 through 82. Pinker outlines a four-step sequence. The learner records the argument structures of verbs heard in the input. When many verbs occur in the same two argument structures, the learner creates a productive lexical rule mapping one to the other. When many verbs occur in only one of the two forms, and the alternating verbs share a property that the nonalternating ones lack, the learner bifurcates the verbs into two classes and constrains the rule to apply only to the class possessing that property. The constraint is applied retroactively, expunging any previously generated forms that violate it. If a hypothesized constraint is falsified because many verbs violating it appear in the input, the learner searches for a new distinguishing property and replaces the old one.

This is a genuine learning update, and it is worth stating exactly what it does and does not require. It does not require negative evidence in the technical sense. It is sensitive to broad statistical patterns across the lexicon, not to the nonoccurrence of particular sentences (file page 82). It does require that the learner can detect shared properties among verbs, that the list of possible properties is finite and not too large, and that the learner can revise a constraint when it fails. Pinker acknowledges that these requirements are substantial. He notes that the criteria involved are heterogeneous, ranging from metrical foot count to prospective possession to directness of causation, and that an open-ended or very large list would make search intractable (file page 91). He also notes that the theory does not explain why particular criteria pair with particular rules (file page 91).

## The refinement: semantic operations rather than syntactic transformations

Pinker's central theoretical move in chapter 3 is to reinterpret lexical rules as operations on semantic structure rather than on syntactic argument structure (file pages 94 through 97). On the older view, a rule takes an argument structure and transforms it, with semantic criteria appended as filters. On the newer view, the rule changes the verb's meaning, and the new argument structure follows automatically via linking rules that map semantic arguments onto grammatical functions. Dativization converts "cause X to go to Y" into "cause Y to have X." Causativization converts "Y changes" into "cause Y to change." Locativization converts "cause X to go into Y" into "cause Y to change state by means of putting X into it." Passivization converts "X acts on Y" into "Y is in the circumstance of X acting on it" (file pages 95 through 97).

This matters for learning because it dissolves the apparent arbitrariness of the semantic constraints. The constraints are not arbitrary filters appended to rules; they are consequences of what the semantic operation requires. A verb cannot undergo dativization if its meaning cannot support a construal as causing possession. A verb cannot undergo the container locative if its meaning does not specify a predictable state change on the container (file pages 111 through 115). The learner's task is therefore not to memorize arbitrary lists but to determine which verbs can support which semantic construals.

However, Pinker is explicit that this gives necessary conditions, not sufficient ones (file pages 137 through 139). Many verbs are cognitively compatible with a semantic construal but still do not alternate. He gives the example of carry versus throw: both can result in a change of possession, but only throw dativizes (file page 141). The solution is the distinction between broad and narrow conflation classes. Broad-range rules define the necessary semantic conditions. Narrow-range rules pick out the specific subclasses of verbs that actually alternate. Membership in a broad class is necessary; membership in a narrow class is sufficient (file page 142).

## Narrow conflation classes and the autonomy of lexical semantics

The analysis of transitive action verbs at file pages 143 through 146 provides the clearest evidence for narrow classes. Verbs of hitting, cutting, breaking, and touching differ systematically in which alternations they enter. The conative alternation applies to verbs of motion followed by contact (hit, cut) but not to verbs of touching or breaking. The middle alternation applies to verbs specifying an effect (cut, break) but not to verbs of mere contact. The anticausative applies to verbs specifying an effect with no specification of cause (break) but not to verbs specifying motion or contact (file page 145).

Pinker's crucial claim is that what matters is not the typical or characteristic features of the events a verb can refer to, but the specific aspects of the event that the verb's semantic structure constrains (file pages 146 through 148). Kissing typically involves motion followed by contact, but the semantics of kiss do not require it. Breaking typically involves motion followed by contact, but the semantics of break do not require it. The semantic structure of a verb is mute about features that are merely typical, and this muteness is what determines syntactic behavior. Pinker calls this the autonomy of lexical semantics (file page 148).

This is directly relevant to the thinking-machine design problem. A machine that learns categories from statistical regularities in observed events will capture typicality. A machine that learns reusable distinctions for compositional reasoning must capture what is required versus what is merely characteristic. The source does not tell us how a learner comes to represent required features as distinct from typical ones. That is an open problem.

## Innate structure versus acquired knowledge

Pinker's position is neither blank-slate empiricism nor wholesale nativism. The learner brings constraints on the hypothesis space, linking rules that map semantic arguments to grammatical functions, and a set of possible semantic elements (cause, go, be, act, and their arguments) from which verb meanings are composed (file pages 107 through 110). What is acquired is the specific lexicon: which verbs have which meanings, which narrow classes they belong to, which rules apply to them. The cross-linguistic survey at file pages 131 through 135 suggests that the linking rules and the broad thematic cores are universal or near-universal, while the exact sets of verbs participating in each alternation vary by language.

For the thinking machine, this suggests a division of labor. The machine should come equipped with a fixed inventory of semantic primitives and linking rules, and should learn the specific mappings from experience. But the source does not specify how the semantic primitives themselves are grounded in perception or action. The thematic roles like agent, patient, and theme are defined by their positions in structured semantic representations, not by perceptual features (file pages 109 through 110). How a machine comes to associate these abstract positions with grounded observations is not addressed in this section.

## Temporal memory and role binding

This section contains almost nothing about temporal memory or episodic recall. The learning problem is framed in terms of accumulating lexical entries and generalizing across types, not in terms of remembering particular events in sequence. The argument structures bind semantic roles to grammatical functions, which is a form of role binding, but this is a static structural relation, not a temporal one. The examples of children's errors (file pages 42 through 52) are individual utterances, not sequences of experience.

The one relevant observation is Pinker's discussion of how a verb's semantic structure constrains certain aspects of an event and is mute about others (file page 147). For a machine that must combine partial observations across time, this suggests that the representation should specify what is required and leave open what is variable, so that later observations can fill in the unspecified slots. But this is my engineering inference, not a claim Pinker makes about temporal memory.

## Engineering proposals, clearly marked as ours

The following are my proposals, not claims by Pinker.

First, the machine should implement a two-level generalization architecture corresponding to Pinker's broad and narrow conflation classes. Broad rules should encode necessary semantic conditions for a transformation. Narrow rules should encode the specific feature combinations that license productive application. When a new item is encountered, the machine should check it against the narrow class definitions first, and fall back to conservative item-by-item storage when no narrow class matches. This prevents overgeneralization while preserving productivity.

Second, the machine should implement a constraint revision mechanism matching the four-step procedure at file pages 81 through 82. When a hypothesized constraint is contradicted by accumulating evidence, the machine should search for a new distinguishing property from a finite, pre-specified inventory. The inventory should be small enough to make search tractable and should be organized so that properties likely to be relevant to a given rule are checked first.

Third, the machine should distinguish required features from typical features in its representations. For the circle-versus-square test, this means representing "closed curve of constant curvature" as required for circle, not merely "round-looking," and representing "four straight edges meeting at right angles" as required for square. Borderline cases like rounded squares or ellipses should trigger a check against the required features, not merely a similarity comparison.

Fourth, the machine should store each observation as a structured record binding participants, roles, spatial relations, temporal order, and outcome. Generalized knowledge should be extracted from sets of records that share structural features, with pointers back to the source records so that generalizations can be audited and retracted. This is an extension of Pinker's learning procedure to the temporal domain, which the source does not address.

## Mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is to treat the semantic constraints as arbitrary filters. Pinker's argument is that they follow from the nature of the semantic operations. A machine that memorizes "dative verbs must be monosyllabic" without understanding the morphological class structure will fail on novel verbs.

The second mistake is to extrapolate from the morphophonological constraint on the dative to a universal law of learning. The native versus Latinate distinction is a historical accident of English (file pages 74 through 75). It is psychologically real for English speakers (file pages 83 through 84), but it is not a deep principle of cognition.

The third mistake is to treat the absence of a counterexample as confirmation. Pinker's own theory requires that constraints be revised when falsified (file page 82). A machine that has not yet encountered a negative exception has not proven its rule correct; it has merely not yet been tested.

The fourth mistake is to assume that the narrow conflation classes are psychologically primitive. Pinker suggests they may be learned by memorization, by insight into their motivation, or by some intermediate process (file page 148). The mechanism is not specified.

The fifth mistake is to equate this architecture with human intelligence or consciousness. The design proposals concern control structures for learning and generalization. They do not establish understanding or sentience.

## Unresolved questions

The most important gap is that chapter 6, which Pinker describes as containing the actual learning mechanisms (file page 23), is not in this section. The four-step procedure at file pages 81 through 82 is the closest we get, but it is explicitly a rough outline. The next reader should determine whether Pinker specifies how the learner detects shared properties among verbs, how it chooses among candidate constraints, and how it represents the semantic primitives.

The second gap is grounding. The thematic roles and semantic primitives are defined structurally, not perceptually. How a machine comes to associate "cause," "go," "be," and their arguments with grounded observations of the world is not addressed. The next reader should look for any account of how semantic structure connects to perception or action.

The third gap is temporal memory. This section says nothing about how events are encoded in sequence, how partial observations are combined across time, or how episodic memory relates to generalized knowledge. The next reader should determine whether later chapters address this, or whether it is entirely absent from the source.

The fourth gap is the selection problem. When multiple candidate constraints could explain a pattern, how does the learner choose? Pinker notes that the list of possible criteria must be finite and not too large (file page 91), but does not specify the selection criterion.

The fifth gap is the status of the narrow conflation classes. Are they stored as explicit feature bundles, or as exemplars, or as something else? Pinker's discussion of the autonomy of lexical semantics (file pages 146 through 148) suggests that the relevant features are precise and discrete, but the format of representation is deferred to chapter 5, which is not in this section.
