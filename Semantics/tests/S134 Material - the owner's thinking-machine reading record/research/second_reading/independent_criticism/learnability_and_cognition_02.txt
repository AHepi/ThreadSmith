# Research Memorandum: Independent Criticism and Alternative Explanations

## Scope and Method

This memorandum covers file pages 164 through 318 of *Learnability and Cognition: The Acquisition of Argument Structure*, which comprise the continuation and completion of chapter 4, the whole of chapter 5 on representation, and the beginning of chapter 6 on learning, ending mid-discussion of syntactic ergativity at file page 318. This is genuine argumentative prose by Steven Pinker, not apparatus. I therefore treat it as evidence and ground every attribution in the text I have read. I cite file pages only. I do not cite printed page labels, and I do not cite anything outside this section. Where a claim depends on a figure or tree diagram whose internal structure I cannot fully verify from the extraction, I say so.

My assigned job is independent criticism and alternative explanations. I try to defeat the provisional theory using what Pinker actually says here. I keep Pinker's claims separate from our engineering proposals. I do not assume that a non-neural machine is what Pinker is arguing for; he is arguing about human language acquisition, and the question of what substrate implements his mechanisms is not addressed in this section. I do not treat linguistic findings as universal laws of learning. Where the source is silent, I say so.

## The Central Threat: The Provisional Theory's Primitives Are Either Smuggled In or Missing

The provisional theory proposes "bounded deterministic program construction over calibrated sensor records without supplied circle or square definitions." Pinker's chapter 5 is the strongest possible challenge to this. He argues that the learner needs a restricted inventory of semantic elements — conceptual constituents like event, state, thing, place, path, property, and manner; predicates like go, be, act, and have; force-dynamic features; object properties like animacy, dimensionality, count versus mass, rigidity, and substance versus aggregate; temporal objects; and mechanisms like open arguments, coindexing, semantic field annotation, and quoted constants (file pages 265 through 266). He is explicit that this inventory is small relative to the thousands of distinctions verbs can make, that its elements have reflexes in closed-class morphemes across languages, and that each has cognitive content (file pages 264 through 265).

Pinker also argues that the primitives cannot simply fall out of the combination rules. He says we must dissociate primitiveness from constrainedness, because certain rules must see the componential structure of a verb while not every arrangement of components can serve as a possible verb (file pages 263 through 264). He proposes broad conflation classes as intermediate-level clusters that license only certain subsets of combinatorially possible configurations (file page 264). This is a direct warning against the provisional theory's "bounded deterministic program construction": bounding the search is not enough. The search space itself must be shaped by prior constraints on which combinations are licensed.

**Discriminating task.** Give the machine a corpus of events involving motion, contact, and state change, with no labels for cause, patient, or theme. Ask it to predict which novel event descriptions can be rephrased as caused-motion versus caused-possession. If it can do this without a supplied inventory of semantic primitives and without supplied conflation constraints, the provisional theory has found something Pinker's argument does not anticipate. If it cannot, the primitives and the conflation constraints are hidden in the design.

## The Second Challenge: Intelligent-Sounding Names Without Operations

Pinker is careful to distinguish semantic structure from what he calls scripts, frames, or stereotypes. He says cognitive similarity tends to be defined by typical chains of events as defined by mental schemas, scripts, or stereotypes, whereas linguistic semantic similarity is defined by constraints on a smaller set of necessary components of events (file page 219). He gives the example that verbs as cognitively similar as cut, break, hit, touch, and raise belong to five very different subclasses (file page 219).

The provisional theory's "grounded structured records of observations, events and possible actions" is vulnerable here. If these records encode typicality, scripts, or statistical co-occurrence, they will not support the fine discriminations Pinker describes. A machine that represents "kissing" as a cluster of features including approach and contact will predict that "kiss at" is possible, which is false. The records must encode what the representation constrains, not what typically accompanies it.

Pinker's own solution is hybrid structures: a scaffolding of universal, recurring, grammatically relevant meaning elements plus slots for bits of conceptual information about things like shrimp, butter, and fame (file page 218). The rich and idiosyncratic nuances derive from the information in the grammatically irrelevant conceptual slots, the cognitive content of the grammatically relevant elements, and general principles of lexicalization (file page 218). This is a specific architectural proposal, not a vague appeal to "grounded records."

**Discriminating intervention.** Construct two event representations that are identical in typical features but differ in which aspects are marked as constrained versus optional. Test whether the machine's rule-application decisions track the constrained features or the typical ones. If it tracks typicality, the representation is a script, not a semantic structure in Pinker's sense.

## The Third Challenge: Excessive Innate Information Versus Insufficient Constraints

Pinker's position is neither blank-slate nor fully specified. He says linking rules are essentially innate and do not have to be learned at all (file page 313). He says conceptual structures are formed by the perception of the situations in which verbs are used, providing a set of constraints outside of the linguistic system altogether (file page 313). He says the child can arrange structures into a hierarchy in which acquisition of one structure constrains the acquisition of the ones above and below it (file pages 312 through 313).

But he also acknowledges problems. He notes that linking rules are not completely universal, citing syntactic ergativity as a counterexample (file page 317). He says he will not solve this challenging problem and that as far as he knows there are no successful proposals (file page 317). He also acknowledges the possibility of vicious circles in acquisition, where the child might build incorrect but mutually compatible structures in the syntactic and lexicosemantic components (file page 315). He proposes two ways to break the circle: either the child begins with input that can be handled correctly by universal linking rules, or the child combines a variety of sources of partially predictive evidence in a constraint-satisfaction problem (file pages 315 through 316).

This is a genuine tension. The provisional theory says "initial structure and goal priorities are supplied explicitly." But how much? If too little, the search space is unconstrained and the machine will not converge. If too much, the machine is not discovering abstractions but executing a pre-loaded grammar. Pinker does not resolve this for a general learning machine; he resolves it for a child acquiring a specific language with a specific set of alternations.

**Discriminating observation.** Vary the amount of supplied structure across conditions. Measure the number of training events required for the machine to correctly classify novel verbs as alternating or nonalternating. If performance degrades gracefully as structure is removed, the theory has a plausible learning story. If it collapses abruptly, the supplied structure is load-bearing and the discovery claim is overstated.

## The Fourth Challenge: Compositional Failures and the Narrow-Range Problem

Pinker's distinction between broad-range and narrow-range rules is the most technically demanding part of the argument and the most dangerous for the provisional theory. Broad-range rules state the semantic operation. Narrow-range rules restrict which verb subclasses may undergo it. The narrow classes are defined by which aspects of an event the verb's semantic structure constrains: motion, contact, effect, manner, direction (file pages 268 through 270). He gives detailed representations showing that throw verbs differ from pull verbs in whether the causing act is an instantaneous event preceding the motion or a continuous process temporally coextensive with it (file pages 277 through 278). He shows that smear verbs differ from splash verbs in the temporal representation linking the act event and the go event (file pages 291 through 292).

The provisional theory's "composing elementary operations and previously constructed subprograms" does not specify how narrow classes are individuated. If the machine composes operations freely, it will overgenerate: it will apply the dative rule to carry and pull, which is ungrammatical. If it composes only within pre-defined classes, it has not solved the learning problem but deferred it.

Pinker's own solution is that narrow-range rules specify input structures that filter out non-dativizable verbs, and that the matching function must be defined so as to include verbs that behave similarly while excluding those that behave differently (file pages 270 through 271). But he admits that the format for the input structure of a rule and the matching function are not yet specified (file page 270). This is a gap in Pinker's own theory, not just in the provisional theory.

**Discriminating task.** Present the machine with novel verbs whose event descriptions differ only in whether the manner of motion is instantaneous versus continuous. Ask it to generate double-object forms. If it correctly permits toss and blocks carry without being told the distinction, the composition mechanism has discovered the narrow class. If it requires the distinction to be supplied, the theory has not reached beyond a shape classifier in the relevant sense.

## The Fifth Challenge: Temporal Memory and Episode Versus Knowledge

Pinker's discussion of temporal information at file pages 262 through 263 describes a time-line representation in which events and states are located as parts of the line, and aspectual distinctions correspond to how those parts are delineated. He says states or processes are regions of the line with no distinct boundaries; instantaneous events are points; accomplishments are regions bounded at their ends by a point; achievements are points bounding the end of a region (file page 262). He notes that the time line introduces considerable redundancy in semantic representations, and that events must precede or be simultaneous with their effect and but subordinate events, must follow their cause and despite subordinate events (file page 263).

But Pinker does not specify a memory architecture. He does not say how episodes are stored, indexed, or distinguished from generalizations. The provisional theory's "episode-specific records versus reusable knowledge" is an engineering extension, not a claim Pinker makes here.

The risk is that the machine's episode store becomes a lookup table, and "reusable knowledge" becomes a compressed summary of episodes rather than an independently structured representation. Pinker's argument requires that the semantic structure of a verb be available before the verb is heard in a given argument structure, otherwise the learner is back to conservatism (file pages 210 through 211). This means the reusable knowledge must be derivable from partial exposure and must support prediction of unobserved combinations.

**Discriminating intervention.** Train the machine on a verb in one argument structure only. Then present a novel context that would license the alternation. Inspect whether the machine's decision is based on the stored episode of the training event or on a generalized semantic representation that supports extrapolation. If it retrieves the episode and finds no match, it is conservative. If it constructs a prediction from structure, it has the property Pinker requires.

## The Sixth Challenge: Goals, Emotion, and the Frame Problem

Pinker's discussion in this section is almost entirely about linguistic generalization, not about goal pursuit or emotion. He mentions purpose as a semantic element that verbs can encode (file page 226), and he discusses the 'for/to' subordinating relation for goals (file pages 258 through 259). But he does not discuss how goals are set, how emotions modulate learning, or how the machine decides what to attend to. The provisional theory's "initial goal priorities are supplied explicitly" is therefore unsupported by this section and possibly in tension with the observation that the theory requires a learner capable of distinguishing property-predicting from existence-predicting rules (file pages 209 through 210).

The frame problem is not named in this section, but the blame-assignment problem is: Pinker notes that a child who receives a negative signal faces the problem of figuring out which rule to change (file page 315, in the discussion of vicious circles). The provisional theory's "revises models after failures" inherits this problem in full. A failure could implicate the perception module, the semantic representation, the linking rule, the narrow-class definition, or the goal structure. Without a theory of blame assignment, revision is unconstrained search.

**Discriminating task.** Induce a failure that is ambiguous between a perceptual error and a rule error. Inspect which component the machine revises. If it revises randomly or revises everything, the blame-assignment problem is unsolved. If it localizes the revision using structural cues, it has a mechanism worth specifying.

## Alternative Mechanisms the Source Leaves Open

Pinker's argument does not rule out connectionist implementations. He notes at file page 215 that the Unrestricted Conceptual Representation hypothesis allows for multidimensional semantic spaces, sets of partially overlapping features, or nonorthogonal vectors composed of large numbers of values of microfeatures. He cites McClelland and Kawamoto (1986) as an example of a microfeature approach (file page 223). He does not discuss production systems, case-based reasoning, or constraint satisfaction networks in this section, though he mentions constraint-satisfaction as a possible implementation for identifying grammatical functions (file page 316).

The provisional theory's "bounded deterministic program construction" is therefore one option among several that the source does not compare. A case-based mechanism that retrieves and adapts specific past episodes would satisfy the anti-conservatism evidence if it can generalize across episodes, but Pinker's argument predicts it will fail on the narrow-class discriminations unless it also encodes what the verb constrains rather than what typically occurs.

## What the Source Does Not Establish

Pinker's argument is about argument structure in natural language. It does not establish that the same mechanisms suffice for perception, motor control, reasoning about possible actions, or consciousness. He does not claim sentience anywhere in this section. The provisional theory's refusal to claim consciousness is correct and should be maintained. The theory should also not claim that success on verb alternations demonstrates general thinking.

Pinker himself is careful to say that his semantic system has many indeterminacies, arbitrary decisions, and areas of vagueness, and that he does not claim it is anything like a formal theory (file page 310). He says the primary goal is to flesh out the proposed resolution of Baker's paradox with a fairly explicit and precise theory of the crucial distinctions it appeals to (file page 216). He does not claim to have solved the learning problem; he says the representations presented in this chapter have brought us to the point where we can discuss learning mechanisms with a degree of precision that was not possible before (file page 310).

## Unresolved Questions

The next reader should address whether the discussion of learning mechanisms in chapter 6 provides the operational detail that the provisional theory needs, or whether it remains a descriptive vocabulary. The question of how children acquire the semantic primitives themselves — the elements go, be, act, have, and the object properties — is raised but not answered in this section; Pinker gestures at cross-linguistic universality (file pages 219 through 220) but does not give a learning mechanism for the primitives. The relationship between narrow conflation classes and the learner's discovery procedure is left open: Pinker says the format for the input structure of a rule and the matching function are not yet specified (file page 270). The role of discourse and pragmatics in licensing argument structures is noted as statistical rather than absolute (file page 210), and the provisional theory has no account of how statistical discourse cues interact with categorical rules. The question of whether the machine's "calibrated sensor records" can support the kind of semantic abstraction Pinker requires — representations that constrain specific aspects of events and are mute about others — is entirely open and is the point at which the provisional theory is most likely to fail. Finally, the question of how the machine would handle the vicious circle problem that Pinker identifies at file pages 314 through 316 — where syntax and semantics must be learned simultaneously but each depends on the other — is entirely unaddressed in the provisional theory and is a fundamental obstacle to any learning architecture.
