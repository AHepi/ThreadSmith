# Research Memorandum: Implementable Cognitive Mechanisms in *How the Mind Works*, File Pages 118 through 235

## Scope and Extraction Status

This section covers the end of the discussion of the cognitive niche and human evolution, the whole of the chapter on vision, the whole of the chapter on reasoning, and the opening portion of the chapter on emotions, ending mid-discussion of reciprocal altruism at file page 235. The text is substantive throughout. One extraction gap matters: the many visual demonstrations referenced in the vision chapter are only partially recoverable. The Adelson zigzag drawings at file pages 142 through 143, the stereogram constructions at file pages 130 through 137, the geon illustrations at file page 159, the shape-recognition stimuli at file pages 164 through 166, the imagery diagrams at file pages 170 through 172, and the infant-experiment displays at file pages 185 through 187 are described in prose but their visual content cannot be verified from the extracted text. Claims about what these figures show therefore rest on the accompanying descriptions. The fee tables in the Adelson and Pentland parable at file pages 148 through 149 are recoverable as text.

## The Central Engineering Contribution: Vision as Constraint Satisfaction Over Grounded Representations

The most valuable material in this section for an executable architecture is the account of how the visual system converts an underdetermined retinal projection into a usable description of the world. Pinker, following David Marr, states that vision is "a process that produces from images of the external world a description that is useful to the viewer and not cluttered with irrelevant information" (file page 127). The description is not spoken English but an internal representation in mentalese, and it must be available to the rest of the mind through a shared blackboard (file page 127). This is the strongest statement in the section of what a perception module must produce: not a picture, but a structured record that other processes can read.

The mechanism by which this is achieved is constraint satisfaction. The correspondence problem in stereo vision is the clearest case. A mark on one retina must be matched with its counterpart on the other, but with many marks there are combinatorially many possible matchings, and the correct one cannot be found by inspecting any single pair in isolation (file page 134). Marr and Tomaso Poggio designed a network in which input units stand for points in the two retinal images and feed into an array of units representing all possible pairings (file pages 138 through 139). Each unit turns on only if it receives matching input from both eyes, embodying the assumption that each mark is anchored to one surface. Units for competing matches along the same line of sight inhibit one another, embodying uniqueness. Units for neighboring points at nearby depths excite one another, embodying surface cohesion. Activation reverberates until the network stabilizes, and the settled pattern traces a contour in depth (file page 139).

This is directly implementable as an iterative relaxation process over a lattice of candidate assignments. My engineering proposal is that the same skeleton should serve as the general-purpose inference engine for the whole architecture: a set of candidate hypotheses, each with a confidence value, connected by excitatory and inhibitory links that encode domain assumptions, iterated until convergence or a resource limit is reached. The source supplies the principle and one concrete instantiation. It does not supply the general scheduling policy, the stopping criterion, or the mechanism for combining solutions from different constraint networks, and those remain open.

Pinker is careful to note that the Marr and Poggio model is incomplete. When people are placed in artificial worlds that violate the assumptions of uniqueness and smoothness, they do not perform as badly as the model predicts (file page 139). The brain uses additional cues: the fine contour features within a random-dot pattern, which drastically reduce the number of false matches, and monocularly visible features at occlusion boundaries, which the visual system interprets as edges in depth (file pages 139 through 140). Ken Nakayama and Shinsuke Shimojo demonstrated the latter with stereograms whose depth information lay in dots visible to only one eye (file page 140). For an engineering implementation, this means the constraint network must accept heterogeneous evidence from multiple specialized detectors, not just point matches.

## Specialized Analyzers and Their Coordination

Pinker's account of the visual system as a society of specialized analyzers is the most concrete material in the section on modular architecture. The Adelson zigzag illusion at file pages 142 through 143 shows that identical retinal input can be interpreted as different three-dimensional scenes depending on how shading, contour, and shape information are combined. Each of the three image-forming laws — perspective, reflectance, and shading from slant — is inverted by a separate analyzer, and each analyzer must make assumptions that the others can violate (file pages 143 through 147).

The Adelson and Pentland workshop parable at file pages 147 through 151 is the key design proposal. A shape specialist, a lighting specialist, and a reflectance specialist each propose an interpretation, and a supervisor coordinates them by assigning costs: simple and common operations are cheap, complex and unusual ones are expensive, and the cheapest overall interpretation wins (file pages 148 through 149). The program they built passes a rigid three-dimensional model from the shape specialist to the lighting specialist to the reflectance specialist in an assembly line, and settles on the interpretation people see (file pages 149 through 150). Pinker notes that if the specialists had exchanged guesses in a constraint network rather than an assembly line, the program might also have exhibited the depth flips that people experience with the Necker cube (file page 150).

For implementation, this gives a clear coordination principle: specialized processes should propose competing interpretations, and a cost-minimizing arbitration mechanism should select the globally most probable one. My proposal is that the cost function should be computed over structured records rather than scalar scores alone, because the specialists must agree not just on a single number but on shared bindings — which surface belongs to which object, which edge is a paint boundary versus a shadow boundary. The source does not specify how such agreement is represented or enforced, and this is a genuine gap.

## The Product of Vision: The Two-and-a-Half-Dimensional Sketch

Pinker, again following Marr, specifies the output format of the visual system: a two-and-a-half-dimensional sketch, a mosaic of cells each dedicated to a line of sight from the cyclopean eye, with each cell holding information about depth, slant, tilt, color, and surface ownership for surface patches, or boundary type, orientation, and ownership for edges (file pages 153 through 154). This is not a three-dimensional scale model. Pinker gives five arguments against the scale-model theory: vision is not panoramic, we do not see occluded surfaces, we see in perspective, the visual field is geometrically two-dimensional, and we do not immediately see discrete objects (file pages 151 through 153).

This is a strong constraint on any perception-to-symbol pipeline. The intermediate representation is viewer-centered, surface-based, and transient. It is not yet the object-centered, categorical representation that reasoning requires. The gap between the two-and-a-half-dimensional sketch and a grounded concept symbol like "chair" is explicitly left open. Marr's question — "Is a nose an object? Is a head one?" — is quoted at file page 153 to show that the very definition of an object is problematic at this level.

## Reference Frames and the Binding of Location

Pinker's discussion of reference frames at file pages 154 through 158 is directly relevant to the role-binding problem. The two-and-a-half-dimensional sketch is useless as delivered because it is in retinal coordinates, which shift with every eye movement (file page 154). The solution is a movable reference frame, like a cursor, that can be slid over the visual field and that stays locked to a location as the eyes move. The simplest such frame is head-centered, maintained by copying the eye-movement command to a frame-shifting mechanism (file page 155). Other frames compensate for head and body movement, align with gravity using vestibular input, or align with the axes of elongation and symmetry of an object (file pages 155 through 157).

For implementation, this means that every spatial representation must carry an explicit frame-of-reference tag, and that the architecture must include operations for transforming between frames. My proposal is that frame tags should be first-class fields in the structured records that hold spatial information, and that a frame-transformation module should be able to re-express any spatial relation in any available frame. The source describes the frames and their empirical signatures but does not give the transformation algebra.

## Shape Recognition: Multiple Strategies, Not One Algorithm

Pinker's review of shape recognition at file pages 158 through 166 is important because it shows that even within a single perceptual competence, no single algorithm suffices. Marr's object-centered geon description, the multiple-view theory, and the mental-rotation theory were all tested by Michael Tarr and Pinker, and all three were confirmed for different shapes and conditions (file pages 164 through 165). Simple symmetrical shapes are recognized using object-centered frames; complex shapes are stored as multiple views and recognized by mental rotation to the nearest familiar orientation (file page 165).

The mental rotation experiments at file pages 165 through 166 show that people rotate shapes around an optimal axis in three dimensions but not four, and that the rotation takes time proportional to the angular distance (file page 166). This is implementable as a continuous transformation over a spatial array, iterated in small steps. The finding that mirror images are treated differently from standard shapes, and that this difference is predicted by the geometry of three-dimensional rotation, is a strong constraint on any shape-matching mechanism (file pages 165 through 166).

## Mental Imagery as a Depictive Medium

Pinker's argument that mental images are patterns in the two-and-a-half-dimensional sketch loaded from memory rather than from the eyes (file page 168) is directly relevant to the design of a simulation or planning subsystem. The evidence he marshals — the Perky effect, the interference of imagery with perception, the neglect patients of Bisiach and Luzzatti, the positron emission tomography studies of Kosslyn, and the surgery patient of Martha Farah — all support the claim that imagery occupies the same representational medium as perception (file pages 169 through 170).

But Pinker is equally clear about the limits of imagery. Images are fragmentary, perspectival, and organized by a propositional superstructure (file pages 172 through 173). They cannot serve as concepts or as the meanings of words, because they cannot represent abstract, negative, disjunctive, or quantified content (file pages 173 through 174). The conclusion is that "at some point between gazing and thinking, images must give way to ideas" (file page 174). For implementation, this means the architecture needs both a depictive buffer for spatial simulation and a propositional store for abstract structure, with operations that convert between them. The source does not specify the conversion mechanism.

## Categories, Theories, and the Two Modes of Generalization

Pinker's discussion of categorization at file pages 179 through 183 gives the clearest statement in this section of the two modes of generalization. Fuzzy categories with stereotypes and family resemblance arise from recording correlations among features and predicting by similarity (file pages 180 through 181). Well-defined categories with sharp boundaries arise from intuitive theories that capture causal laws and support deduction (file pages 181 through 182). Both are needed. The world is lumpy — features cluster because of physical, biological, and intentional laws — and categories work because they track those clusters (file page 180).

For implementation, this means the architecture needs two generalization mechanisms: a similarity-based prototype or exemplar store that computes distance in a feature space, and a rule-based system that stores causal schemas and supports deductive projection. My proposal is that the two should share a common structured-record format so that a similarity match can propose a category assignment that a rule system can then elaborate or override. The source names both mechanisms and gives examples of their interaction — people judge a three-inch disk more similar to a quarter but more likely to be a pizza (file page 181) — but does not specify the arbitration process.

## Intuitive Theories as Specialized Inference Engines

Pinker's account of the core curriculum at file pages 184 through 194 is the richest source in this section for specialized reasoning processes. He describes distinct intuitive theories for objects and forces, animate beings, natural kinds, artifacts, and minds, each with its own primitive vocabulary and inference rules (file pages 184 through 185). The infant experiments of Elizabeth Spelke and Renée Baillargeon show that object knowledge — continuity, cohesion, contact causation — is present at three to four months and cannot plausibly be learned from the data available to a preverbal infant (file pages 185 through 187). The Heider and Simmel animations show that self-propelled motion triggers an agentive interpretation with goals (file page 188). The folk-biology experiments show that natural-kind reasoning invokes a hidden essence and supports inferences about growth, inheritance, and function that override surface similarity (file pages 189 through 191). The artifact experiments show that artifact categories are defined by intended function, not shape or constitution (file pages 191 through 192). The theory-of-mind experiments show that belief-desire reasoning is a distinct competence, selectively impaired in autism (file pages 192 through 194).

For implementation, this means the architecture should contain multiple domain-specific inference modules, each with its own input vocabulary, its own set of assumptions, and its own output format, all writing to a shared workspace. Pinker is careful to say that these should not be taken as encapsulated boxes; they can mix and match, as when the concept of throwing welds intuitive psychology to intuitive physics (file page 185). My proposal is that each module should be implemented as a set of condition-action rules operating on typed propositions, with a shared blackboard for inter-module communication, and that the workspace should include a mechanism for tagging propositions with their source module so that conflicting inferences can be traced and arbitrated.

## Logic, Probability, and the Frame Problem

Pinker's treatment of logic at file pages 194 through 197 is important for the design of a reasoning engine. People do use logical rules, but the rules are recruited by language understanding, mixed with world knowledge, and supplemented by content-specific inference rules such as the cheater detector described by Leda Cosmides (file pages 196 through 197). The frame problem is restated: any true statement spawns infinitely many true but useless consequences, and the system must guess which implications to explore (file page 196). This remains unsolved in this section.

Pinker's treatment of probability at file pages 200 through 205 is equally important. People are not blind to chance; they are accurate when problems are stated in terms of relative frequencies rather than single-event probabilities (file pages 202 through 203). The implication for implementation is that a learning and inference system should represent uncertainty as accumulated frequency counts over typed events, not as abstract single-case credences. My proposal is that each stored proposition should carry frequency and recency counters, and that inference rules should read these counters to weight competing hypotheses. The source gives the empirical finding and its interpretation but not the data structure.

## Metaphor and the Reuse of Inferential Machinery

Pinker's account of the metaphorical mind at file pages 205 through 211 is a proposal for how new ideas can be composed from old ones. Abstract domains are understood by mapping the structure of a concrete source domain — space, force, motion — onto a target domain, and then borrowing the source domain's inference rules (file pages 205 through 207). The mapping is not arbitrary; it preserves relational structure while allowing the content of the slots to change (file page 207). Pinker suggests that this may have evolutionary roots in the copying and modification of circuits for intuitive physics (file page 208).

For implementation, this is a mechanism for conceptual combination and abstraction. My proposal is that a metaphor operation should take a structured schema from one domain, blank out the content of its slots, and rebind them to entities in another domain, then transfer the inference rules associated with the source schema. The source gives the linguistic evidence and the evolutionary speculation but not the algorithm for discovering or validating a mapping. This is a genuine unsolved part.

## Emotions as Goal-Setting Mechanisms

Pinker's opening discussion of emotions at file pages 217 through 219 is directly relevant to the goal architecture. Emotions are "mechanisms that set the brain's highest-level goals" (file page 219). Fear, triggered by a signal of impending harm, lights up the short-term goal of escaping and the long-term goal of avoiding the hazard, and gives the goal high priority (file page 219). Without goals, intelligence is meaningless; the topmost goal in an artificial system comes from the programmer, in an organism from natural selection (file pages 218 through 219).

For implementation, this means the architecture needs a goal stack with priority levels, and a set of trigger conditions that can activate or deactivate goals based on perceived circumstances. My proposal is that each goal record should carry a priority value, a trigger condition, a set of subgoals, and a deactivation condition, and that a scheduler should select the highest-priority active goal and allocate processing resources to it. The source gives the functional description but not the arbitration policy among competing goals, which remains open.

## What the Source Leaves Unsolved

The transition from the two-and-a-half-dimensional sketch to discrete grounded concept symbols is not specified. The mechanism for variable binding and quantification is named as necessary in the earlier section and is not addressed here. The frame problem is restated but not solved. The arbitration policy among competing specialized processes and among competing goals is gestured at through the cost-minimization parable but not given as an algorithm. The relationship between similarity-based and rule-based generalization, and the process for revising a mistaken generalization, is named but not specified. The data structure for episodic memory is not supplied. The mechanism for discovering or validating metaphorical mappings between domains is not given. And the question of how a machine would acquire genuinely new concepts rather than recombine existing ones is not addressed. These are the points the next section or the second book must be checked against.
