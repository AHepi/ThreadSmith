# Research Memorandum: Implementable Cognitive Mechanisms in *How the Mind Works*, File Pages 362 through 455

## Scope and Extraction Status

This section is almost entirely apparatus rather than argument. File pages 362 through 408 continue and complete the reference list that began in the previous section. File pages 409 through 453 are the book's index. File pages 453 through 455 are the table of contents. There is no substantive prose from Steven Pinker anywhere in this section. Every entry is bibliographic or indexical data, and the table of contents is a structural outline. I have therefore not attributed any argument to Pinker on the basis of these pages, because none is present here. The previous-reader memorandum covered the substantive text through file page 326, and the endnotes ran through file page 356; this section picks up after all of that.

This creates a real limitation for my assigned job. I cannot ground new claims about implementable mechanisms in Pinker's reasoning here, because there is no reasoning here to ground them in. What I can do honestly is three things: report what the apparatus itself reveals about the architecture of the book and therefore about the shape of Pinker's theory; extract from the index and reference list the specific computational and representational proposals that Pinker engaged with, which tells us what design space he considered relevant; and mark clearly where I am extending beyond the source into my own engineering proposals. I will not pretend that a bibliography entry is evidence for a claim in the text.

## What the Apparatus Reveals About the Design Space

The reference list is not neutral. The works Pinker cites map the intellectual territory he treats as relevant to reverse-engineering the mind, and several clusters are directly relevant to the thinking-machine problem.

The first cluster concerns symbolic computation and its critics. The references include Allen Newell and Herbert Simon on human problem solving and on symbols and search (file page 390), Jerry Fodor on the language of thought and the modularity of mind (file page 370), Zenon Pylyshyn on computation and cognition (file page 395), and David Marr on vision (file page 385). On the other side are the connectionist and parallel distributed processing works: James McClelland, David Rumelhart, and the Parallel Distributed Processing Research Group (file pages 386 through 387 and 397), Geoffrey Hinton on distributed representations (file page 377), Paul Smolensky on tensor product variable binding (file page 400), and Gary Marcus on systematicity in connectionist language learning (file page 385). The presence of both camps, and of Pinker and Alan Prince's joint critique of connectionist language acquisition (file page 393), tells us that the symbolic-versus-connectionist debate is live in the source and that Pinker engages it rather than dismissing it. This matters for our project because it means the representational format is contested in the source, not settled. I cannot claim from these pages that Pinker proves symbolic representations sufficient; I can only note that he cites both sides.

The second cluster concerns perception and mental imagery. The references include Stephen Kosslyn on image and brain (file page 381), Roger Shepard and Lynn Cooper on mental images and their transformations (file page 398), Martha Farah on imagery-perception interaction (file page 369), and Shimon Ullman on visual routines (file page 405). There is also a substantial set of computational vision references: Whitman Richards, Edward Adelson, and Pawan Sinha on shading and three-dimensional form (file pages 399 and 405), and David Lowe on the viewpoint consistency constraint (file page 384). These tell us that the vision and imagery material in the earlier chapters is grounded in a specific computational literature, not speculation. But the reference list alone does not tell us which of these models Pinker endorses and which he criticizes. That requires the substantive text, which is not in this section.

The third cluster concerns learning, concept acquisition, and developmental psychology. The references include Susan Carey on conceptual change (file page 363), Frank Keil on concepts, kinds, and cognitive development (file page 380), Elizabeth Spelke on initial knowledge (file page 400), Jean Mandler on conceptual primitives (file page 385), and Rochel Gelman and Susan Gelman on essentialist beliefs and natural kinds (file page 372). There is also a set of machine learning references: Gerald de Jong and Raymond Mooney on explanation-based learning (file page 367), Michael Pazzani on learning causal patterns (file page 391), and Thomas Dietrich on thinking computers (file page 368). The coexistence of developmental psychology and machine learning in the bibliography suggests that Pinker treats the learnability problem as one where both fields have something to contribute. This is consistent with the title of the second book in the project, but I cannot confirm from these pages what position Pinker takes.

The fourth cluster concerns emotion, motivation, and social cognition. The references include Robert Frank on passions within reason (file page 370), Jack Hirshleifer on emotions as guarantors of threats and promises (file page 377), Robert Trivers on reciprocal altruism and social evolution (file page 404), Leda Cosmides and John Tooby on cognitive adaptations for social exchange (file page 365), and Antonio Damasio on emotion, reason, and the brain (file page 366). The presence of Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict (file page 397) and his work on self-command (file page 397) confirms that the commitment and doomsday-machine logic discussed in the previous section is drawn from a specific game-theoretic literature. This is useful for our design because it tells us the functional logic has formal roots, but the reference list does not give us the formalism itself.

The fifth cluster concerns the arts, humor, music, and religion. The references include Nelson Goodman on languages of art (file page 373), Arthur Koestler on the act of creation (file page 381), Ray Jackendoff on musical parsing and musical affect (file page 378), Fred Lerdahl and Ray Jackendoff on a generative theory of tonal music (file page 383), and Dan Sperber on apparently irrational beliefs (file page 400). These confirm the sources of the arguments summarized in the previous memorandum, but add no new mechanism.

## What the Index Adds

The index is more useful than the reference list for my job, because it shows how Pinker organizes concepts and which terms he treats as central. Several index entries are directly relevant to implementable mechanisms.

The entry for "Artificial intelligence" at file page 411 cross-references emotions, goal-seeking, reasoning, robotics, and vision, and points to the "Robots: Challenge of building" entry. The entry for "Computation" at file page 416 lists pages 66 through 68 and 69 through 77, and the entry for "Computational theory of mind" at the same page lists pages 21, 23, 24 through 27, 64 through 69, and 76 through 77, with cross-references to arguments against it at pages 93 through 96. This tells us that the computational theory of mind is the backbone of the book and that Pinker devotes explicit space to objections. The entry for "Mental representations" at file page 435 lists pages 69 through 70, 84 through 93, and 291, with cross-references to mentalese and propositional representations. The entry for "Mentalese" at the same page lists pages 69 through 70, 86 through 90, and 213, and notes its relation to neural networks at pages 118 through 125.

The entry for "Modularity of mind" at file page 436 lists pages 21, 23, 27 through 31, 143 through 144, 241 through 242, 243, 249, 251, 255, and 314 through 316, with a note that the term has misleading connotations. This is important: it suggests Pinker qualifies the modularity claim rather than asserting strong encapsulation without caveat. The entry for "Compositionality" at file page 416 cross-references combinatorics, and the combinatorics entry at the same page lists language, music, thoughts, object parts, reasoning, social interactions, and visual features. This tells us that Pinker treats compositionality as a general property of thought, not just language.

The entry for "Consciousness" at file page 417 distinguishes access to information from sentience, and lists evolutionary benefits of both access and lack of access. The entry for "Emotion" at file page 420 lists adaptive function, facial expressions, neuroanatomy, phylogeny, and universality, with cross-references to specific emotions. The entry for "Goals" at file page 425 lists pages 61 through 62 and 372 through 374. The entry for "Case-based reasoning" at file page 414 lists page 543, which is in the fiction discussion covered by the previous memorandum. The entry for "Creativity" at file page 417 lists pages 360 through 362. The entry for "Frame problem" at file page 423 lists page 15. ## Engineering Proposals, Clearly Marked as Mine

Since the source in this section provides no argument, everything in this section is my own engineering extension, informed by the shape of the apparatus but not grounded in Pinker's prose here.

For the symbolic-versus-connectionist question, I propose that the architecture should support both structured records with explicit role bindings and graded similarity measures over those records. The structured records handle systematicity and compositionality; the similarity measures handle the fuzzy categorization and generalization that the index shows Pinker discusses. The operation for composition would take two structured records and produce a new record with merged role fillers, checking for type compatibility. The operation for similarity would compare two records by shared structure and produce a graded score. Neither operation is specified in this section.

For perception and grounding, I propose that each perceptual record should carry not only features but a pointer to the sensory source and the time of registration, so that the system can distinguish remembered from currently perceived content. The index entry for "Reality monitoring" at file page 379, citing Marcia Johnson and Colleen Raye, suggests this distinction is discussed in the source, but I cannot confirm the mechanism from these pages.

For memory, I propose separate stores for episodic records, semantic generalizations, and procedural skills, with a consolidation operation that can extract a generalization from a set of episodic records and a retrieval operation that can match a cue against any store. The index confirms multiple memory systems are discussed but does not specify the interaction.

For goals and coordination, I propose that each specialized process writes proposals to a shared workspace with source tags and time horizons, and that a supervisor applies binding constraints. This extends the previous section's discussion of mental agents and commitment, but the arbitration policy remains unsolved.

## Unresolved Questions for the Next Reader

The central unresolved question is what Pinker actually argues in the substantive text about the sufficiency of symbolic computation, the nature of mental representations, and the learnability of language and concepts. The reference list and index tell us the topics and the interlocutors but not the positions. A reader with access to the substantive chapters, particularly the chapters on thinking machines and good ideas, should determine what representational format Pinker defends, what he concedes to connectionism, and what he claims must be innate. The second book, *Learnability and Cognition*, should be checked for the specific learning mechanisms: what is built in, what is learned, and how the learner moves from examples to generalizations. The gap between computational possibility and practical discovery remains open: even if a representational format is formally adequate, the search procedure for finding the right representations in the right situations is not addressed by anything in this section. Finally, the question of how a machine would acquire genuinely new concepts rather than recombine existing ones is not touched by the apparatus and must be answered from the substantive text or left as a research problem.
