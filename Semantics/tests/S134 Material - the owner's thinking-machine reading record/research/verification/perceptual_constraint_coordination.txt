# Verification of Stereo Vision and Perceptual Representation Claims

## Claim: The correspondence problem is solved by mutually constraining candidate matches

**Verdict: Supported.** File pages 138 and 139.

Steven Pinker describes the stereo correspondence problem as a chicken-and-egg riddle: disparity cannot be measured until a match is chosen, yet thousands of candidate matches exist. He reports David Marr's proposal that built-in assumptions about the world prune the possibilities. Three assumptions are stated: each mark is anchored to one position on one surface at one time, so a match must pair identical points; a dot in one eye matches no more than one dot in the other; and matter is cohesive and smooth, so neighboring lines of sight usually end on nearby depths. Pinker then explains constraint satisfaction: keep several guesses for each part of a puzzle, compare guesses across parts, and retain those that are mutually consistent. In stereo vision, the dots are the clues, the matchups and their depths are the guesses, and the three assumptions are the rules.

## Claim: Excitation and inhibition implement the constraints

**Verdict: Supported.** File page 139.

Pinker reports that he and Tomaso Poggio designed a constraint network. Input units represent points and feed an array of units representing all possible matchups. A unit turns on only if it receives the same inputs from the two eyes, embodying the anchoring assumption. Units for different matches along the same line of sight inhibit one another, embodying uniqueness. Units for neighboring points at nearby depths excite one another, embodying cohesion. Activations reverberate until the network stabilizes, with activated units tracing a contour in depth.

## Claim: Global consistency emerges from local interactions

**Verdict: Supported with qualification.** File page 139.

Pinker states that thousands of processors make tentative guesses and hash it out among themselves until a global solution emerges. He notes the psychological parallel: a hidden figure in a random-dot stereogram does not erupt instantaneously but coalesces gradually. However, he does not claim the network finds a provably optimal global solution, only that it stabilizes.

## Claim: The model has admitted limitations

**Verdict: Supported.** File page 139.

Pinker explicitly states that the Marr-Poggio model captures the flavor of stereo computation but that real circuitry is surely more sophisticated. When people view artificial worlds violating uniqueness and smoothness, they do not see as badly as the model predicts. The brain uses additional information: distinctive features such as diagonals, T's, and zigzags reduce false matches, and occlusion cues (features visible to only one eye) mark object boundaries. Nakayama and Shimojo demonstrated that people perceive a floating square defined by monocularly visible dots.

## Claim: Perceptual representation is richer than a list of object labels

**Verdict: Supported.** File pages 153 and 154.

Pinker describes the product of vision as Marr's two-and-a-half-dimensional sketch, a mosaic of cells each dedicated to a line of sight. Each cell represents either a surface (with depth, slant, tilt, color, and a surface-membership label) or an edge (with boundary type, orientation, and ownership). This is a structured, spatially organized representation with geometric and qualitative attributes, not a list of names.

## Claim: Reference frames differ from a label list

**Verdict: Supported.** File pages 154 through 156.

Pinker explains that the two-and-a-half-dimensional sketch is delivered in a retinal frame of reference, which becomes defunct when the eyes, head, or objects move. He describes multiple reference frames: a head-centered frame shifted by copies of eye-movement commands, frames compensating for head and body motion, a world-aligned grid for judging real extents, and a gravity-aligned frame driven by the vestibular system. He notes that shape representation depends on the up-down axis, so a rotated outline of Africa is not recognized. Location is always relative to a frame, and the same information can be accessed through different frames, as in J. J. Gibson's distinction between the visual field and the visual world.

## Is this a complete general learning algorithm?

**Verdict: No.** These pages describe a domain-specific inference mechanism for stereo vision and a representational format for perceived surfaces. Pinker does not present a general learning algorithm, does not explain how the constraints are acquired rather than built in, and does not address language, planning, or abstract reasoning here.

## Is a non-neural discrete constraint propagation implementation an engineering alternative?

**Verdict: Yes, as our proposal, not as Pinker's demonstration.** Pinker describes a network of interconnected units with activation, excitation, and inhibition, and he notes consistency with parallel neural processing. He does not demonstrate a non-neural discrete implementation. Designing a discrete constraint propagation system inspired by these principles is our engineering extension.

## What remains to be invented

The source supports the idea that physically motivated constraints, mutually constraining candidate matches, and structured spatial representations with reference frames can ground perception. It does not supply a learning mechanism for acquiring constraints, a method for composing representations across domains, temporal memory, goal selection, or model revision. Those are our design problems.
