## Verdict on the Claims

**Claim: Emotions help set or coordinate goals.** Supported. On file page 219, Steven Pinker writes that "the emotions are mechanisms that set the brain's highest-level goals" and that a triggered emotion "triggers the cascade of subgoals and sub-subgoals that we call thinking and acting." He adds that fear, for example, lights up short-term goals of fleeing or deflecting danger, gives them high priority, and also lights up longer-term goals of avoiding the hazard and remembering how escape succeeded. The qualification is that Pinker frames emotions as evolved adaptations, "well-engineered software modules," not as arbitrary control knobs; and he denies any sharp line between thinking and feeling because goals and means are woven into a multiply nested structure.

**Claim: Means-end reasoning does not by itself supply top-level goals.** Supported. On file page 219, Pinker asks where the topmost goal comes from. For artificial intelligence systems, he says, it comes from the programmer; for organisms, from natural selection. He illustrates means-end reasoning with the locked apartment: forcing a window, calling the landlord, or reaching the latch through the mail slot, each decomposed into subgoals such as finding pliers and then buying pliers. The chain of subgoals is supplied by reasoning, but the top of the chain is not. Qualification: Pinker does not claim means-end reasoning is useless for goals, only that it does not originate the highest one.

**Claim: Human versus programmed machine goals.** Supported with an important nuance. On file page 219, Pinker states plainly that for artificial intelligence systems the topmost goal "comes from the programmer," who designs the system to diagnose soybean diseases or predict the Dow Jones Industrial Average. For organisms, natural selection installs goals shaped by ancestral reproduction, though he is careful to say the brain's goal is not reproduction itself, since people knowingly subvert it. He lists understanding the environment and securing the cooperation of others among human high-level goals. Qualification: Pinker is describing where goals come from in each case, not endorsing programmer-supplied goals as sufficient for a general thinking machine.

**Claim: Functional goal prioritization is distinct from subjective feeling.** Supported. On file page 219, Pinker says fear "gives the goal high priority, which we experience as a sense of urgency," separating the functional prioritization from the experiential accompaniment. He explicitly notes that whether robots would be sentient of such emotions "is another question." Qualification: Pinker treats the subjective feeling as real in humans but logically separable from the control function; he does not claim to solve the problem of machine sentience.

**Claim: A specific exploration policy is supplied.** Not supported. On file page 220, Pinker discusses habitat preference, mental resource maps, and the finding that people like landscapes that are "easy to explore and remember," but he gives no algorithm, rule, or policy for exploration. The passage on file page 218 mentions intellectual curiosity as a drive to set and solve problems, but again without mechanism. Qualification: any exploration policy in our design is our own engineering proposal, not attributable to Steven Pinker.

**Claim: Evolutionary examples are requirements for an engineered machine.** Not supported and should be avoided. Pinker's discussion of savannas, Four Fs, and reproductive goals is reverse-engineering of biological design. He does not argue that a machine must inherit these particular goals.

## How Far the Source Supports the Engineering Use

Steven Pinker supplies a clean architectural principle: a thinking system needs a source of top-level goals that means-end reasoning serves but does not generate, and emotions are his candidate mechanism for setting and prioritizing those goals in a time-sensitive way. The passage on file page 219 about the hungry and thirsty animal committing to one goal at a time is directly useful: it motivates a design in which competing goals are ranked and one is selected for action rather than pursued in parallel without arbitration. The nested subgoal structure he describes maps naturally onto a stack or hierarchy of goal symbols, which he says most artificial intelligence systems already use.

What remains to be invented is substantial. Pinker gives no representation language for grounded percepts, no mechanism for composing new ideas, no learning rule for revising generalizations, no temporal memory architecture, and no algorithm for active observation or model revision. He does not specify how a machine would acquire goals without a programmer, how goals would be revised, or how the system would handle ambiguity in the present view by consulting past observations. The exploration policy, the circle-versus-square demonstration, and the machinery of executable grounded representations are our engineering proposals and must be labeled as such. Pinker's account of emotions as evolved modules is a biological hypothesis; adopting the functional role of goal-setting and prioritization in a machine does not require adopting the evolutionary origin story, and doing so would be a designer's extension.
