# Query wording

This file gives the wording for each check, what a good answer and a worrying answer look like, follow-ups for common replies, and how to run the checks on your own review before you send it.

A query is anything you put to the author, or to yourself, that you cannot settle by looking. Most checks do not need a query at all: you redo the sum, follow the reference, compare the quotation. Queries are for what only the author, or a source you cannot open, can tell you.

Contents: 1 Before you query anything. 2 Opening questions. 3 Wording for each check. 4 Data and sources. 5 Common replies and follow-ups. 6 Checking your own review. 7 When to stop. 8 Traps.

---

## 1. Before you query anything

- **Try to settle it yourself first.** Redo the sum, follow the pointer, search the document for the other use of the term. A query about something you could have checked wastes the author's time and weakens the queries that matter.
- **Quote or locate first.** Every query starts with where the problem is: "Section 4.2, second paragraph", "Equation 7", "Table 3, row 'Other'". If the location is ambiguous, quote a few words.
- **Say what you found.** "The text gives 12%; from the figures in the same sentence I get 14.6%" is a query the author can answer in a line. "Please check this figure" is not.
- **One issue per query.** Stacked questions get the easy half answered.
- **Offer the likely answer.** "Should this be Table 4?" "Is the base here the 2023 figure?" A yes or no is quicker than an explanation.
- **Neutral wording.** Write about the text. "This sentence can be read two ways" rather than "you have been unclear".

## 2. Opening questions

These settle the brief. Ask them, or answer them yourself and state your answers at the top of the report.

| To find out | Ask |
|---|---|
| The purpose | "What is this document meant to do for its reader: inform, persuade, instruct, specify, prove?" |
| The audience | "Who will read it? What can I assume they already know?" |
| The kind of review | "Do you want a full review, a check of correctness only, a proofread, or a check of the references?" |
| The stage | "Is this a first draft, a near-final draft, or a proof?" |
| The standard | "Is there a house style, a journal's rules, a template or a regulation it must meet?" |
| The priorities | "Is there a part you are most worried about, or a part I can skip?" |
| The companions | "Are there other documents it must agree with: an earlier version, a data file, a contract, a specification?" |
| The deadline | "When do you need it, and would a partial review earlier be useful?" |

If you cannot ask, write the brief you assumed. A review against the wrong brief can be accurate and useless.

## 3. Wording for each check

### Terms
- **Ask yourself:** "Where is this term defined? Does this use match the definition?"
- **To the author:** "'Active user' is defined in section 2 as someone who logged in during the last 30 days. Section 6 counts 'active users' over 90 days. Which is intended here?"
- **Good answer:** one meaning, and a correction to the other place.
- **Worrying answer:** "Both, depending on context." Then the term needs two names or a stated rule for which applies where.

### Cross-references
- **Ask yourself:** "Does the target exist? Does it have this number? Does it show what the text says it shows?"
- **To the author:** "Section 3 refers to 'Figure 5' for the monthly trend. Figure 5 shows the regional split; the trend appears to be Figure 6. Should this read Figure 6?"
- **Good answer:** a correction.
- **Worrying answer:** "The figure was removed." Then the claim it supported has lost its evidence; check whether the claim still stands.

### Arithmetic
- **Ask yourself:** "What do I get? What base, what units, what rounding?"
- **To the author:** "The five items in Table 2 add to £51,800; the stated total is £52,300. Is there a missing line?"
- **Good answer:** the missing item, or a corrected total.
- **Worrying answer:** "The total comes from a different source." Then the table and the total do not describe the same thing, and the reader will not know which to trust. Ask for them to be reconciled or the difference explained in the text.

### Worked examples
- **Ask yourself:** "Working from the stated inputs, without looking at the answer, what do I get?"
- **To the author:** "In Example 3, applying the formula in equation 4 to the inputs given, I get 0.40, not 0.48. The difference seems to arise in the second line, where 0.6 is used for the rate instead of 0.5. Is the rate meant to be 0.5 as stated above?"
- **Good answer:** a correction to the input or the result.
- **Worrying answer:** "The example uses a slightly different method." Then the example does not illustrate the method it is attached to; ask for one or the other to change.

### Proofs
- **Ask yourself:** "What justifies this line? Is it a definition, an earlier result, an assumption, algebra?"
- **To the author:** "In the proof of Lemma 4, the step from line 3 to line 4 divides by *a − b*. Where is it established that *a* ≠ *b*?"
- **And:** "The theorem states the result for every *n* ≥ 1. The induction begins at *n* = 2. Is the case *n* = 1 covered elsewhere?"
- **And:** "The proof concludes that there exists *y* with the property for each *x*. The statement says there is one *y* that works for every *x*. Which is intended?"
- **Good answer:** a missing line supplied, or the statement corrected to match what is proved.
- **Worrying answer:** "It is obvious." Ask which rule makes it so; if the author can name it, suggest adding it to the proof.

### Quotations
- **Ask yourself:** "Does the quoted text match the source word for word? Is anything omitted without an ellipsis? Does the omission change the sense?"
- **To the author:** "The quotation on page 4 ends 'made war inevitable'. The source (p. 212) reads 'made war more likely than it had been, though not inevitable'. Could the quotation be extended or paraphrased?"
- **Good answer:** a corrected quotation.
- **Worrying answer:** "That is what the author meant." Then it should be a paraphrase, with the source's wording available for the reader to check.

### Citations
- **Ask yourself:** "Does the cited work contain this claim, or evidence for it? Is the citation specific enough to find?"
- **To the author:** "The figure of 38% is cited to Smith (2019). I could not find it in that report. Could you give the page or table?"
- **Good answer:** a page, or a corrected citation.
- **Worrying answer:** "It is widely cited." Being widely cited is not the same as being in the cited source. Ask for the original.

### Figures and tables
- **Ask yourself:** "Does it have a caption, labelled axes, units and a source? Is it referred to in the text? Does the text describe it correctly?"
- **To the author:** "The text says the rate 'rose steadily from 2015'. Figure 2 shows a fall in 2018 and 2019. Should the text mention the fall?"

### Structure
- **Ask yourself:** "Could a reader skip to this section and understand it? Is the main point where a reader will look for it?"
- **To the author:** "The recommendation appears only in section 4, paragraph 6. Would it help to state it in the summary and the introduction?"

### Audience
- **Ask yourself:** "Would the intended reader know this term? Would they know what to do next?"
- **To the author:** "The guide is for new volunteers but uses 'FIFO' without explanation. Could it be spelled out at first use?"

### Notation
- **Ask yourself:** "Does this symbol mean the same thing as the last time I saw it?"
- **To the author:** "*n* is the sample size in chapter 1 and the number of groups in chapter 3. Would a different letter for one of them help?"

## 4. Data and sources

- "Where do these figures come from? Can the reader find the same source?"
- "Is this the latest available figure, or the figure at the time of writing? Should the date be stated?"
- "Are these numbers for the same period, place and definition as the figures they are compared with?"
- "Is this quotation from the original, or from a secondary source quoting it?"
- "The reference list includes Jones (2020), which I could not find cited in the text. Should it be cited or removed?"

## 5. Common replies and follow-ups

| They say | Ask next |
|---|---|
| "It's clear from context." | "Which context? A reader starting at this section would see only this paragraph. Could the term be defined here or pointed to?" |
| "Everyone in the field knows that." | "Is the intended reader in the field? If so, fine. If not, a line of explanation would help." |
| "That's just a typo." | "Understood. Is the rest of the sentence right as it stands, with the typo corrected?" |
| "It's in the supplementary material." | "Could the main text say so, with a pointer, so readers know where to look?" |
| "The numbers are rounded." | "Rounded to what? If to one decimal place, the parts should add to within 0.5 of the total. They are 4 apart." |
| "We'll tidy it in the final version." | "Fine. I have listed it so it is not lost." |
| "You've misread it." | "Possibly. If I misread it, other readers may too. How would you like it to read?" |
| "The source says that." | "Could you give the page? I could not find it." |
| "It's a standard result." | "Could you name or cite it? Then readers can check its conditions are met here." |
| "The two terms mean the same." | "Then could one be used throughout, or the equivalence stated at first use?" |
| "The two terms mean different things." | "Then could each be defined? At present only one is." |
| "That section is out of scope." | "Could the document say so, so readers do not expect it to be covered?" |

## 6. Checking your own review

Before sending a review, run a short check on it. A review is a document too, and it will be read by someone who cannot ask you what you meant.

1. **Is every finding located?** Could the author go straight to each one?
2. **Is every finding graded?** Is the most serious one first?
3. **Did I check each finding twice?** Redo each sum you report as wrong. Reopen each cross-reference you report as broken. A wrong finding costs more trust than a missed one.
4. **Did I separate errors from preferences?** Strike or relabel anything that is only how you would have written it.
5. **Did I say what I checked, and what I did not?** Sections skipped, sources not opened, sums not redone.
6. **Is the tone about the text?** Read each comment as the author will. Remove anything that sounds like a judgement of the person.
7. **Is it in proportion?** A few pages of comments on a one-page memo, or one line on a book, suggests the balance is wrong.

Do this quickly. The author needs the finished review, not an account of how you checked it.

---

## 7. When to stop

Stop when every note has one of five outcomes:

- **Confirmed finding** - checked twice, located, graded, with a proposed fix where possible.
- **Query** - you cannot settle it, and you have said what would.
- **Withdrawn** - answered elsewhere in the document, or on second reading not a problem.
- **Merged** - part of another finding; the locations are listed together.
- **Out of scope** - real, but outside the brief. Mention it briefly if it matters.

If a note will not take one of these, you probably do not yet understand the passage. Reread it, and the sections it depends on.

---

## 8. Traps

- Asking the author something you could have checked yourself.
- Asking "is this right?" without saying what you found.
- Stacking several issues in one query.
- Accepting "it's obvious" or "it's standard" without asking which rule or which result.
- Reporting a finding you checked only once.
- Letting queries stand in for findings you were not sure enough to state.
