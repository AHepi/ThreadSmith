## 1. How the Mind Works

### 1.1 The thesis

1. The book's thesis is that the mind is a system of organs of computation, designed by natural selection to solve the problems our ancestors met as foragers. In Pinker's own later summary, "the human mind is a naturally selected system of organs of computation" **[checked: P2005, abstract and pp. 1–2; HTMWpub; Edge1997, Pinker's first answer]**.
2. The book joins three ideas: the mind is a computational system; it is made of faculties specialised for different problems; and their design comes from the natural selection of replicating genes **[checked: P2005, pp. 1–2]**. Fodor, reviewing it, counts four: computation, massive modularity, much innate structure, and that structure being an adaptation serving the propagation of genes **[checked: F1998]**.
3. **Reverse engineering.** The method: work out what natural selection designed a part of the mind to do in the ancestral environment, and read its structure from that function, as one would guess what an antique gadget's parts are for once one knew its purpose **[checked: Edge1997; HTMWpub]**. Fodor objects that this needs only a notion of present function, which physiology had before Darwin, so evolution adds less than Pinker claims **[checked: F1998]**. Pinker answers that function without natural selection is incomplete: adaptive organs are wildly improbable arrangements of matter that need an explanation, and selection also rules out some functions (benefit to the species, functions for an environment the organism never lived in) **[checked: P2005, pp. 19–20]**.

### 1.2 Computation, representation and knowledge

4. **Computation** in HTMW is not what a commercial digital computer does and not a Turing machine's architecture; it is a generic "mechanical rationality": representations with both causal and semantic properties, whose transitions mirror a normatively valid system (logic, statistics, cause and effect) **[checked: P2005, pp. 2–3, 6]**. It includes parallel, analogue and graded (probabilistic) computation **[checked: P2005, p. 3]**.
5. **What knowledge is, on this view.** In a computational system "knowledge and goals are represented as patterns in bits of matter"; the system is designed so that one representation causes another, and so that "if the old representations were accurate, the new ones are accurate as well" **[checked: P2005, p. 2]**. Beliefs are a kind of information, thinking a kind of computation, and emotions and desires are feedback that reduces the gap between a current state and a goal **[checked: P2005, p. 2]**. So knowledge here is accurate representation, used in inference; it is defined by what it stands for and how it is used, not by whether it copies or keeps itself **[Claude's reading]**.
6. **Representations are of many formats**: arrays for images, tree structures for sentences, networks for long-term memory, mental models and analogue imagery, not only sentence-like symbols **[checked: P2005, pp. 3, 7; SEP-CTM, which cites Pinker 2005 for this pluralistic line]**.
7. **Combinatorial representation.** HTMW argues that thought needs structured, combinatorial representations (distinguishing individuals from kinds, binding variables, composing propositions, recursion), which plain associative networks lack unless they are built to implement them **[unverified: memory of HTMW, chapter 2]**. The same point, made for language by Pinker and Prince (1988) against a connectionist model of the past tense, is recorded by SEP-Lang (section 4.5): such networks lack the discrete, articulated structure needed **[checked: SEP-Lang, 4.5]**. Pinker also says the mind combines specialised concepts into larger assemblies (an artifact is the concept of an object joined to the concept of a goal) **[checked: P2005, p. 4]**.
8. **Specialisation is not encapsulation.** HTMW's faculties are domain-specific organisation, not Fodor's sealed modules; they are not cut off from relevant information, and they dictate what inferences and goals a kind of information should trigger **[checked: P2005, pp. 15–16]**. Reasoning is organised into domains, each around core intuitions: physical objects persist and are moved by forces; living things have hidden essences; minds have beliefs and goals; artifacts are made by a mind for a goal **[checked: P2005, p. 15]**. SEP-Mod lists HTMW among the original statements of the massive modularity hypothesis **[checked: SEP-Mod]**.
9. **Global inference without a master plan.** Against Fodor's claim that computation cannot do abduction, Pinker points to constraint satisfaction networks (HTMW pp. 103–109): nodes stand for possible states of affairs, connections for their consistency, and the network settles into "a new state of knowledge"; the connection weights can be set by innate tuning, by experience of correlations, or by deduction **[checked: P2005, pp. 11–13]**. Domain organisation prunes the search **[checked: P2005, p. 15]**. SEP-CTM records Pinker 2005 as the reply that conceded Turing-style modelling might fail at abduction but denied there was no alternative **[checked: SEP-CTM, section on Fodor's critique]**.

### 1.3 Adaptation, learning and the need for input

10. **Learning is a designed faculty, not the opposite of innateness.** In 1997 Pinker wrote that evolutionary psychology argues most strenuously that the learned-or-innate dichotomy is incoherent, and that "The brain is not a list of "constraints" but a highly structured and adapted organ" that processes information both as it builds itself and as it runs **[checked: Edge1997, Pinker's reply to Quartz]**. HTMW says that learning needs innate machinery to do the learning **[unverified: memory of HTMW's wording; the same point is checked in P2004, item 18 below]**.
11. **The mind is designed for useful approximations in the ancestral world, not for truth.** Computing truth costs time; "outside the realm of mathematics and logic, there is no such thing as a universal true-belief-fixer"; inductive systems must make fallible assumptions about the world (that surfaces are mostly cohesive, that languages follow a universal grammar), and when the world changes these assumptions misfire, as in visual illusions; beliefs also serve loyalty, display and self-deception **[checked: P2005, pp. 18–19]**.
12. **Mismatch.** Many errors (gambling, junk food, fearing snakes more than cars) come from the gap between the environment the mind is adapted to and the present one **[checked: P2005, p. 5]**.
13. **Science is social, not one head.** Pinker answers Fodor that the scientific community's long, tool-aided, peer-criticised work over centuries is not the measure of what one mind does in seconds **[checked: P2005, pp. 10–11]**.
14. **Knowledge needs the world, at the world's rate.** In 2023 Pinker said new knowledge cannot be deduced but "has to be explored empirically at a rate that will be limited by the world"; Deutsch agreed, saying experiments cannot be done instantaneously **[checked: DP2023]**.
15. **Learning and evolution.** HTMW discusses how learning within a lifetime can guide evolution (the Baldwin effect, with Hinton and Nowlan's 1987 simulation) **[unverified: memory of HTMW chapter 3]**. The simulation itself (Hinton and Nowlan, "How learning can guide evolution", *Complex Systems* 1, 495–502, 1987) is well attested **[checked: bibliographic details only, from search results]**.
16. **The cognitive niche.** HTMW describes humans as evolved to occupy a "cognitive niche" (after Tooby and DeVore): living by out-thinking other species through causal reasoning, language and cooperation **[unverified: memory of HTMW chapter 3]**.

## 2. Learnability and Cognition, with the 1984 book and the 1979 paper

### 2.1 The learnability problem

17. **What the problem is.** The "logical problem of language acquisition" is not a claim that the input is too poor; it is the research question of how any learner could generalise correctly from a finite sample of sentences in context to the infinite set that makes up the language **[checked: P2004, p. 949]**.
18. **Why constraints are unavoidable.** As in every problem of induction, infinitely many generalisations fit any finite sample, so any learner that gets it right "must respect prior ('innate') constraints on its hypothesis space; the data alone are insufficient"; this holds for behaviourist, connectionist and emergentist theories alike, which only put the constraints in different places (generalisation gradients; the features and wiring of a network; cues and competition) **[checked: P2004, pp. 949–950]**.
19. **The four parts of any learning problem.** Learnability theory defines learning by four parts: a class of possible targets (one is the target, unknown to the learner); an environment (the information the learner gets: sentences, their context, any feedback, in some order); a learning strategy (which makes hypotheses and tests them against the input); and a success criterion (when the hypotheses count as having reached the target). Assumptions about any three constrain the fourth **[checked: P1995, section 4.1 (paraphrased); P1979, sections III–IV]**.
20. **Six conditions on a theory of language learning** (1979): it must explain that language is learned at all (Learnability), any human language and not one built in (Equipotentiality), in about the time children take (Time), from the information children actually get (Input), through the stages children actually show (Developmental), with the faculties children actually have (Cognitive) **[checked: P1979, pp. 218–219]**.
21. **Gold's results, as Pinker uses them.** Gold (1967) modelled learning as "identification in the limit": a learner sees one string per trial, guesses a grammar each time, and succeeds if after some finite time it keeps the right one, without ever knowing it has succeeded **[checked: P1979, p. 225]**. With both sentences and labelled non-sentences, every class up to the primitive recursive languages is learnable; with sentences only, no class beyond the finite languages is **[checked: P1979, pp. 225–226]**. The reason, in Gold's words as Pinker quotes them: "if you guess too large a language, the sample will never tell you you're wrong" **[checked: P1979, p. 226]**. No procedure beats enumerating the grammars one by one, and that procedure is astronomically slow (over a googol finite-state grammars of seven symbols and seven states) **[checked: P1979, pp. 227–228]**.
22. **The ways out.** Learning from sentences alone becomes possible if the order of presentation carries information, if success is relaxed (approaching or approximating the target), if the sample has a statistical distribution, or if the learner's hypotheses are constrained in advance; the last commits one to at least a weak nativism, and the prior set must be neither too broad nor too narrow **[checked: P1979, pp. 228–232]**. Pinker notes that Horning's probabilistic learner reinforces the point, since it needs prior probabilities for all grammars **[checked: P2004, p. 951]**. He corrects a common misreading: Gold proved the finite languages learnable from positive evidence, not the finite-state ones **[checked: P2004, p. 950]**; and Chomsky never relied on Gold, while two anti-nativists (Braine, Bowerman) were the first to stress the absence of negative evidence **[checked: P2004, p. 950]**.
23. **Meaning as input.** If the child receives each sentence paired with its meaning, worked out from context, Gold's barrier against learning without negative evidence falls (Anderson's semantic version of Gold), but the time cost and the need for constraints remain; and using semantics is "in some senses stronger, not weaker" as a hypothesis, because the learner must assume how meanings map onto strings **[checked: P1979, pp. 243–244]**. Pinker's own 1979 conclusion: one must find out how learning *could* work for the data on children to tell how it *does* **[checked: P1979, p. 270]**.

### 2.2 What the child actually gets

24. **Positive evidence is plentiful and clean.** Speech to children is almost all grammatical (one estimate: 99.93%) **[checked: P1995, 6.1]**.
25. **Negative evidence is absent or unusable.** Parents approve true statements, not grammatical ones (Brown and Hanlon 1970); later studies found only weak statistical differences in parents' replies, far short of reliable information about any string, and a boy unable to speak from birth still learned to judge grammaticality **[checked: P1995, 6.2]**. So the child must have mechanisms that rule out vast numbers of plausible strings without outside correction **[checked: P1995, 6.2]**.
26. **Input is needed, and it must be a language in use.** Children raised without language are mute; children who hear only a pidgin, in a community of children, build a full creole; deaf children's communities create sign languages; and children do not learn language from radio or television, because they cannot tell what the speakers are talking about **[checked: P1995, 6.1 and 6.5]**.
27. **Context is part of the input.** Children learn from speech about the here and now, guessing the speaker's meaning from the situation and from words they know; many models (including Pinker's 1982 and 1984) assume the input is a sentence paired with a meaning inferred from context, an idealisation that requires the child to keep an updated model of the situation, of objects, events and other people's intentions **[checked: P1995, 6.5]**. In 2023 Pinker put it as: children correlate a speaker's likely intention with the signal; it is "not pure cryptography over the signals themselves", since language "is more like a transducer than just a pattern signal" **[checked: DP2023]**.

### 2.3 Bootstrapping: the 1984 book

28. **The bootstrapping problem.** Knowing innately that nouns exist does not help a child who cannot find them in speech; the features that define grammatical categories are not audible until the grammar is known, and tallying correlations among audible features either never finds the categories or assumes them **[checked: P1995, 8 and 8.1]**.
29. **Semantic bootstrapping.** Pinker's answer (1982, 1984): the child assumes that, in early speech, semantic and syntactic categories are linked in restricted ways (names of things are nouns, names of actions are verbs, the agent of an action is the subject), uses meanings inferred from context to find the first categories and rules, and then learns the words that break the pattern from where they occur in structures already learned **[checked: P1995, 8.2 (paraphrased)]**. The 1984 book builds acquisition procedures of this kind for a whole grammar (in the framework of Lexical-Functional Grammar) and assumes the child's grammar is of the same kind as the adult's throughout (the "continuity assumption") **[unverified: memory of the 1984 book]**.

### 2.4 Argument structure and Baker's paradox: the 1989 book

30. **The paradox.** Pinker named it "Baker's paradox", after C. L. Baker (1979) **[checked: Cop2008, p. 1]**. It arises from three premises held together: languages have productive generalisations; there are arbitrary exceptions to them; and the learner has no negative evidence **[checked: Cop2008, p. 1]**. The standard case is the dative: *give the museum the money* is fine and *donate the museum the money* is not, though *donate the money to the museum* is; hearing many alternating verbs should lead a child to generalise, and nothing tells the child that *donate* is excluded **[checked: SEP-Lang, on Pinker 1989 and Perfors et al.]**. Children do overgeneralise in just this way (*Don't giggle me*; *I'm going to cover a screen over me*) and then stop **[checked: P1995, 4.1 and the argument-structure section; Amb2013, abstract]**.
31. **Pinker's solution: semantic criteria on lexical rules.** A verb's argument structure is not arbitrary. Each construction has a broad semantic core (a "thematic core"), and broad-range rules let verbs consistent with that core take part; then the child learns finer, narrow-range semantic subclasses of verbs that do and do not alternate, and that is the retreat from error **[checked: Amb2013, "Verb Semantics", citing Pinker 1989; SEP-Lang]**. For example, the transitive causative goes with direct, external causation, so verbs of change of state (*open, break*) enter it, while verbs of "volitional or internally caused actions" (Pinker 1989, p. 131, as quoted in Amb2013) such as laughing or disappearing do not **[checked: Amb2013]**. For the dative, Pinker 1989 (incorporating Gropen et al. 1989) adds a morphophonological constraint, that Latinate verbs such as *donate* and *explain* resist the double object form, and names subclasses that alternate whatever their form, among them verbs of future having (*bequeath*, *allocate*), verbs with a negative meaning (*deny*, *refuse*) and verbs of instrument of communication **[checked: Cop2008, pp. 2, 63–66]**. Further subclasses, such as instantaneous ballistic motion (*throw*, alternating) and manner of speaking (*shout*, not alternating), come from Claude's memory and a search engine's summary **[unverified]**. Pinker himself "would not be prepared to push" a semantic explanation of the Latinate constraint **[checked: Cop2008, p. 63, quoting Pinker 1989, p. 123]**.
32. **Linking rules connect meaning to syntax.** The entity a verb specifies as affected becomes the direct object: *fill* specifies the container's end state (fill the glass with water), *pour* the liquid's manner of motion (pour water into the glass). A child who thinks *fill* names a manner of pouring says *filling water into the glass*; when more contexts show that *fill* names the end state, the same principle withdraws the error **[checked: P1995, argument-structure section]**. Experiments: children who misread *fill*'s meaning also made the syntax error; and children taught a new verb (*pilk*) for an event whose distinctive part was either a zigzag motion or a colour change in the cloth used the object that the meaning predicted, without ever hearing the verb in a sentence (Gropen et al. 1991) **[checked: P1995, same section]**.
33. **The book's wider claim** is described by its publisher as a theory of how verb meaning and verb syntax are represented and learned, with implications for the relation between language and thought **[checked only as a search engine's summary of the publisher's page; the page itself refused access]**. In the book, the semantic distinctions that matter to grammar are drawn from a small, universal set of features, a "grammatically relevant" subsystem of concepts **[unverified: memory of the 1989 book]**.
34. **Criticism.** Later work argues that semantics is not the whole story: pre-emption (hearing *Don't make me giggle* blocks *Don't giggle me*) and entrenchment (hearing *giggle* often as intransitive makes the transitive less acceptable) also act; some argue Pinker's procedure is unfeasible; and the classes may be graded rather than discrete **[checked: Amb2013, abstract and "Verb Semantics"]**. Perfors and colleagues show a statistical learner can infer that a construction is impossible for a verb from its absence where it would be expected **[checked: SEP-Lang]**. Pinker's 2004 reply: such appeals still presuppose innate constraints, only unstated (which items compete, what an item is, which categories are monitored for absence), and children are not conservative item by item (*fax him the instructions* is accepted on first hearing) **[checked: P2004, pp. 951–952]**.

### 2.5 Learning as a mapping from data to grammars under constraints

35. Put together: a learner is a function from finite input (strings, ideally paired with meanings, with no reliable labels for non-sentences) to grammars, chosen from a constrained class, under a success criterion; the constraints (the hypothesis class, the linking rules, the semantic criteria) are not learned from that input and must come from somewhere else, on Pinker's view from natural selection **[checked: P1979, pp. 218–232; P2004, p. 949; P1995, 4.1; Edge1997 for the source in selection]**.

### 3(a). Which transports learning from external data needs, between what and what (S78)

The semantics' transport (Part IV) is a map *t = (π, τ, σ, λ)* from one organization *D* to another *E*, faithful on a contract when parts and whole agree under every admitted change (Part V's F1 and F2); its functional form is π∘S_a = T_a∘π for each admitted change (the represented process tracks the target process step by step); its relational form is a relation that preserves transitions forward, and backward too for equivalence (Part VIII, T1). On Pinker's account, read in these terms, learning from external data needs at least five transports before it can begin. The first two carry data in; the third lets the data be read; the fourth and fifth make generalisation possible.

**A point the semantics already makes.** The semantics' Argument 3, "Selected transports are underdetermined on unseen changes their population leaves open", is the logical problem of language acquisition in other words: what a finite history *H* leaves open about a transport is what its population 𝒯 leaves open (Part IV, Selected). Pinker's 1979 Figure 2 says the same about the learner's prior class of grammars (item 22). This match is worth giving Astra: on both accounts, generalising beyond the data is fixed by the candidates, not by the data.

**Who selects.** On Pinker's account the selector in learning is inside the learner (its strategy tests hypotheses against the input, item 19); the world only supplies data, and supplies no verdicts on wrong answers (item 25). The owner's S72 makes the Avida execution environment the selector, as an autonomous agent selects. Mapped onto Pinker, that puts the execution environment in the learner's place and the programs in the place of its hypotheses; then T1 has to bring in data from something the execution environment does not contain. In every Avida set-up so far, what the execution environment tests against is its own task list or a rule in its own code (S117, S120), so on Pinker's account it has been checking its hypotheses against itself, not learning from outside.

**What would be minimal.** A minimal case of learning from external data, on this account: a source *W* outside the selector with a regularity the selector does not hold (T1); some second view of the same events, or at least a way to tell which data belong together (T2, which can be weak: even an ordering that the learner can use counts, item 22); a fixed, prior link from the learner's categories to features of the data (T3); and a bounded candidate class (T5). Then T4 can be built and tested on data not yet seen.

### 3(b). Is Avida the right environment? What it supplies and lacks on Pinker's account (S78)

**A lead from Avida itself.** Pontes, Mobley, Ofria, Adami and Dyer (2020) evolved navigation and associative learning in digital organisms in a custom version of Avida: organisms had to follow nutrient trails whose cues they could sense, and each was tested on a randomly chosen trail before reproducing. They found that patterns stable across generations favour reflexes, while "environmental patterns that vary across generations but remain consistent for periods within an organism's lifetime foster the evolution of learning behavior"; reflexes evolved first and were the building blocks of learning, and an intrinsic value system evolved alongside **[checked: Pontes2020, abstract via PubMed]**. This matches Pinker's split exactly: what is the same in every generation is built in (the faculty); what differs between generations but holds for a life (which language one's community speaks) is learned **[Claude's reading]**. It also shows that Avida can be changed to supply T1 (sensed cues from a world the organism moves in) and to make within-lifetime learning pay. That work was not reproduced here.

**The options for S78's question, set out, not chosen:**

- **(i) Avida as it stands.** Right for studying selection (S111 to S118), wrong for learning from external data on Pinker's account: no T1 in his sense, no T2, no T3.
- **(ii) Avida with an external source.** The execution environment pays programs for matching a stream that comes from outside Avida's code (a recorded physical signal, another process, a data file not written for the purpose), so the selector checks its candidates against something it does not contain. This keeps the S72 framing (the environment as selector). It adds T1 only; T2 and T3 stay absent, and Gold's results predict slow progress unless the candidate class is narrowed.
- **(iii) Avida organisms as learners within a lifetime**, as in Pontes2020: patterns that vary between generations but hold within a life, sensed through inputs. This moves the learner into the program and needs a modified Avida.
- **(iv) Another environment** with a second channel built in (two views of the same events). The S123 agent should say what it would cost to build T2 in Avida before choosing.

### 3(c). The owner's meanings of knowledge, and where Pinker agrees or conflicts with Deutsch and Marletto

**The owner's three properties (S59) and S76.** Pinker's knowledge (item 5) is defined by representation and inference, not by copying, resisting change and remaining. But his account has a level where the three properties hold: inherited adaptations, which persist because they are copied in genes and kept by selection; Fodor's review puts it that modules contain "lots of specialised information about the problem-domains that they compute in" **[checked: F1998]**. And a language, on Pinker's own evidence, keeps itself in being in the owner's sense: each generation's speech is the next generation's input, and children rebuild a full grammar even from a pidgin (item 26). That is copying by reconstruction, not by relay; in the semantics' words (Part X), "Reconstruction by a learner is construction; relay is not." Avida's program replication is relay. On S76 (a causal effect on the surroundings, not only on other programs): on Pinker's account knowledge affects the world through action guided by accurate representations, and new knowledge is limited by the rate at which the world can be tested (item 14); nothing in it requires knowledge to change the rules of its own environment.

**Where Pinker agrees with Deutsch and Marletto.**

- **Data alone do not make knowledge.** Pinker: the data alone are insufficient (item 18). Deutsch: knowledge is "conjectured explanations", not "generating or justifying extrapolations from sense data" **[checked: Aeon2012]**. Both deny that a learner reads knowledge off its input; the S89 record has Deutsch saying that non-explanatory systems cannot reach evidence they have not met **[checked: S89 record, quoting Deutsch p. 73]**, which is Gold's problem seen from the other side.
- **Children form explanations.** In 2023, after Pinker said children are not doing "pure cryptography" on the signal, Deutsch said "Yes, they're forming explanations." and Pinker answered "They're forming explanations, exactly." **[checked: DP2023]**
- **Inherited adaptations carry knowledge.** Deutsch: "biological knowledge is non-explanatory, and therefore has limited reach" **[checked: S89 record, quoting Deutsch p. 78]**; Marletto: genes are knowledge as information that keeps itself instantiated and enables transformations **[checked: S110 record, O1 to O4 and its notes, which quote and paraphrase Marletto and Deutsch]**. Pinker: innate faculties are adaptations full of domain information (items 8, 10). They agree that selection makes knowledge in this sense.
- **Knowledge needs the world.** Both said in 2023 that knowledge is the rate-limiting step and must be won from the world by experiment (item 14).

**Where they conflict.**

- **Universality.** Pinker: intelligence is "an algorithm that can solve certain problems in certain environments, and maybe not others in other environments"; AGI is incoherent; specialisation is more efficient **[checked: DP2023]**; and there is no universal true-belief-fixer outside mathematics and logic (item 11). Deutsch: computational universality guarantees "there exists some program which will meet the criteria for being an AGI", the problem being the knowledge to write it, and one cannot tell in advance what knowledge a problem will need **[checked: DP2023]**; people are universal explainers **[checked: S89 record, M10, Deutsch p. 146]**. The semantics abstains (Part XIII: "Recursion does not entail universality").
- **Adaptation against explanation.** For Pinker, most of the machinery that makes knowledge was selected for an ancestral world and is only fitted for it (items 11, 12); explanation in the strong sense is a social, cumulative achievement (item 13). For Deutsch, creating explanations is what distinguishes people, it is one universal logic of conjecture and criticism, and it cannot be got by improving performance on tasks **[checked: Aeon2012]**.
- **Where the needed constraints come from.** Pinker: from natural selection, as innate constraints (items 10, 18, 35). Deutsch: from creative conjecture, with nothing more than an infinitesimal amount built in to a universal machine **[checked: DP2023]**; in the same dialogue he calls creativity the main determinant of human behaviour, beyond genes and environment, while Pinker answers by treating heritability as a measure of differences within a sample **[checked: DP2023, on heritability]**.
- **Can selection create explanatory knowledge?** Deutsch: no, biological knowledge is non-explanatory (above). Pinker: selection built the faculties that, within a lifetime, make the knowledge a person has; he does not claim that selection itself writes explanations **[Claude's reading of items 1, 10, 11; no source found where Pinker addresses the question in these words]**.

**What this means for S77's hypothesis of knowledge in Avida.** There are at least three rival readings to test, not one: (K1) constructor theory's and the owner's: information that keeps itself in being by causing its own copying and resisting change (S59), which Avida's programs already show (S111); (K2) Deutsch's: such information arisen by error correction, with explanatory knowledge a further kind that selection does not make (S89, S110); (K3) Pinker's: accurate representations whose causal transitions preserve accuracy, produced within a lifetime by a learner whose constraints were selected. Avida has instances of K1, arguably of K2's non-explanatory part, and none of K3, because no Avida program represents anything it learned from data within its life.

### 3(d). Is a learned grammar selected, constructed or declared? And the analogue in Avida

The semantics gives each transport whose domain is physical exactly one provenance (Part IV): **selected** (a population of candidates, a variation operator, a finite history, a survival condition requiring fidelity on that history; and no earlier occurrence represents the transport, the history, the survival condition or the codomain, D12.1); **constructed** (an episode whose construction trace prepares it, in which it, or the organization it carries to, is a represented target); or **declared** (neither). On Pinker's account a child's grammar has parts of each shape. Three readings, for the S123 agent and the owner, none applied:

- **Reading 1: selected.** The grammar is the member of a bounded candidate class (T5) that survived on the finite history of heard sentences (T1), under the survival condition *consistent with the input and with the constraints*. The fit to Pinker's own framing is close (items 19, 21, 22). But D12.1's negative clause asks that nothing earlier represent the transport's codomain, and the child does represent the meaning side, from context (item 27; T2). So, on the semantics' own definition, the grammar as a sound–meaning transport fails the selected provenance.
- **Reading 2: constructed.** The meanings are represented targets in each episode, the child's processes run inside its own boundary, and what is built is a binding between forms and meanings (the argument structure of *fill*, item 32), not a relay of content: "Reconstruction by a learner is construction; relay is not" (Part X). The *pilk* experiment (item 32) even looks like an intervention test of a constructed binding: change the meaning component and the syntax changes with it, which is what the semantics' Dependence condition asks of an account.
- **Reading 3: constructed on selected material.** The hypothesis class, the linking rules and the semantic features are selected (phylogeny, T3 and T5); the particular grammar is constructed within them. The semantics allows this ("Construction may operate on selected material", Part IV). **Declared** applies only to a theorist's claim that a child has some innate rule when no history is given.

**The analogue in Avida.** An evolved Avida program's capability is selected in the semantics' sense (S117), with the open point S117 left to the owner (whether the task list and its checking code count in the program's history). No Avida program has a represented target, so nothing in Avida is constructed. The nearest analogue of Reading 3 would be an execution environment (the selector, S72) that holds a represented target taken from external data (T1, T2) and builds programs against it; or, on option (iii), a program that, within its life, binds a sensed cue to an action (Pontes2020), which would be a candidate for construction on selected material if its binding were made within its own run and tested as above.
