## 5. The proposed experiment, assessed

**Well formed?** Mostly. Arms, the measure (common tasks at 50,000, plus the pay change at an identical endpoint), the controls (M without memory, Y with T's schedule replayed blind, a register version to check the arithmetic) and what counts against are stated before any outcome. Two pieces are left open: the total pay B and its mapping to Avida's reward values ("fix B ... before outcome runs"), and the audit's step limit. Both must be fixed before it can run.

**What it would show about the owner's question.** The causal check (part one) would show, cheaply and exactly, that a selector with a fast and a slow trace pays differently after "rose then fell" than after "fell then rose" at the same present. That is true by construction of the formula (the reply's own arithmetic shows it: 0.543 against 0.51), so it is a check of the plumbing, not a finding about Avida. Part two (continuations) is the real question: does paying for a **rising** share, on top of rarity, make the program population come to do more tasks? A positive result would say that an execution environment that remembers the direction of change selects more productively than one that sees only the present. It would still be grade 2: rarity and "rising" are written rules, and the tasks are Avida's 77.

**Flaws.**
1. **The temporal bonus is small.** max(fast − slow, 0) is a few hundredths for ordinary changes (0.033 in the reply's own example) against a rarity term near 1 for every uncommon task; after normalization over 77 tasks and the 0.5/77 floor, T and M offer nearly the same pay. A null result would then say little about memory. The size of the bonus is a free constant that decides the outcome.
2. **"Rising" is reply 02's selector A of S119**, which S120 found pays almost nothing once nothing rises. Here the rarity term keeps pay up (no stall), but the bonus itself goes quiet whenever shares are flat, which is most of the time after the first tasks (S113: every count levelled off).
3. **Pay scale.** As in reply 01 and S120's pilot of S119's reply 02: unless B is large (S118's P1 paid up to one doubling per task; S120's 0.1 per task made nothing common in 20,000 updates), all three arms may do nothing from the task-free ancestor, and the comparison is empty.
4. **The ten-task effect size** is taken from S113's spreads in two other execution environments; the variance in T, M and Y is unknown. The reply says so.
5. **Reload effects** are matched across arms but not removed; the reply says so.

**Cost.** Part one: no Avida run beyond one restored snapshot per history pair (seconds). Part two: 3 arms × 6 seed blocks × 50,000 updates = 18 CPU-hours at the brief's rate, about 10 at the rate measured in the S122 pilot from the ancestor (more as programs do tasks), 6 wall-hours at three processes; 36 blocks: 108 CPU-hours (about 60 at the pilot's rate), 36 wall-hours. Both need the owner's word.

## 6. What it adds to the owner's question

- **The clearest statement so far of what timing can and cannot give the execution environment**: persistence, forecast, alarm, release, all cheap; but every one of them still needs to be told **what** it is about (which task, which program, which case), and that "what" is where the named targets live. Making the instinct a dynamics does not by itself lower the grade.
- **A third reading of the owner's idea** that is open to test and does not depend on neurons: schedules nobody enumerated, arising from the feedback loop between the execution environment and its program population (reply 01's design is a case of it).
- **The identical-present, different-history check** as the minimum any temporal selector must pass before costly runs.

## 7. Strengths and weaknesses

- **Strengths**: strict on grades; separates the plumbing check from the outcome; arithmetic exact; its limits section is careful (no circuit recovers what was never recorded).
