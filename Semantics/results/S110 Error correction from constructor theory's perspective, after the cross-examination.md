## In brief

- **Error correction is not defined in the book.** Its forms (error-corrected, error-correction, error correction, error-correct) occur five times, in chapters 1, 2 and 7 [Xd1: as sent, "in chapters 1 and 7 only"]. What the book gives is a setting in which it is needed: laws of physics that preserve nothing complex without help, so that any complex structure decays unless something prevents and corrects its errors (ch. 1).
- **In the book's terms it is a transformation performed reliably by something that keeps the ability to perform it again** [implied]: the mole cricket that clears its hole every day, the cell that repairs copied DNA, the factory that fixes a flaw in its line. The book calls such a thing a catalyst (ch. 5).
- **Error correction needs information** [implied]: to put a thing back one must tell the damaged state from the intended one and copy the intended one in; the book's information is exactly the possibility of those two operations (ch. 3). Deutsch makes the same point from the other side: in a continuous quantity no error can be told from a correct value at sight (Deutsch, ch. 6).
- **Knowledge, in the book, is information that keeps itself in being** (ch. 1, ch. 5). Under laws that preserve nothing complex, keeping itself in being means taking part in the correction of its own errors [implied]. So in constructor theory knowledge and error correction are tied at the root: knowledge is information that recruits error correction for itself.
- **Two kinds of correction appear and the book keeps them apart without naming the difference** [reading]: correcting a *copy* (the scribe, the cell) and correcting a *content* (criticism, which the book describes as seeking and correcting errors in drafts, ch. 1, paraphrase). Copying errors are both what the first kind fights and, at the right pace, the raw variation that natural selection works on (ch. 1).
- **On creative agents the book says little beyond a definition**: creativity is the ability to create new knowledge, by thinking; human brains have it; how it works is open, and so is a question the book asks outright: “What is it, exactly, that we have to store and error-correct?” (Marletto, ch. 7) In its own table of laws, the entry for the laws of knowledge reads “To be discovered!” (Marletto, ch. 7).

## 3. Error correction: what the book states

Every place the book speaks of error or its correction, in order. **[book]** throughout this section.

1. **Why correction is needed at all** (ch. 1). Under no-design laws most interactions introduce small glitches into any complex structure: “Unless something intervenes to prevent and correct those errors, the structure will eventually fade away or collapse.” (Marletto, ch. 1) The more complex and unlike elementary stuff a system is, the harder it is to counteract its errors (paraphrase). The example is the scribe who copies a manuscript and double-checks each word (ch. 1, paraphrase).
2. **Correction of copies in the cell** (ch. 1). Genes are copied letter by letter, like a scribe's text, and the copy “it can be error-corrected via a similar method, which in bacteria is implemented by the cell once the replication has happened.” (Marletto, ch. 1) This is how DNA's pattern outlives any one bacterium (paraphrase).
3. **Copying errors as variation** (ch. 1). Because the laws are no-design, “errors can happen as a result of the interaction with the environment: these result in nonpurposeful changes (‘variations’) in the replicators.” (Marletto, ch. 1) The book adds that the rate of copying errors matters: “When errors happen at the right pace, not too often, and not too rarely, they produce novel variants of the genes” (Marletto, ch. 1). Natural selection then keeps the variants able to keep themselves in existence; it is blind, and most changes lead to extinction (paraphrase).
4. **Criticism as correction of content** (ch. 1). Following Popper, the book says that “knowledge creation always starts with a problem, which we can think of as a clash between different ideas someone has about reality.” (Marletto, ch. 1) Conjectures are the analogue of variation and may be full of errors; then: “Criticism is the act of seeking and correcting errors in an attempt to improve on the solutions—the analogue of natural selection.” (Marletto, ch. 1) The process may be opaque to the one doing it (paraphrase).
5. **What correction leaves behind** (ch. 1). A final product of conjecture and criticism may be a masterpiece: “it is hard to change further, while still meeting the criteria, because it has been obtained by tentatively removing flaws in previous versions” (Marletto, ch. 1). (Noted only, S34.)
6. **Fallibility** (ch. 1). The process is tentative: no-design laws give no guarantee of progress. “There are no absolute sources of certain truth: any good solution to a problem may also contain some errors.” (Marletto, ch. 1) Fallibilism, it adds, tells us that errors are “extremely interesting things to look for.” (Marletto, ch. 1)
7. **Jumps** (ch. 1). “natural selection, unlike conjecture and criticism, cannot perform jumps” (Marletto, ch. 1): each step of a lineage must itself survive; a sequence of ideas leading to a good idea need not consist of good ideas (paraphrase). Dogmas harm knowledge creation because they restrain conjecture and criticism (ch. 1, paraphrase).
   **7a. Testability and the progress of science** (ch. 2) [Xd1: missing as sent]. After Galileo's experiments refute Aristotle's theory of falling bodies, which is then dismissed (paraphrase), the book says that the testability of predictions “is central for the possibility of error correction, and therefore for the progress of science as a whole.” (Marletto, ch. 2) Explanations whose predictions are found wrong become problematic and are usually dropped for alternatives (paraphrase).
8. **The cricket** (ch. 5). The author's changes to a mole cricket's hole were undone every day; when the cricket left, “my changes to the hole, and all the other changes that were naturally occurring, were no longer corrected.” (Marletto, ch. 5) The hole faded in a few days (paraphrase).
9. **The factory** (ch. 5). A slight flaw in a production line is repaired: “If it is a well-run factory, there is a way to fix the problem and thus restore the production process.” (Marletto, ch. 5) The recipe makes construction reliable because “the final product is checked against the procedure until it meets the criteria set by the quality control of the company.” (Marletto, ch. 5) What the factory cannot lose is the recipe, including the instructions for repairing the factory (paraphrase).
10. **Open questions** (ch. 7). Among questions the book says become scientific once knowledge is defined objectively: whether death can be deferred “by means of some sort of error correction” (Marletto, ch. 7), and, in the next sentence of the same list of questions, “What is it, exactly, that we have to store and error-correct?” (Marletto, ch. 7)

**What the book does not state.** It gives no definition of error, of correction, or of error correction; no condition under which a correction succeeds; no account of how criticism is carried out in a brain; nothing on error-correcting codes, noise or channel capacity; and no law about error correction. Its statements that error correction bears on knowledge are item 1, together with the definition of knowledge as resilient information, and item 7a, which makes it the condition of the progress of science [Xd1].

## 4. Error correction: what the book implies (short arguments)

Each of these is **[implied]**: the premises are the book's, the step is Claude's, and each can be attacked at its step.

**I1. Reliable error correction is a catalysed task.** The book: any transformation performed reliably and accurately needs a catalyst, a thing that keeps its ability to perform it again (ch. 5, section 2). Restoring a damaged structure to its intended form, again and again, is a transformation performed reliably (the cricket's hole, item 8; the cell's repair of copied DNA, item 2). So reliable error correction is a task performed by a catalyst: a task that takes any state in a set of damaged states back into the intended set, by something that stays able to do it. *Step to attack*: whether "restoring" is a transformation in the book's sense when the damaged states are not listed in advance. The book's own tasks are given as input–output lists (the flip, the copy, ch. 3); a correcting task must take a whole family of possible damages as inputs.

**I2. Resilience under no-design laws needs error correction.** Item 1 says so for complex structures. Resilience is the ability to keep oneself in existence in a changing environment (section 2). So any resilient complex structure is one in whose surroundings, or in which, some error correction runs (the cell for DNA, the scribes for the manuscript, the maintenance crew for the factory). *Step to attack*: whether redundancy alone (many copies, some of which survive) counts as correction. The book's natural selection keeps a recipe in being by many copies and the loss of the failures, with no single copy repaired (item 3); on the book's words that is resilience, and whether it is error correction is not said.

**I3. Knowledge takes part in its own error correction.** Knowledge is information that keeps itself instantiated (section 2); by I2, a complex instantiation is kept only where error correction runs; and the book's example is one in which the correcting organization is itself built by the information it keeps (the cell that repairs DNA is built by the DNA's recipe: in the paragraph of item 2 the book says that “the rest of the new cell is constructed by executing the recipe in the DNA” (Marletto, ch. 1), and then that the cell error-corrects each copy, so the joined example is the book's own [Xd4: as sent, the citation joined item 2 to chapter 5]; of the genome, chapter 5 adds that “it causes itself to remain instantiated in physical systems over generations” (Marletto, ch. 5)). So knowledge, in the book's sense, is information that brings about the correction of its own copies, directly or through what it builds. *Step to attack*: the book's definition says "capable of" remaining instantiated, which may be met by a very simple, very stable pattern needing no correction; the book's own restriction to complex, non-elementary structures (ch. 1, ch. 5) is what rules that out, and it is a restriction stated for recipes, not written into the definition.

**I4. Only information can be error-corrected in this sense.** To correct, a corrector must tell whether the present state is in the intended set (a distinguishing, copy-like operation) and must be able to put the intended state back (setting the state, the flip's kind of operation). Those are the two operations that make a system an information medium (ch. 3); and the book itself counts telling states apart as a copy: its second property is that the states “can be received and distinguished in some other location” (Marletto, ch. 3), which it goes on to call, in its own words, “the possibility of performing a copy-like operation.” (Marletto, ch. 3) So a corrector that tells the medium's states apart performs a copy from the medium [Xd7]. So what is error-corrected is always something the book counts as information. The book says the matching thing about bits: “many slight variants of the states of the physical system carry the same information” (Marletto, ch. 7), which is what lets a small drift be undone. Deutsch states the other side: in a continuous quantity “there is no way of distinguishing an erroneous value from a correct one at sight” (Deutsch, ch. 6), so universal computers are digital, and “information that cannot be reliably retrieved is not really being stored.” (Deutsch, ch. 6) *Step to attack*: whether the corrected thing itself must admit flip and copy, or only be told apart and set by its corrector; the book's copy-like operation makes the two the same for the states the corrector reads, and whether it does for states the corrector cannot read is open [Xd7]. And whether content-correction (criticism, item 4) needs the same two operations. In criticism the "intended state" is not given in advance: it is what the problem asks for, and is itself conjectural.

**I5. The book's two kinds of correction differ in what counts as the error.** In a copy, an error is a departure from the source (the scribe's typo, the DNA mismatch): the standard exists before the correction and is the thing copied. In criticism, an error is a flaw in a conjecture relative to a problem, a clash between ideas (item 4): the standard is itself a conjecture that can change. In natural selection there is no represented standard at all: what counts as an "error" is only what fails to keep itself in existence in an environment (item 3, and chapter 5's requirement, quoted under I7, that an adaptation be useful in its environment). So the book uses "error" for departures from three different kinds of standard: a source, a problem, an environment. *Step to attack* [Xd9]: the book calls copying slips errors (item 3) and flaws in conjectures errors (item 4), but never calls a variant's failure to survive an error; the third kind is this argument's classification.

**I6. Knowledge can come about without design, because error correction can.** The book states that “the laws of physics allow for knowledge creation, but they do not guarantee it.” (Marletto, ch. 1) It also states, of evolution with no designer, that “Natural selection is a process that, by nonpurposefully selecting for biological adaptations, can accidentally create knowledge.” (Marletto, ch. 1) The argument: variation needs only copying errors, which no-design laws supply (item 3); selection needs only that some variants fail to keep themselves in being, which no-design laws also supply (ch. 1); the copy correction that keeps the successful recipe needs only a catalyst the recipe itself codes for (I3). None of the three needs a designer. What the book adds for thought is that its variation (conjecture) and its correction (criticism) can jump (item 7), and it says of the knowledge that sustains civilisation that “it is produced by thinking, and it can reach further than knowledge that emerges directly by natural selection.” (Marletto, ch. 1) *Step to attack* [Xd9]: the third clause leans on I3, which is itself attackable; and the book's word is "accidentally", while "because error correction can" is this argument's step.

**I7. Resilience is relative to an environment, so knowledge is too.** The book: “To be resilient in a given environment, an adaptation needs to be useful” (Marletto, ch. 5); the same piece of DNA may or may not be an abstract catalyst, the chapter says, “depending on whether or not it can propagate itself” (Marletto, ch. 5), and the pine's yellow-needle adaptation is not one where insects eat yellow plants (ch. 5). So whether a piece of information is knowledge depends on the environment it is in. And the book says what adaptations are knowledge *of*: “for adaptations, it is knowledge of some features of the environment” (Marletto, ch. 1). This bears on the earlier frameworks' claim that constructor theory's knowledge is "a one-place predicate on information" (FW3 T2, FW4 Part VI): on the book's own examples it has at least an environment place, and an "of" [reading on the frameworks, argued in the change map and in file 3]. *Step to attack* [Xd9]: whether the environment and the "of" are places of the definition or only of the book's informal use (section 7, item 5).

## 5. Creative agents: what the book says and does not say

**[book]**

- The book defines, in its closing chapter, “creativity as the ability to create new knowledge” (Marletto, ch. 7). Of human brains it says plainly: “Human brains have this ability.” (Marletto, ch. 7) Other systems may have it, much less manifestly (ch. 7, paraphrase).
- The knowledge that sustains civilisation is made by thinking; “Such knowledge consists of thoughts.” (Marletto, ch. 1)
- Knowledge creation in the mind follows Popper: problem, conjecture, criticism (ch. 1); “The thinking process, in contrast, can perform jumps.” (Marletto, ch. 1)
- It can stall: “Particularly detrimental to knowledge creation are the immutable limitations imposed by dogmas, as they restrain the ability to conjecture and criticise.” (Marletto, ch. 1)
- The objective definition of knowledge makes creativity a subject for physics: whether AI can be creative, how to set up creativity in a computer, what must be stored and error-corrected (ch. 7, paraphrase). Of this step the book says: “That is still very far from providing a theory of those phenomena” (Marletto, ch. 7).
- Whether knowledge can be created, destroyed or transformed is posed as the question for a future theory: “This problem is a deep one, and it is only partially solved so far.” (Marletto, ch. 5)
- The book's closing image: a journey ends well when knowledge has been created along the way and the traveller keeps the capacity to create more (ch. 7, paraphrase); a reader who creates knowledge while reading keeps its implications for as long as the reader's mind survives (ch. 7, paraphrase).

**What the book does not say.**

- How to tell, in a mind, which thoughts are knowledge. Its test for knowledge, “it is exactly the thing one would ultimately have to eliminate in order to prevent a particular transformation from being performed reliably.” (Marletto, ch. 5), is applied to a cricket's genome and an aircraft factory's recipe, never to a thought.
- Whether a thought that keeps itself in being by being repeated, and that is mistaken, is knowledge. The definition, read literally, counts it; the book neither says so nor denies it. Deutsch, on whom the definition leans, names such ideas: “Memes of the older, static-society kind, which survive by disabling their holders’ critical faculties, I shall call anti-rational memes.” (Deutsch, ch. 15) Of his example, a story about a hobgoblin told to frighten children, he says “its overt content – the idea of the hobgoblin’s existence – need contain no truth.” (Deutsch, ch. 15), yet the meme must carry knowledge: “it is essential that its implicit knowledge of these vulnerabilities be true and deep.” (Deutsch, ch. 15) On that reading such a thought is knowledge, but knowledge *of the minds it lives in*, not of what it says [reading].
- How explanation relates to knowledge. The book treats a physical theory as a "conjectured explanation" (ch. 1, paraphrase) and says thought reaches further; it does not say whether an explanation, as such, is resilient information, or what makes it so.
- What criticism is physically: which transformations, on which media, carry it out.
- Any law of knowledge: the table in chapter 7 gives, as the physical laws of knowledge, “To be discovered!” (Marletto, ch. 7).

**Deutsch, where the book leans on him [book: Deutsch].** Of the information that genes and brains evolved to store he says that “once they are physically embodied in a suitable environment, they tend to cause themselves to remain so.” (Deutsch, ch. 4) He goes straight on to say how such information arises: “Such information – which I call knowledge – is very unlikely to come into existence other than through the error-correcting processes of evolution or thought.” (Deutsch, ch. 4) On computation, two chapters later, he writes: “Without error-correction all information processing, and hence all knowledge-creation, is necessarily bounded. Error-correction is the beginning of infinity.” (Deutsch, ch. 6) Later still he treats the growth of knowledge itself as error correction, in a sentence that begins “Since the growth of knowledge is a process of error-correction, and since there are many more ways of being wrong than right” (Deutsch, ch. 11). And his glossary defines being rational as “actively pursuing error-correction by creating criticisms of both existing ideas and new proposals.” (Deutsch, ch. 1) So what Marletto leaves implicit, Deutsch states: the processes that create knowledge *are* error-correcting processes. Marletto's book does not state this in these words.

## 6. Claude's reading

**[reading]** throughout; none of it is the book's.

1. **One picture.** From constructor theory's side, error correction is a retained ability to take the states of a carrier that have drifted out of an intended set back into it. It needs (a) states that can be told apart and set (information, I4); (b) a standard, the intended set; (c) a thing that keeps the ability (a catalyst, I1). Knowledge is information that, in its environment, brings such correction about for itself (I3). A creative agent is, on the book's definition, something that creates new knowledge; the book leaves how.
2. **The standard is where the kinds part.** In copy correction the standard is the source; in selection there is none, only survival; in criticism it is a problem, which is conjectural and can change (I5). This is the point at which constructor theory's account stops and an account of creative agents has to start: constructor theory says *that* criticism corrects errors in content, and says nothing about where its standard comes from. The owner's own words say the same from the other side: whether a candidate matches the problem "is always tentative and may be wrong" (S21).
3. **Resilience is not the same thing as being corrected.** A thought can keep itself in being by being corrected against a problem (it survives criticism), or by disabling correction (Deutsch's anti-rational meme). Both are resilient; only the first is kept by the kind of correction the book calls criticism. The book's definition of knowledge does not tell them apart; its account of how knowledge is *created* (conjecture and criticism) does. This is the gap the owner's question (S57) lands in, and file 3 takes it up.
4. **The earlier frameworks' split lines up with the book's two kinds of correction.** FW2's "two layers of error correction" (one correcting errors of representation, by redundancy and settling; one correcting errors of content, by criticism) is the copy-versus-content distinction of I5, stated for a realization; FW5 kept the distinction and dropped the claim that the two need separate mechanisms (change map, theme "error correction").
5. **The present theory has error correction without a term of its own: the words "error correction" occur once, in the sentence quoted below, which carries the owner's S27** [Xc8: as sent, "without the word"]. It has criticism with bearing (K1), usable arguments (K2), what a test rules out (K3), repair (P), "a failed answer stays failed" (Part VIII), two responses to a violation (re-tuning by selection, or construction) (Part IV), and one sentence: that using a claim taken as given "is a costly gamble for all creative agents; and if this were not possible, error correction could become impossibly costly to perform" (Part IX, the owner's S27 in the theory). It has constructor theory's machinery in its physical module (tasks, possibility, retained realization) but not its information or its knowledge (change map, PT.20 to PT.23 and PT.32; interoperability without its name, PT.6) [Xd6: as sent, "PT.6 to PT.30"].

## 7. What is left open

1. What error correction is, as a task, when the damaged states cannot be listed (I1).
2. Whether redundancy with loss of failures is error correction or only resilience (I2).
3. Whether a simple, stable pattern that needs no correction is knowledge on the book's definition (I3).
4. Whether criticism needs the flip-and-copy operations of I4, and on what.
5. Whether knowledge, as the book uses it, has an environment place and an "of" place, and so whether it is one-place at all (I7; bears on FW3 T2 and FW4 R2).
6. Whether a mistaken, self-propagating thought is knowledge (section 5): the definition's literal reading says yes; the book is silent; Deutsch's meme passages say it is knowledge of its hosts.
7. How explanation and resilience are related: the book does not say whether being an explanation makes information more resilient, or under what conditions.

## 8. How the book relates to information theory

**[book]** Chapter 2 says the author and Deutsch developed the counterfactual approach by “applying it to information theory” (Marletto, ch. 2); chapter 6 calls the fact that work media are information media “a profoundly unifying link between information theory and thermodynamics” (Marletto, ch. 6). The book never names Shannon and never uses his measures (entropy, channel capacity, codes). Its information is defined by what can be done to a system (flip, copy), not by how many messages it can distinguish or with what probability. Chapter 4 adds that quantum media have pairs of observables that cannot be copied together: “quantum systems have at least two observables, such as P and SP, which are not copiable jointly” (Marletto, ch. 4). **[reading]** Shannon's theory takes for granted a medium whose states can be set and read; the book's two properties are what such a medium must be able to undergo. None of the six earlier frameworks or the present theory uses Shannon's measures (change map, section 7) [Xd5: as sent, "section 6"].
