1.  
(a) Part IX, heading "What a test refutes", label **(K3)**.

(b) "For \(T\land B\land I\Rightarrow O\), an established \(\neg O\) yields \(\neg T\), since \(B\) and \(I\) are background. (K3)"

(c) This is not a valid inference. From \(T\land B\land I\Rightarrow O\) and \(\neg O\), modus tollens gives only \(\neg(T\land B\land I)\), i.e. \(\neg T\lor\neg B\lor\neg I\). To conclude \(\neg T\) one needs the further premises \(B\) and \(I\). A failed test refutes the theory, the background, and the test setup together, not the theory alone. The document elsewhere acknowledges fallibility of observations and background, so treating \(B\) and \(I\) as automatically true is inconsistent.

2.  
(a) Part VI, heading "Work, support, and interference", under "Finite monotone theorem".

(b) "If \(\Gamma\) is finite, \(\mathsf S\) is upward closed, and \(\Gamma\in\mathsf S\), then the critical singletons are exactly \(\bigcap\min\mathsf S\) and the globally indispensable ones are exactly \(\bigcup\min\mathsf S\)."

(c) The second equality is false. A globally indispensable element is one that belongs to every support; under upward closure this is exactly an element of every minimal support, i.e. an element of \(\bigcap\min\mathsf S\). The set \(\bigcup\min\mathsf S\) contains every element that appears in any minimal support. Counterexample: \(\Gamma=\{a,b\}\), and \(\mathsf S\) is the upward closure of \(\{\{a\},\{b\}\}\). Then \(\min\mathsf S=\{\{a\},\{b\}\}\), \(\bigcup\min\mathsf S=\{a,b\}\), but neither \(a\) nor \(b\) is globally indispensable. The proof itself says deletion of \(d\) preserves support exactly when some minimal support omits \(d\), which entails the intersection, not the union.

3.  
(a) Part V, under "Account", the condition headed "Non-circular dependence".

(b) "There exists \((a,b)\in C\) that removes or replaces a nonempty block of \(\Gamma\) while preserving the other boundary conditions, under which the answer profile changes, ceases to be determined in the claimed way, or is left as it was."

(c) The final disjunct "or is left as it was" makes the condition vacuous or close to it. If removing or replacing a block of active commitments leaves the answer profile unchanged, that is exactly what should fail non-circular dependence, not satisfy it. As written, a circular explanation with an idle commitment can remove that commitment, observe that the answer is left as it was, and thereby satisfy the condition. The condition should require that the answer profile changes or ceases to be determined in the claimed way.

4.  
(a) Part XVI, Derivation 2, "Indistinguishable is identical".

(b) "Claim. Two candidates \(\mathcal E,\mathcal E'\) for the same \(p\) that both satisfy (F1), (F2), and (A) on \(C\) are one account at grain \(C\): their components are pairwise of one kind on \(C\) and their answer profiles coincide. Proof. Immediate from Derivation 1 and (A)."

(c) The conclusion does not follow. (A) gives that the answer profiles coincide, but (F1) and Derivation 1 do not give a bijection between the components of \(\mathcal E\) and \(\mathcal E'\), nor do they force the two candidates to use the same anchors in \(D\). Two different faithful decompositions of the same target can produce the same answers while using different internal components. The document itself says in Part 0 that systems with identical outputs and different internal routes are different organizations, and Part VI says two systems with the same output table may differ in which routes are active. Derivation 2 contradicts that.

5.  
(a) Part IV, "Three provenances", definition of **Constructed**; also Part X, "Construction is not selection".

(b) "Constructed. There is an episode (Part X) whose construction witness prepares \(t\), and in which \(t\), or the organization it targets, is available as a represented target; or \(t\) is the survivor of a variation-and-survival history on a population that contains it, which serves as its construction witness."

Later: "Construction is not selection. A selected transport has no represented target in its history; a constructed one does."

(c) The second disjunct of Constructed says that a survivor of a variation-and-survival history on a population containing \(t\) is constructed. But Selected is also defined as survival of a member of a population under variation and selection. Thus, as written, the same history can make \(t\) both selected and constructed, contradicting the claim that the provenances are distinguished and that construction is not selection. A mere survivor of selection need not have a represented target or a construction witness in the Part X sense, so the definition is internally inconsistent.

6.  
(a) Part V, formula (F1) and its use in Part XVI, Derivation 1.

(b) "\(\operatorname{proj}_{V_k}\!\big[\operatorname{Sol}_{\lambda(k)}(a,b)\big]=L_k(\tau(a),\sigma(b))\). (F1)"  
and in Derivation 1: "\(\operatorname{sig}_C(\lambda(k))=\{(a,b,\operatorname{proj}_{V_k}\operatorname{Sol}_{\lambda(k)}(a,b))\}\)".

(c) \(\operatorname{Sol}\) and \(\operatorname{sig}_C\) are defined for organizations and components, not for arbitrary subnetworks. In Part IV, \(\lambda(k)\) is defined only as "a subnetwork of \(D\)"; no definition is given for \(\operatorname{Sol}_{\lambda(k)}\) or for the signature of a subnetwork. Unless every anchor \(\lambda(k)\) is explicitly treated as an organization with its own boundary, ports, and hidden ports, (F1) and the proof of Derivation 1 contain an undefined expression. That is a formal gap in the central fidelity condition.

7.  
(a) Part VII, heading "Obstruction".

(b) "Twenty-four indivisible tokens cannot be split equally three ways; permitting division changes the state space and does not refute the scoped result."

(c) This is simply false. Twenty-four indivisible tokens can be split equally three ways: 8 tokens each. No cutting or division of a token is required. The intended example of an obstruction should use a number not divisible by three, such as twenty-three.

8.  
(a) Part XV, "What defeats this class", final paragraph.

(b) "A candidate under any of these headings counts as a defeat only once an episode satisfying (EK) has accepted it as one; until then the class stands."

(c) This makes defeat depend on acceptance by an episode, which contradicts the document's own explanatory realism in Part I: whether a transport is faithful, or a theorem is false, is independent of acceptance. A mathematical counterexample or false statement is a defect whether or not an episode satisfying (EK) has accepted it. As written, the clause immunizes the class from unaccepted counterexamples and turns objective errors into social-historical events.
