1. **(a)** Part VI, heading “Finite monotone theorem.”  
**(b)** “If \(\Gamma\) is finite, \(\mathsf S\) is upward closed, and \(\Gamma\in\mathsf S\), then the critical singletons are exactly \(\bigcap\min\mathsf S\) and the globally indispensable ones are exactly \(\bigcup\min\mathsf S\).”  
**(c)** This is reversed or false under the natural meanings. Let \(\Gamma=\{a,b\}\) and \(\mathsf S=\{\{a\},\{b\},\{a,b\}\}\). Then \(\min\mathsf S=\{\{a\},\{b\}\}\), with intersection \(\varnothing\) and union \(\{a,b\}\). But neither \(a\) nor \(b\) is globally indispensable, since \(\{a\}\) and \(\{b\}\) are each supports. The proof’s own last sentence implies that indispensable elements are those in every minimal support, i.e. the intersection, not the union. If “critical singleton” means critical for some support, then those are the union, not the intersection.

2. **(a)** Part XVI, Derivation 2, “Indistinguishable is identical.”  
**(b)** “Two candidates \(\mathcal E,\mathcal E'\) for the same \(p\) that both satisfy (F1), (F2), and (A) on \(C\) are one account at grain \(C\): their components are pairwise of one kind on \(C\) and their answer profiles coincide.”  
**(c)** The answer-profile claim follows from (A), but the component-kind claim does not. Take \(D\) with ports \(x,y,z\) and components \(y=x+1\), \(z=y^2\). Let \(E_1\) have one component \(z=(x+1)^2\) anchored to the whole \(D\) with hidden port \(y\), while \(E_2\) has two components \(y=x+1\) and \(z=y^2\) anchored separately. Both satisfy (F1), (F2), and (A), but \(E_1\)’s composite component has neither the footprint nor the signature of either component of \(E_2\). So the “one account at grain \(C\)” claim is false as stated.

3. **(a)** Part V, heading “Account.”  
**(b)** “The pair \(\mathcal E=(E,p,t,\Gamma)\) satisfies \(\operatorname{Account}(\mathcal E)\) exactly when the following four conditions hold” and later “\(\operatorname{Account}(\mathcal E)\iff \text{(F1)}\land\text{(F2)}\land\text{(A)}\land\text{NonCircular}\land\text{NonVacuous}\land\text{Coherent}.\)”  
**(c)** There are not four conditions. The section lists five named conditions—Component fidelity, Question fidelity, Non-circular dependence, Non-vacuity, and Baseline coherence—and the final biconditional contains six conjuncts because Component fidelity is split into (F1) and (F2). Part 0 also says “all four conditions of Account.” The central definition is internally inconsistent about how many conditions it imposes.

4. **(a)** Part IV, heading “Three provenances,” and the later paragraph beginning “Neither provenance is reducible to the other.”  
**(b)** “Constructed. There is an episode (Part X) whose construction witness prepares \(t\), and in which \(t\), or the organization it targets, is available as a represented target; or \(t\) is the survivor of a variation-and-selection history on a population that contains it, which serves as its construction witness.” Later: “a selected transport has no represented target and no criticism in its history; a constructed one has both.”  
**(c)** The second disjunct makes a survivor of a variation-and-selection history count as Constructed. That is the same kind of history used to define Selected, and it does not require a represented target or criticism. This contradicts the later claim that constructed transports always have represented targets and criticism, and it contradicts Part X’s statement that “Construction is not selection.” It collapses the two provenances the document says are irreducible.

5. **(a)** Part XIV, heading “Indices, not primitives,” against Part III, heading “A question can be wrong.”  
**(b)** Part XIV: “Grain \(\ell\), boundary \(\beta\), continuity \(\Omega\), and the contract \(C\) are declared indices. Every claim is relative to them; none is a predicate that could be true or false.” Part III: “A question may fail to pick out its alleged target, assume an incompatible baseline, or combine incompatible requirements.”  
**(c)** A contract cannot simultaneously be a mere index that is not true or false and something that can be wrong, fail to pick out its target, or be found and constructed. Part III gives contracts provenance and allows them to be discovered; Derivation 5 goes further and says a contract is an organization that can be built and be the content of origin. That is a substantive content, not a mere index. The two descriptions contradict each other.

6. **(a)** Part V, under “Non-circular dependence.”  
**(b)** “There exists \((a,b)\in C\) that removes or replaces a nonempty block of \(\Gamma\) while preserving the other boundary conditions, under which the answer profile changes, ceases to be determined in the claimed way, or is left as it was.”  
**(c)** The final disjunct “or is left as it was” is wrong for a non-circularity test: removing a block with no change to the answer shows the block was idle, not that the account was non-circular. The earlier sentence saying the target’s answer must not appear as an unanalysed boundary input is not formalized. As written, the formal condition can be satisfied by commitments that do no work, so it does not enforce the claimed exclusion of “\(p\) because \(p\).”

7. **(a)** Part XVI, Derivation 3, “Selected transports are underdetermined on unseen changes.”  
**(b)** “The component relations \(L_j(a,b)\) are supplied independently for each \((a,b)\). Survival on \(H\) constrains only \(\{L_j(a,b):(a,b)\in H\}\). Alter \(L_{j}(a,b)\) for one \((a,b)\notin H\) to any other admitted relation; the result survives on \(H\) and differs at \((a,b)\).”  
**(c)** The proof varies the wrong object. A transport is \(t=(\pi,\tau,\sigma,\lambda)\); the component relations \(L_j\) belong to the organization \(E\), not to \(t\). Selected provenance is defined over a population of candidate transports, so changing \(L_j(a,b)\) produces a different organization \(E'\) and does not show that a candidate transport \(t'\) exists in the original population. Unless selection is allowed to range over arbitrary organizations as well as transports, the claimed underdetermination does not follow.

8. **(a)** Part V, under “Non-vacuity,” against Part III, “Contracts have provenance.”  
**(b)** Part V: “The contract \(C\) is a declared subset of the physically admitted edits.” Part III: “\(\rho_p\in\{\text{declared},\ \text{selected},\ \text{constructed}\}\)” and “Any assessment may use any of the three.”  
**(c)** If “declared” has the provenance sense defined in Part III, then Non-vacuity says every account’s contract must be declared, which excludes selected and constructed contracts. But Part III says any assessment may use any of the three, and Part IV/Derivation 5 make constructed contracts central to question-finding. If “declared” is meant merely as “explicitly stated,” the wording clashes with the defined use of the term.

9. **(a)** Part IX, heading “What a test refutes.”  
**(b)** “For \(T\land B\land I\Rightarrow O\), an established \(\neg O\) yields \(\neg T\), since \(B\) and \(I\) are background. (K3)”  
**(c)** This is not a valid deductive step. From the implication and \(\neg O\), the valid conclusion is \(\neg T\lor\neg B\lor\neg I\). To conclude \(\neg T\), both \(B\) and \(I\) must be established true, not merely background. Counterexample: \(T\) true, \(B\) true, \(I\) false, \(O\) false makes the implication true and \(\neg O\) true, but \(\neg T\) false.

10. **(a)** Part IX, heading “Bearing.”  
**(b)** “Let \(p_\delta\) be the question about the defect. Then \(\operatorname{Bearing}(c,z,p)\iff\operatorname{Account}(\mathcal E_c,p_\delta). \tag{K1}\)”  
**(c)** The target \(z\) and original question \(p\) do not appear on the right-hand side. As formalized, any account of any defect \(\delta\) counts as bearing on any target and question, no matter how irrelevant. The text says a criticism has a connection, but the biconditional omits that connection, so K1 fails to capture its own bearing condition.

11. **(a)** Part XV, heading “What defeats this class.”  
**(b)** “A candidate under any of these headings counts as a defeat only once an episode satisfying (EK) has accepted it as one; until then the class stands.”  
**(c)** This makes the status of a mathematical error or counterexample depend on acceptance by a knowledge-creating episode. That contradicts Part I’s explanatory realism: “Whether a transport is faithful on a contract is independent of whether anyone accepts it.” A counterexample to a theorem refutes it whether or not a system satisfying (EK) accepts the refutation. The sentence is a protection mechanism, not a consequence of the semantics.

12. **(a)** Part XVI, Derivation 1, “Kind preservation is a theorem, not a condition.”  
**(b)** “By (K), \(\operatorname{sig}_C(\lambda(k))=\{(a,b,\operatorname{proj}_{V_k}\operatorname{Sol}_{\lambda(k)}(a,b))\}\) and \(\operatorname{sig}_{\tau[C]}(k)=\{(\tau(a),\sigma(b),L_k(\tau(a),\sigma(b)))\}\).”  
**(c)** This silently redefines the signature notion. Formula (K) defines \(\operatorname{sig}_C(j)\) only for a component \(j\), as \(\{(a,b,L_j(a,b))\}\). But \(\lambda(k)\) is a subnetwork of \(D\), not a component, and no relation \(L_{\lambda(k)}\) is defined for it. The proof therefore does not follow from the stated definition of kind in Part II unless an additional definition is supplied.

13. **(a)** Part IV, under “Selected,” in “Three provenances.”  
**(b)** “No member of the history represents \(t\), \(H\), or the survival condition.”  
**(c)** \(H\subseteq C\) is a set of edit–boundary pairs, so its members are ordered pairs \((a,b)\), not organizations or systems that could represent anything. The condition as written is meaningless. The intended restriction was presumably that no member of the population represents \(t\), \(H\), or the survival condition.
