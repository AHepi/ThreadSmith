### 5. What it gives up

It gives up the assertion that every unseen pair has a differing survivor merely because the selection history is incomplete. It gives up the inference from mathematical freedom to write a component relation to the existence of an admitted, realizable population member implementing it. It also gives up the blanket consequence that every untested value is unconstrained: population restrictions, physical relations or other stated constraints may already determine it. A finite incomplete history alone does not guarantee an available error-producing alternative or an actual surprise.

It retains fidelity on the tested history as the selection requirement; actual two-survivor underdetermination where its new premise is supplied; the possibility of fallibility where admitted cases permit it; the distinction between selected and constructed histories; and the necessary incomplete-history condition for surprise. It does not replace the source’s population with all imaginable devices, claim that a survivor must be wrong, or assert that a counterexample to one universal derivation defeats every definition of Account.

**[A-E48] Part: XVI. Heading: 4. Surprise requires an incomplete history.** Source: `authority/10 Claude Fable Semantics - standalone theory.md`, lines 575–581.

> ## 4. Surprise requires an incomplete history
>
> **Claim.** A system can be surprised only if it holds a transport selected on a history \(H\) strictly smaller than the contract \(C\) of changes the world admits.
>
> *Proof.* Surprise is defined as a violation at \((a,b)\notin H\). If there is no transport there is no expectation and hence no violation. If \(H=C\), every occurring \((a,b)\) is in \(H\), so no violation at \((a,b)\notin H\) exists. ∎
>
> **Consequence.** Surprise is not a feeling added to the semantics; it is the signature of a selected transport meeting a change outside its history. The two responses — extend \(H\) and re-tune, or construct a new transport — are the difference between learning and creating, and the semantics distinguishes them by their witnesses, not by their outcomes.
