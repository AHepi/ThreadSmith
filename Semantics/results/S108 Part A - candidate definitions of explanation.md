## 2. For the owner: the flagged candidates in plain words

Each: one sentence, an everyday example, and the past decision it seems to go against, in the owner's words. What the computation found is in §1.

**C1 (V1.1).** *In one sentence:* each part of an explanation has to match what its piece of the thing ends up doing inside the whole, not the rule that piece follows on its own. *Example:* explaining a bicycle's speed with a part standing for the gears: the part may no longer say "the gears multiply whatever the pedals do", only "the gears turn the wheel at the speed they turn it now, with this rider"; so the general gear rule stops counting and a part that copies what the whole bicycle does keeps counting. On your own case, the shop sign explained by a red part for Mondays and a blue part for Tuesdays would no longer be an explanation at all, while the sign explained by one part with both colours written in would still be one. *Seems to go against* S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C2 (V1.5).** *In one sentence:* something can only be explained in answer to a question that was itself picked out by trial or worked out; a question that was simply laid down is no question, so nothing answers it. *Example:* a customer simply asks "why is the sign red on Mondays?", with no story of how that question came about; under this reading no candidate explains the sign, neither your two-part explanation nor the one-part one, and nothing explains why the weathervane points north. (This is how it comes out because the program keeps no story of where any question came from; where that story is recorded as found or chosen, nothing changes.) *Seems to go against* S44: "In either case, it is an explanation." and S41 Q15: "Yes, it can be explained".

**C6 (V2.4).** *In one sentence:* something whose answer is simply written into one of its parts fails the tests and is not an explanation at all, not even a bad one. *Example:* "the sign has one part that is red on Mondays and blue on Tuesdays" would no longer be an explanation of the shop sign; neither would a table that just lists the answers. *Seems to go against* S45: "Yes, take the test out", and S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C7 (V2.5).** *In one sentence:* a link counts as picked out by trial even when nothing at all was tried, so a link someone simply writes down from nothing can be an explanation. *Example:* someone who has never watched a pendulum writes a formula on a napkin and says "this stands for the pendulum"; with nothing tried and nothing copied, under this reading it is an explanation. A student copying the same formula from a textbook still is not. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2: "No, not if just declared".

**C8 (V3.4).** *In one sentence:* a link someone worked out counts as worked out only if the explanation they leaned on while working it out itself passes the tests; if it does not, the link counts as merely declared. (Only under one reading of what "the construction" of a link is.) *Example:* a navigator works out a course with a method that does not hold for the open sea, and the course still comes out right for the harbour she was asked about; under this reading her course counts as merely declared, so it is not an explanation of the way to the harbour, although it passes every test there. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2, "No, not if just declared": the question you answered set a link simply declared against one found by trial or worked out, and her link was worked out.

**C11 (V4.1).** *In one sentence:* something whose answer is simply written into one of its parts can still pass the tests, but is not an explanation. *Example:* "the sign has one part that is red on Mondays and blue on Tuesdays" passes the tests but would not count as an explanation of the shop sign; nor would "it is red because it is red". *Seems to go against* S45: "Yes, take the test out", and S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C12 (V4.2).** *In one sentence:* anything that passes the tests is an explanation, even if its link to what it explains was only declared. *Example:* a student copies a pendulum formula that works and writes "this stands for the pendulum"; under this reading that is an explanation. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2: "No, not if just declared".
