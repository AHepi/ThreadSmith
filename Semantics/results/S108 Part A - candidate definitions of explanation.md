## 2. For the owner: the flagged candidates in plain words

Each: one sentence, an everyday example, and the past decision it seems to go against, in the owner's words. What the computation found is in §1.

**C1 (V1.1).** *In one sentence:* each part of an explanation has to match what its piece of the thing ends up doing inside the whole, not the rule that piece follows on its own. *Example:* explaining a bicycle's speed with a part standing for the gears: the part may no longer say "the gears multiply whatever the pedals do", only "the gears turn the wheel at the speed they turn it now, with this rider"; so the general gear rule stops counting and a part that copies what the whole bicycle does keeps counting. On your own case, the shop sign explained by a red part for Mondays and a blue part for Tuesdays would no longer be an explanation at all, while the sign explained by one part with both colours written in would still be one. *Seems to go against* S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C2 (V1.5).** *In one sentence:* something can only be explained in answer to a question that was itself picked out by trial or worked out; a question that was simply laid down is no question, so nothing answers it. *Example:* a customer simply asks "why is the sign red on Mondays?", with no story of how that question came about; under this reading no candidate explains the sign, neither your two-part explanation nor the one-part one, and nothing explains why the weathervane points north. (This is how it comes out because the program keeps no story of where any question came from; where that story is recorded as found or chosen, nothing changes.) Your engineer's bridge built to a fixed brief was not computed: the program keeps no story of where the brief came from. *Seems to go against* S44: "In either case, it is an explanation." and S41 Q15: "Yes, it can be explained"; perhaps S41 Q6: "Yes, it can" (not computed).

**C5 (V2.3).** *In one sentence:* a difference counts towards an explanation only when it comes with a change made to the thing; a difference that comes only from the thing's circumstances no longer counts. *Example:* a street lamp is on at night and off at noon; under this reading "because it is night" can no longer explain the difference, while "because someone flipped the switch" still can. Your own cases turn on how the change is read. If the north wind is read as a circumstance of the weathervane, not a change made to it, nothing explains why it points north; if Monday and Tuesday are read as circumstances of the sign, your red-part and blue-part explanation is no longer an explanation. If the wind and the day are read as changes made to the vane and the sign, as the program reads them, nothing changes. *Seems to go against*, on the first reading only, S41 Q15: "Yes, it can be explained", and S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C6 (V2.4).** *In one sentence:* something whose answer is simply written into one of its parts fails the tests and is not an explanation at all, not even a bad one. *Example:* a table that just lists the answers would no longer be an explanation; nor would "the sign has one part that is red on Mondays and blue on Tuesdays" (not your case: a companion the program sets beside it). Your own case, a red part that switches on on Mondays and a blue part that switches on on Tuesdays, stays an explanation on the program's reading of "written in", under which a part counts as written in only if it alone gives the answer at every point asked about (each of your parts gives it on one day only). On the other readings, where giving the answer at some of the points is enough, your two-part explanation stops being one too. *Seems to go against* S45: "Yes, take the test out"; and, on those other readings only, S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C7 (V2.5).** *In one sentence:* a link counts as picked out by trial even when nothing at all was tried, so a link someone simply writes down from nothing can be an explanation. *Example:* someone who has never watched a pendulum writes a formula on a napkin and says "this stands for the pendulum"; with nothing tried and nothing copied, under this reading it is an explanation. A student copying the same formula from a textbook still is not. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2: "No, not if just declared".

**C8 (V3.4).** *In one sentence:* a link someone worked out counts as worked out only if the explanation they leaned on while working it out itself passes the tests; if it does not, the link counts as merely declared. (Only under one reading of what "the construction" of a link is.) *Example:* a navigator works out a course with a method that does not hold for the open sea, and the course still comes out right for the harbour she was asked about; under this reading her course counts as merely declared, so it is not an explanation of the way to the harbour, although it passes every test there. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2, "No, not if just declared": the question you answered set a link simply declared against one found by trial or worked out, and her link was worked out.

**C11 (V4.1).** *In one sentence:* something whose answer is simply written into one of its parts can still pass the tests, but is not an explanation. *Example:* "it is red because it is red" passes the tests but would not count as an explanation; nor would "the sign has one part that is red on Mondays and blue on Tuesdays" (not your case: the program's companion to it). Your own red-part and blue-part explanation stays an explanation on the program's reading of "written in" (a part alone giving the answer at every point asked about), and stops being one on the other readings (giving it at some of the points). *Seems to go against* S45: "Yes, take the test out"; and, on those other readings only, S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C12 (V4.2).** *In one sentence:* anything that passes the tests is an explanation, even if its link to what it explains was only declared. *Example:* a student copies a pendulum formula that works and writes "this stands for the pendulum"; under this reading that is an explanation. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2: "No, not if just declared".
