## The sentence and what it does

L161 opens:

> A question may fail to pick out its alleged target, assume an incompatible baseline, or combine incompatible requirements. Its formulation is still an event. Exposing the defect is another question with its own contract.

The sentence does four things. Each is tied to a line.

1. **It keeps the exposing out of the defective question.** The defective question \(p\) keeps its formulation, "still an event" (L161, sentence 2), and exposing \(p\)'s defect does not rewrite \(p\). The word "another" does this work. L151 gives what makes a question another: "Two questions with the same \(D\) and different \((C,\mathcal Q)\) are different questions, and an answer to one is not an answer to the other."
2. **It gives the exposing an index.** "Its own contract" does this. By L367, "A new index is a new claim." By L155, every contract has a provenance \(\rho_p\). The next sentence of L161 reads "An assessment is an event with a frozen contract (Part 0, grievance 10)". So the exposing, which assesses \(p\), stands on a contract, and that contract is not \(p\)'s. The defect can lie in \(p\)'s target, its baseline or its requirements (sentence 1), so the exposing cannot inherit \(p\)'s terms.
3. **It prepares Part IX.** L377 gives the form the other question takes when a criticism alleges the defect: "Let \(p_\delta\) be the question whether \(z\) has \(\delta\) in respect of \(p\)". By L588–L590 a question's contract with its query can be a content, so the defective question can be the \(z\) of such a criticism.
4. **It keeps the exposing open.** The exposing sits under a question of its own. So whether \(p\) has the defect is a claim at that question's index. In Part IX, a criticism's bearing is (K1), \(\operatorname{Account}(\mathcal E_c)\), with \(\delta\) "alleged" (L377). Nothing at L161 says an exposing settles anything (S28).

## Mimo's point: "is" is loose against L377

The point holds only if "is" is read as an identity between an act (an exposing) and a question in the sense of L138, the tuple \(p=(D,\ C,\ b_0,\ \mathcal Q,\ O_p,\ \rho_p)\).

The text does not use "is … another question" that way. At L271 it writes: "It may be faithful under the identification contract, which is a different question (Part III)." A contract is not a six-part tuple either. There, "is a different question" places the matter under a different question. Read the same way at L161, "Exposing the defect is another question with its own contract" places the exposing under another question and gives that question a contract of its own. It does not say the exposing has nothing beyond that question. Mimo's "deflation" is therefore not in the sentence, and L377's premise and connection are not denied. Part IX states them, and no line needs L161 to state them.

The rival does not state them either. "Poses" leaves the premise and the connection as unsaid as "is" does. So for a reader who does take "is" as identity, the rival does not repair the looseness; it only removes the identity reading. On the text's own use of the idiom, there is no looseness to repair.

Mimo raises the point as a note ("Note also"), closes VARIES and not FAILS, and names no line that "is" breaks. **Not shown to be a defect.**

## Weighing Mimo's rival

**1. ", with a contract of its own" for " with its own contract".** Taken alone, this changes nothing the theory needs. It is not clearer:

- With the comma, the phrase can be read as attaching to the whole clause (the exposing poses a question, and does so with a contract of its own) instead of to "another question".
- The text's unbroken "another question with its own contract" binds the contract to the question. That is where L151 and L367 need it.

**2. "poses" for "is".** This is a different claim. There are four reasons for the text's word over this one.

- **(a) Where the exposing stands.** "Is" puts the exposing within the other question, so the exposing is assessed at that question's index. The next sentence, "An assessment is an event with a frozen contract (Part 0, grievance 10)", then applies to it directly, on its own contract and not on \(p\)'s. "Poses" puts the exposing before and outside the question it raises. Its own index is then left to inference; the sentence no longer states it.
- **(b) Which other question.** In plain English, "Exposing the defect poses another question" can be read as raising a follow-on question, such as what to ask instead. That is the replacement of L161's last sentence: "Supplying a meaning for a replacement query can make a coherent new question; it does not answer the original one." The text keeps that replacement apart from the exposing, and says only that it "can" happen. On that reading, the rival would say that every exposing raises a replacement question, and no line says so. "Is" does not open that reading, because the other question is the one the exposing is carried on in.
- **(c) L377, the line Mimo cites.** There, \(p_\delta\) is given by the target, the alleged defect and \(p\). The criticism's connection is the organization of "the explanatory candidate (Part V) for \(p_\delta\)". So the criticism works within \(p_\delta\): it offers a candidate for it. "Is another question", read as at L271, says the exposing is carried on in such a question. "Poses" says only that the exposing raises it. So the rival agrees with L377 no more closely than the text does.
- **(d) S28.** Mimo's step 3 stands: neither wording says the exposing settles the defect. The text keeps the matter open by placing the exposing itself under a question with its own contract, where the exposing is a claim at that index. The rival keeps it open too, but only through the question it poses, and it leaves the exposing's own standing unstated.

**Easy to vary?** Taken whole, the rival is a different claim (change 2), and change 1 is a rewording that is not clearer. So the sentence is **not shown easy to vary on this reading**. That is recorded here, and it is not thereby final.
