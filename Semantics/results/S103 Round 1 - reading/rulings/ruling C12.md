## The sentence and what it does

Line 127 opens: "These are descriptions of patterns in (K), not additional data. The semantics never asks whether a component "is" a cause. It asks what its signature is. In particular, a part that reads or reports another part has a measurement's signature: change only the reading and the part it reports stays as it was; change the part and the reading follows. Which of the two an account offers as producing an outcome is fixed by that signature, not by the account's wording."

"It" is the semantics, the subject of the sentence before (C11). "Its" is the component's: the only thing the text gives a signature to is a component, "The **signature** of component \(j\) on \(C\) is" (L113), \(\operatorname{sig}_C(j)=\{(a,b,L_j(a,b)):(a,b)\in C\}\) (L116, (K)). The sentence does three pieces of work:

1. **It says what replaces the question C11 turns away.** C11 says what the semantics never asks; C12 says what it asks instead. Without C12, C11 is a bare denial, open to the reading Mimo's C11 fault check names (reply line 262): that the semantics does not tell causes apart at all.

3. **It frames the next sentence.** "In particular, a part that reads or reports another part has a measurement's signature" (L127, fourth sentence) particularizes C12's question for a reading or reporting part.

It stands in the ask/never-asks pattern the text keeps: L11, "The definition never asks whether a piece of the explanation "is the same kind of thing" as a piece of the world."; L37; L397, "neither it nor anything else in the semantics asks that the person"; L407, "None of (R), Deploy and Build asks whether … what they ask is whether".

## Weighing the rivals

**Is each rival a rewording or a different claim?** A rewording, each.

- **"on \(C\)".** The text defines no signature without a contract. The term is introduced as "The **signature** of component \(j\) on \(C\)" (L113) and written \(\operatorname{sig}_C(j)\) (L116), with \(C\) fixed for the section by the sentence before it: "Fix an organization \(D\) and a contract \(C\subseteq A\times B\) (Part III)." (L113; C04, ruled KEEP). L119 then says "Kinds are therefore relative to the contract". So "its signature" at L127 already means its signature on \(C\); writing the index out asks the same question. GLM says as much ("the explicit rival merely records it"), and so does Mimo ("already fixed at C04"). Neither argues that the text's wording, without the index, can be read as asking a contract-free question, and no such reading is open: the question L57 says the semantics denies, a difference "available before the admitted changes are fixed, or independently of them", is not something "its signature" can name, since a signature exists only on a contract.
- **"the component's" for "its"** (Mimo only). The antecedent is fixed by (K) and by the sentence before: C11 names one component, and only components have signatures. The rival names what the pronoun already picks out.

So on this reading the sentence is easy to vary in its wording. That is recorded (rule 5).

**Is either rival clearer?** No. Why the text's wording and not Mimo's or GLM's:

- **The index would mark a difference that is not there.** Between L121 and L127 the text uses "signature" five more times with no index, under the same fixed \(C\): "has a signature" in each of the three family bullets (L123, L124, L125), "has a measurement's signature" (L127, fourth sentence) and "that signature" (L127, fifth sentence); L109, "has a measurement's signature (below)", points to them. An index on the third sentence of L127 alone would make it the one indexed use in the run, beside the unindexed "measurement's signature" in the very next sentence. A reader may then ask whether the neighbouring uses mean something else. The text's wording keeps the run uniform: one \(C\), fixed at L113, and every signature in the section on it.
- **Where the text writes the index, it marks something the context does not already carry.** L119 writes "on \(C\)" where it sets \(C\) against "a finer contract" and reads signatures "on \(C\) through \(\tau\) and \(\tau'\)"; L554 writes "a signature on \(\tau[C]\)" against its counterpart's "on \(C\)"; L626 writes "the signature of things on that contract", the extended one; L245, in Part V, far from L113, writes "whose signature on \(C\) differs from its counterpart's". GLM's point that the downstream uses carry the index is so (at L245, L281 and L554–L558), but those passages carry it for these reasons, and none of them applies inside the section that has just fixed \(C\). The downstream indexing therefore gives L127 no call for the index.
- **"Its" is not open in a way the rival closes.** Two misreadings are grammatically available. One takes "its" with the subject, "It" (the semantics' own signature); the other takes it with the scare-quoted "a cause". The first names nothing the text defines, since (K) gives signatures to components only, eleven lines above. The second takes a pronoun from a predicate the sentence is turning away. Neither reader fell into either; both read "its" as the component's. This is a reader's risk that the definition and the slot-for-slot parallel with C11 ("whether a component "is" a cause" / "what its signature is") already close. "The component's" adds explicitness and closes nothing that is open.
- **The contrast is kept in its barest terms.** C11 and C12 set an "is" question against a signature question. Both rivals keep that contrast, so this is no reason against them; it is only that neither adds to it, and each makes the second sentence longer than the contrast needs.

GLM's own argument that the explicit form matches the downstream uses is answered in the second point above. Mimo's argument that the rival "carries the index L119 makes necessary" is answered in the first: L119 makes the index necessary, and the defined term and L113 already carry it.
