**1. X13 · T2 — The quantifier change may cross from WORDING into CLAIM**

The old wording was `"If \(\pi\circ S_a=T_a\circ\pi\) for every generator \(a\),"` with \(S_a,T_a\) undefined. The new wording types them ("Let \(S_a\) be a target process and \(T_a\) a represented process") and narrows the quantifier from "every generator" to "every admitted generator." A reader of the old text could not conclude what \(S_a,T_a\) denote, nor whether the hypothesis covers generators outside \(A\). A reader of the new text can. By the WORDING test ("can a reader now conclude something the current text left unconcluded"), this may require CLAIM.

*Why the attack fails.* L91: "\(A\) is a set of admitted edits, closed under a partial associative composition with identity 1." The only edit structure in the theory is \(A\); a "generator" is therefore a generator of \(A\), by definition an admitted edit. Adding "admitted" is redundant, not restrictive. The "Let" sentence is a stipulation fixing symbol denotations, not a claim from which conclusions follow; the theorem's claim — generator commutation implies composition commutation — is identical. The proof at L353 (\(\pi S_bS_a=T_b\pi S_a=T_bT_a\pi\); induct) uses generators that compose into "admitted finite composition," matching the conclusion. The old text had a genuine erratum (undefined symbols); the new text repairs it without changing any claim. WORDING is correct. "Admitted generator" is a natural composition of terms used consistently across Parts II, III and VIII and creates no clash.

**2. X15 · T1 — "Interpreted as an explanatory candidate" may be ambiguous**

L377 (new): `"\(\mathcal E_c\) the criticism's connection from \(g\) to \(\delta\), interpreted as an explanatory candidate (Part V) for \(p_\delta\)."` Part V (L231) defines an explanatory candidate as a quadruple \((E,p,t,\Gamma)\). A "connection from \(g\) to \(\delta\)" is not of that form. Two reasonable decompositions of the same argument (e.g. one inferential step versus two) could yield different organizations, and (F1) is decomposition-sensitive: a finer decomposition has more components each checked against a smaller anchor. If one decomposition satisfies (E) and another does not, (K1) — Bearing \(\leftrightarrow\) Account(\(\mathcal E_c,p_\delta\)) — gives two verdicts for one criticism.

*Why the attack fails.* The phrase "interpreted as" marks a modelling step, consistent with the theory's practice (L13: "Declaration is permitted only as a modelling convenience"). The criticism's connection has a definite logical structure — the argument actually made — which the interpretation reads off: components are the inferential steps, anchors are the corresponding subnetworks of the target, commitments are the premises doing the work. Where the structure is clear the interpretation is canonical up to recoding, which Derivation 8 (L612) shows preserves (E). Where the structure is unclear or the argument is bare assertion, no interpretation satisfies (E) and Bearing is false, which is correct. Crucially, the gap is not introduced by this change: the old text used \(\mathcal E_c\) in (K1) with no definition whatsoever. The change reduces the gap.

**3. X15 · T3 — "In respect of \(p\)" may clash with Part III's technical "respect"**

Part III's heading (L149) is "The respect is the query," introducing "respect" as the technical term for what a question asks. X15's "in respect of \(p\)" could be forced into that reading, requiring \(p_\delta\) to share \(p\)'s query type — which is not stated and would change (K1)'s scope.

*Why the attack fails.* "In respect of \(p\)" is a standard English prepositional phrase meaning "concerning \(p\)" or "with regard to \(p\)" — a different grammatical construction from the noun "respect" that Part III defines. The context makes the meaning clear: the question is whether \(z\) has \(\delta\), situated in the context of the original question \(p\). No reader is forced into the technical reading.

The declaration: *"\(p_\delta\) is the question whether the target has the alleged defect in respect of \(p\), and \(\mathcal E_c\) is the criticism's connection from its grounds to the alleged defect, interpreted as an explanatory candidate for that question."* Against the new wording: "the target" = \(z\), "the alleged defect" = \(\delta\), "its grounds" = \(g\), "that question" = \(p_\delta\). The "(Part V)" reference is a cross-pointer, not a claim. The declaration adds nothing and omits nothing substantive.

The kind CLAIM is correct. Old: `"Let \(p_\delta\) be the question about the defect."` — vague; \(\mathcal E_c\) entirely undefined. New: both terms specified. The narrowing from "about the defect" (which could mean identification, production, or a yes/no question) to "whether \(z\) has \(\delta\)" (a specific yes/no question) is a genuine specification of claim, correctly captured by the declaration.

*Why the attack fails.* The declaration maps one-to-one onto the new wording.

**5. X13 · T1, T3, T4 — The theorem is true, coherent, and N20 is unmoved**

The proof at L353 is correct: given \(\pi\circ S_a=T_a\circ\pi\) for admitted generators \(a\), one obtains \(\pi S_bS_a = T_b\pi S_a = T_bT_a\pi\) and inducts over finite compositions. "Matching scopes" ensures partial compositions in \(A\) are well-defined. For N20 (keeping count with string), the first method's one-step commutation \(\pi\circ S_a = T_a\circ\pi\) fails at every step (measurement error prevents exact correspondence), so the hypothesis is not met and Functional transport gives nothing. The second method's rounding ensures exact one-step commutation, so the conclusion extends to any composition of joins and cuts. The change from "every generator" to "every admitted generator" is inert here — both methods' operations are basic counting operations. No verdict movement.
