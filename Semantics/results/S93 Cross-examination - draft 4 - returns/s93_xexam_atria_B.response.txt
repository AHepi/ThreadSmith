## Report

### 1. X10 · (c2) — the strongest attack: "both fit" leaves internal failures unresolved

**Attack.** X10's whole machinery turns on "both fit what is established for an assessor" (L317), and "fits" is defined in X09 (L315) as "no result established for that assessor shows it failing a condition of (E)," where a result is established for one who holds a usable receipt (Part IX, L397: "An evidence leaf is a reference to an event with an interpreted claim. A receipt is a derivation tree over leaves"). Nothing in Part IX or X10 says whether a failure found by *examining the candidate itself* — a failure of non-circular dependence (L255), a component anchored to nothing, an internal contradiction — yields such a receipt. So on one reading a self-contradictory or circular rival still "fits," and X10 then makes a good candidate easy to vary relative to junk.

**Why it fails to challenge X10.** The gap is upstream, in the notions X09 and Part IX supply, not in X10, which uses them correctly. And the bad consequence largely dissolves on the text's own terms. A self-contradictory candidate has an empty answer profile, while non-vacuity (L257) gives the target a nonempty one at the baseline; their answers therefore *differ* at every pair of C, so by L315 they conflict at pairs **in** C — a problem of the *first* kind, not the second, and one the machinery resolves by establishing the target's answer there. A merely circular candidate, if unexamined, fits only in the assessor-relative sense X10 builds in explicitly ("pose, **for that assessor**"); its failure is objective (Part I, L67: fidelity "is independent of whether anyone accepts it") whether or not anyone has the receipt. So X10's claims stay true on either reading of "established."

**What should nonetheless be done.** The text should settle it, because whether a rival "fits" gates whether a candidate is easy to vary, and an assessor who has not checked a rival for circularity gets a different verdict from one who has — with no textual statement of which. The settling clause belongs in X09 or Part IX, not in X10; X10 needs no change:

```text
A failure of (E) that is decidable from the candidate, the question and the
contract alone is established for an assessor who has examined the candidate,
without a test of the target; (K3) does not qualify it, because the failure is
in the candidate itself and rests on no background or instrument.
```

### 2. X10 · T1 — attack on "a test that solves the problem whatever it shows"

**Attack.** In kind (i) (L317), suppose the established target relations at the conflict pair are such that *neither* rival meets (F1), (F2) and (A). Then zero fit, and calling the problem "solved" looks wrong; and the sentence "an answer it refutes stays refuted on p (Part VIII)" adds a claim the declaration does not name.

**Why it fails.** "At most one of them fits" is satisfied by zero; the conflict is settled precisely because both are refuted, which is a resolution. And Part VIII (L369) already says a failed answer "stays failed… from the candidate's own answer there, with no record of which candidates failed before"; the citation makes the sentence an application of an unchanged result, not a new claim. The declaration's "solves the problem whatever it shows" entails persistence.

### 3. X10 · T1 — attack on "a test inside C can refute one of them without the other only for a failure of its own"

**Attack.** Take a pair in C where the rivals' answers agree and where some admitted target relations would let *both* meet (F1), (F2) and (A) — so by L315 they do not conflict there. If the *actual* relations favour only one of them, a test establishing them refutes the other, and that other can meet the conditions under some admitted relations, so its failure is not purely "its own."

**Why it fails.** Read in place, the sentence is about the test's *power to adjudicate between rivals*, not about the candidate's internal structure. Since the two do not conflict at that pair, the test cannot separate them as rivals; it can only pick off one that cannot meet the conditions under the actual relations — which is exactly its own failure to fit the world. Where both meet (E) on C, both meet the conditions under the actual relations at every pair of C, and no such test refutes either.

### 7. X10 · (a) — faithfulness to the owner's position and the drafters' rule

The rule forbids a listed set, an enumeration, a count, a grade, and a record doing the explanation's work. X10 states easy to vary only through discovered rivals and the problems they make: "Two rivals that both fit what is established for an assessor pose, for that assessor, a problem for p"; "A candidate is easy to vary… when it and a rival pose a problem of the second kind." It keeps the owner's "if two discovered variations fit, that is a problem," with discovery built into "rivals" at L315 ("among the candidates someone has offered"). It disclaims every forbidden device in its own words: "Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates"; "the term says nothing about which of them is right"; and X09's "No list of all rivals is supposed." No record or log is proposed: the problem "stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting," and refutation persists "from the candidate's own answer there, with no record of which candidates failed before" (L369) — the content does the work, which is the owner's "the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in" without a redundant record. The symmetry of "easy to vary" is what keeps it from being a grade: an asymmetric reading would rank the correct explanation above its rival, which the rule forbids.

**(a) X10: FAITHFUL**

### 8. X10 · (c1) — the tilt and the myth on the Greek question

Checked against the text: yes, this is what it says. On the Greek question the two agree at every pair of C and conflict only at admitted pairs outside it (southern lands), so by L317 the problem is of the second kind, each is easy to vary relative to the other, and each stays so until the question is widened; a finer contract containing a southern pair "makes a new question… on which… they pose a problem of the first kind while both fit," and once the southern seasons are established the myth no longer fits and the tilt ceases to be easy to vary. This is a harmless consequence, not a defect. Three things keep it so. The term is question-relative and assessor-relative by construction, and says nothing about correctness: the tilt remains an *account* of the Greek question throughout (Parts I and V), and being easy to vary is not a verdict against it. The myth's being easy to vary is exactly N3's verdict ("its details… could be swapped for others that fit the Greeks' facts just as well"). And the tilt's hardness appears precisely relative to the widened question — which is the historical point. To say instead that the tilt is hard to vary *on* the Greek question would require counting how many of its own variations still fit, which is the enumeration and the grade the owner's rule forbids; there is no wording that says differently without bringing one back.

**(c1) X10: HARMLESS**

### 9. X10 · (c2) — what counts as established

The text does not settle it. "Established" runs through Part IX's receipts, which are derivation trees over evidence leaves referencing *events with interpreted claims* (L397), and (K3) qualifies a result "only together with the background and instruments of the test that yields it" (L395). Nothing says whether examining a candidate is such an event, and nothing says it is not. Part VIII's pattern (L369) is world-directed — one establishes the *target's* answer, and the candidate's own answer then refutes it — which suggests "established" is for world-facts; but an examination is an occurrence in a history (L375) and so can serve as a leaf, which suggests the opposite. It should be settled, because "fits" gates the problems two rivals pose: on one reading an unexamined circular rival fits and makes a good candidate easy to vary for that assessor; on the other, the examination establishes the failure and the rival drops out. What turns on it is whether easy-to-vary tracks genuine underdetermination or also tracks unexamined junk. The clause proposed in point 1 settles it in the direction the theory's other commitments point (Part I makes (E) objective; (K3)'s caveat is empty for a failure resting on no background or instrument), and X10 itself needs no change.

**(c2) X10: UNSETTLED BY THE TEXT**
