# Report — Part B (X10)

## Points

**1. `X10 · T1` — the definition of a **test** in case (i).**
L317: "establishing it, the target's relations as well as its answer where their answers there agree, is a **test** that solves the problem whatever it shows, since afterwards at most one of them fits; an answer it refutes stays refuted on \(p\) (Part VIII)."
*Attack.* On the most available grammatical reading, "where their answers there agree" restricts "its answer", so that where the rivals' answers *differ* — the one case in which an answer decides — the test would establish only the target's relations. Then the defined test would not separate two candidates whose relations coincide and whose answers differ, the next clause ("an answer it refutes…") would be idle, and the declaration's flat "its relations as well as its answer" would not match the wording.
*Why it fails.* (a) The object of "establishing it" is carried from the preceding clause: "whether or not anyone can establish what it does; establishing it, …", where "it" is the target and "what it does" is its full doing at \((a,b)\); the appositive names that doing, and the qualifier marks the case in which the answer alone cannot separate the rivals. (b) The very next clause presupposes that a test can refute an answer. (c) The two halves of the doing are not independent: \(\operatorname{Ans}_p(a,b)=\mathcal Q(D,a,b)\) (L144) is computed from the target's component relations at \((a,b)\) and the boundary \(b\), so establishing "the target's relations" there fixes its answer there. A test as defined therefore solves the problem in both sub-cases of (i). The declaration's paraphrase is the reading the paragraph forces. The wording is compressed, not false.

**2. `X10 · T1` — "a test inside \(C\) can refute one of them without the other only for a failure of its own".**
*Attack.* Construct a second-kind pair separated by a test inside \(C\) for a reason that is not the refuted candidate's own. Take two candidates anchored to *different* subnetworks, with relations \(R_1,R_2\), and let the target's actual relation at the first anchor be \(R_1'\ne R_1\): a test refutes the first alone. *Why it fails.* The refutation is that candidate's own component relation failing (F1) against its own anchor; and the pair is not a conflict pair at all (some admissible relations let both meet, since distinct anchors are set independently), which is why the pair is second-kind. Try instead two active components with *one* anchor and different relations there: no admissible relations let both meet at that pair, so it is a conflict pair of \(C\) and the case is (i), not (ii). Try NonVacuity: the baseline and the scope conditions belong to \(p\) and bear on both rivals alike. No counterexample stands.

**3. `X10 · T3` / `(c2)` — can a second-kind problem ever close?**
*Attack.* The paragraph offers tests as the closing device ("a test that solves the problem"; "a test inside \(C\) can refute…"), and the (K3) gloss at L315 speaks of "the background and instruments of **the test that yields it**". If only tests could yield results, nothing could show a failure of non-circular dependence or of non-vacuity; and since in case (ii) "a test inside \(C\) can refute one of them without the other only for a failure of its own" while results outside \(C\) cannot show a failure of (E) on \(C\), the sentence "the problem for \(p\) stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting" would be unsatisfiable, and a defective rival would keep a clean candidate marked "easy to vary" for good.
*Why it fails.* The fit clause is deliberately wider than the test clause: L315 says "no result established for that assessor shows it failing **a condition of (E)**", and the same paragraph distinguishes "(F1), (F2) and (A) … and the rest of (E) on \(C\)"; if only the three testable conditions were meant, the text would have named them. Receipts are proposition-general: L397, "For \(\phi\), \(P_j(\phi)\) and \(N_j(\phi)\) are the usable receipts for and against." Part VIII already makes the candidate's own content a premise of a receipt: L369, "this is established for that assessor of every such candidate alike, **from the candidate's own answer there**, with no record of which candidates failed before or of how any was changed." Part IX (K1) makes any criticism — including an internal one — an account of a defect-question. So a derivation whose leaves are the candidate's own content yields an established result, the candidate stops fitting, and the problem closes without a test. The "test that yields it" gloss is a caveat about how far a refutation reaches, not a restriction on what can be established.

**4. `X10 · T2` — the declaration.**
*Attack.* The declaration reads "establishing what the target does there, its relations as well as its answer", while the wording carries "where their answers there agree"; and the declaration does not mention "an answer it refutes stays refuted on \(p\)" or "while both fit". *Why it fails.* The first difference is the compression settled in point 1: the declaration gives the reading the paragraph forces ("establishing it", it = what the target does there). "An answer it refutes stays refuted on \(p\) (Part VIII)" only restates unchanged content the item does not touch; "while both fit" is already built into the opening definition of a problem ("Two rivals that both fit what is established … pose … a problem"). Nothing is claimed that the declaration does not cover; nothing the declaration says is missing from the wording. Kind CLAIM is right.

**5. `X10 · T3` — the dropped assessor index.**
*Attack.* The opening sentence indexes problems ("pose, **for that assessor**, a problem"), but "A candidate is **easy to vary** … when it and a rival pose a problem of the second kind" states the predicate flat, so a reader may take it as an objective mark on the candidate. *Why it fails.* The index is inherited from the definition the sentence cites; "fits" (L315) is defined only for an assessor and is the sole route into a problem; and the criticism clause routes the predicate back through Part IX's receipts, which are held by an assessor. "For that assessor" would be smoother; without it the text is not incoherent.

## Questions

**(a) X10.** The wording is faithful. It states the notion only through a discovered rival and the problem they make — "A candidate is **easy to vary**, in the sense used here, when it and a rival pose a problem of the second kind" — never through a set, an enumeration or a count; "Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates"; "the term says nothing about which of them is right"; the predicate attaches symmetrically ("the rival is then easy to vary too"). No record or log does any work: exclusion is carried by the target's established answer read against the candidate's own ("an answer it refutes stays refuted on \(p\) (Part VIII)", which itself says "with no record of which candidates failed before or of how any was changed"). The closing clause "the problem for \(p\) stands until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting" keeps error correction in the content, as the owner required.
(a) X10: FAITHFUL

**(c1) X10.** The text does say it: with the stipulated fit and outside-\(C\) conflict the pair poses a second-kind problem, so each is easy to vary relative to the other. One correction to the paraphrase: the tilt does not wait for the question to be widened to lose the label — the label ends when the problem ends, either because a finer contract turns it into a problem of the first kind, or because the rival stops fitting (for the myth of Demeter, its non-circular failure does that as soon as a receipt for it exists; point 3). The consequence is harmless: the label is a statement about the dispute ("the contract does not contain their conflict"), not about the tilt's own variability, and it says nothing about which is right; the asymmetry the fixed verdicts live on is carried by (E) — the myth fails non-circular dependence (N3), the tilt does not (N1) — and by the first-kind problem a wider question creates (N2, N4). The Deutschian asymmetry appears on the other side: the myth has a fitting rival that is a variation of itself (N2's amendment), while any variant tilt would conflict at a pair of the contract and fail established results. Words that marked the tilt "hard to vary" and the myth "easy to vary" at the restricted question would be exactly the grade the owner's rule forbids ("never as a grade"), and words tracking swappable details would need the set of versions the same rule forbids.
(c1) X10: HARMLESS

**(c2) X10.** Yes: a failure found by examining the candidate counts, once the assessor holds a usable receipt for it, and the candidate then no longer fits. The fit clause says "a condition of (E)" and (E) includes non-circular dependence and non-vacuity, which no test can show; receipts are derivations over leaves referencing events, and Part VIII already derives an exclusion "from the candidate's own answer there"; so a leaf may be the candidate's own content, and "this component restates the answer" or "this component's anchor is empty" is an established result. It should count: otherwise no second-kind problem could ever close and the paragraph's "until what is established leaves at most one of them fitting" would be idle. What turns on it: if it did not count, the myth of N3 could "fit" for ever and keep the tilt marked easy to vary; with it, the problem closes by analysis alone, and "A criticism that a candidate is easy to vary must supply such a rival" can only be met by a rival nothing established defeats. The one phrase inviting the wrong reading is the (K3) gloss; if the drafters want the room gone, insert after "…shows it failing a condition of (E)" (L315): "A result may show this from the candidate's own content, by analysis; it need not come from a test."
(c2) X10: ESTABLISHED
