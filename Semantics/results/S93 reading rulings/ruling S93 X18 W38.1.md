## Rulings

### 1. The *Reach* pointer, "(Parts I and VI)" (Atria 1, Mimo 3): KEEP

Both replies ask for the same change; that decides nothing (rule 5). On the texts:

- The note's clause is L317's clause word for word, and L317 stands in Part VI (L285–L319). Part VI therefore states exactly what the note reports. Part I is the text's own ground for it: L317 cites Part I, and Part I's explanatory realism (L67) makes faithfulness "independent of whether anyone accepts it". Both Parts the note names say or ground what the note says, so the defect T3 names, a pointer to a Part "that does not say what the item says it says", is absent.
- The two parentheses do different jobs, and they do not clash. The note's pointers name the Parts where the semantics says what the note reports, as with "(Part VI)" after hard-to-vary stated through rivals, "(Parts 0 and IV)" after blind selection and "(Parts 0, VI and XIV)" after idle parts. L317's parenthesis names the grounds of a sentence standing in Part VI, which could not usefully cite its own Part. Neither contradicts the other, and a reader who follows the note's pointer reaches L317, and there the text's own "(Parts I and V)".
- The proposed "(Parts I and V)" would take away the only Part in which the clause is stated. Part V (L231–L265) defines Account and says nothing about when a question is first asked or whether anyone has checked. Mimo's reason assumes that the pointer is meant to lead to the definition of Account. The note does not say so, and the Part it names contains the sentence it reports.
- History, which is not the ground of this ruling: in draft 3 the line ended "(Part VI)", where the text then defined reach. Draft 4 kept Part VI, where the sentence now stands, and added Part I.

If the orchestrator wants the note to carry the text's grounds as well, "(Parts I, V and VI)" would be true. It is not required and is not ruled here.

### 2. The *Hard to vary* application (Atria 2): KEEP

- Atria is right that the draft-4 text never applies L317 to the tilt or the myth. T2, however, asks whether a line states a claim that the revised text does not make. The application is L317's definition applied to the case the note names: two rivals offered in place of each other, both fitting what is established on the Greeks' question and conflicting only at pairs outside it, pose a problem of the second kind, and "A candidate is easy to vary, in the sense used here, when it and a rival pose a problem of the second kind; the rival is then easy to vary too" (L317). Atria's own (c1) paragraph derives this from L317 and says "The text says exactly this", and Mimo's agrees. A consequence that the text's definitions give on a named case is what a careful reader would say the theory concludes (brief §5, T4). Stating it adds no claim to the theory, and adding a claim is what the declaration rules out ("add nothing to it").
- "This note says nothing about what follows from them" (l.491) can be read with "them" as the books or as the definitions. On the first reading Atria's objection has nothing to meet. On the second, the line does say what a definition gives, but only what the text itself gives, and the departure cannot be recorded without it. The fourth departure is that the text's sense makes easy to vary what Deutsch calls hard to vary. Checked by page marker: on p.24 he calls the axis-tilt explanation of the seasons hard to vary, and on p.28, at the end of the passage beginning on p.27, the modified theory is for him no longer an explanation but a rule of thumb. Without the application, "symmetric" would stand without the departure it records.
- Atria's wording does not meet Atria's own objection. With "would be", the sentence still says what the definition gives on the Greeks' question, only in the conditional, and "when the two are offered in place of each other" already makes it conditional. It changes no claim, so it repairs nothing.
- One condition the texts carry, which neither reply raised: a problem is posed "for that assessor" whose established results both rivals fit (L317). So the tilt is easy to vary relative to the myth for an assessor only while the myth fits what that assessor has established. The note carries this condition through the definition it is applying in the same bullet ("a rival that fits as well"). The line is compressed, not false, and this is no ground for a fix.

### 3. The recognized-difficulty sentence (Mimo 1 and 2): FIX

- The note gives, after a colon and a pointer to Part X, a definition that is not Part X's. Both quotations are found (l.502, L429). The note drops "what the system holds" and "only by".
- The words at fault can be read two ways, and on both the note departs from L429. First, "meeting a claimed obligation fails a protected one" may be said of one way of meeting the obligation. Then the note counts Mimo's case (a system holding both a rushed route and a legal route), which L429 does not count, and Mimo's "strictly broader" holds. Second, "a conflict" may mean that the obligation cannot be met at all without failing the protected one. Then the note's condition is about obligations in the abstract, not about what the system holds, as Mimo's (ii) says. It misses a case L429 counts: a system that holds only a route that fails the protected obligation, where a route it does not hold would not. On either reading the note points to Part X for a definition Part X does not give (T3), and it states a claim that the text does not make (T2).
- L317 uses the same "only by" in its last sentence ("meets a claimed obligation only by dropping the other"). Restoring L429's words also makes the note agree with Part VI.
- The smallest change that meets the challenge puts L429's clause in place of the note's clause and changes nothing else. No book words are touched. Nothing in it brings back a list, an enumeration, a count of versions, a grade or a record (S20, S26). KIND stays META, and the DECLARATION and the entry's OLD are unchanged.

## For the owner

**(c1): whether "easy to vary" should point at one side (the tilt and the myth on the Greeks' question).** Both replies say HARMLESS, and neither proposes words. This ruling does not settle the choice.

**Does the present wording state the matter truly?** Yes. L317 defines easy to vary through a problem of the second kind. It says outright that the property is symmetric ("the rival is then easy to vary too, and the term says nothing about which of them is right") and that it is not a grade ("Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates."). On the Greeks' question, the tilt and the myth are offered in place of each other, both fit what an assessor has established, and they conflict only outside \(C\). The text therefore makes each easy to vary relative to the other. That lasts until a finer contract containing a pair where they conflict makes a new question, on which they pose a problem of the first kind while both fit (L317). The note says the same and records it as a departure from Deutsch, who calls the tilt hard to vary (p.24). The one condition that the note leaves to the definition it applies is that the myth fit what the assessor has established.

**Atria's reasons:** L317 defines the term symmetrically, and on the Greeks' question each is easy to vary relative to the other. This is a declared departure from Deutsch's grading use, and it coheres with the theory's abstention from merit functions (L25). "The theory should not say differently."

**Mimo's reasons:** The narrow contract does not contain the difference between the tilt and the myth, so a wider question is needed, and the text says so ("A finer contract… makes a new question"). "In the sense used here", the symmetry clause and "Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates" keep the property from working as a grade. The result is a harmless consequence of the theory's question-relativity, not a defect.
