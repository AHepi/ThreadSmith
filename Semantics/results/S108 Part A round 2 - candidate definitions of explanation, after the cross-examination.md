## 4. For the owner: the flagged candidates in plain words

Each: one sentence, an everyday example, and the past decision it seems to go against, in the owner's words.

**C1.** *In one sentence:* each part of an explanation has to match what its piece of the thing does inside the whole, not the rule that piece follows on its own. *Example:* a bicycle's gear part may only say "the wheel turns at the speed it turns now with this rider", not "the gears multiply whatever the pedals do". On your shop sign, the red-part and blue-part explanation would no longer be an explanation, however the change of day is read. *Seems to go against* S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C2.** *In one sentence:* only a question that was itself found or worked out can be answered; a question simply laid down has no answer. *Example:* a customer just asks "why is the sign red on Mondays?", with no story of how the question arose; on the reading that such a question counts as laid down, nothing explains the sign or the weathervane. Your engineer's bridge is touched only on further readings: that the work included criticizing a first design, and that "creating" is taken in the program's narrow sense; then nothing counts as created there. Without a criticized first design, nothing counted as created there before either, so nothing changes. Where the question's story is recorded as found or worked out, nothing changes. [Xd1] *Seems to go against*, on that reading, S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."; S41 Q15: "Yes, it can be explained"; S41 Q6: "Yes, it can".

**C5.** *In one sentence:* a difference counts towards an explanation only when it comes with a change made to the thing, not from its circumstances. *Example:* "because someone flipped the switch" can explain a lamp being on; "because it is night" cannot. On the reading that the north wind and the day of the week are circumstances of the vane and the sign, nothing explains why the vane points north, and your two-part sign explanation is not one; on the reading that they are changes made to them, nothing changes. *Seems to go against*, on that reading, S41 Q15: "Yes, it can be explained", and S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C6.** *In one sentence:* something whose answer is simply written into one of its parts is not an explanation at all. *Example:* a table that just lists the answers stops being an explanation. Your red-part and blue-part sign stays one on the reading that a part is "written in" only if it alone gives the answer everywhere asked; on the readings where giving it somewhere is enough, it stops being one. *Seems to go against* S45: "Yes, take the test out"; and, on those readings, S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C7.** *In one sentence:* a link counts as found by trial even when nothing at all was tried, as long as nothing earlier already stood for the answer. *Example:* someone who has never seen a pendulum writes a formula on a napkin and says "this stands for the pendulum"; it counts as an explanation. A student copying it from a textbook still does not. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2: "No, not if just declared".

**C8.** *In one sentence:* a worked-out link counts as worked out only if the explanation it leaned on while being worked out itself passes the tests. *Example:* a navigator works out a course with a method that fails on the open sea; her course still comes out right for the harbour, but on this reading it counts as merely declared. (Only on the reading that "worked out" asks about the explanation the working used.) On the same reading, an explanation of your weathervane worked out with a claim about another part of the vane that does not pass the tests stops being an explanation. *Seems to go against*, on that reading, S41 Q2: "No, not if just declared" (her link was worked out), and S41 Q15: "Yes, it can be explained".

**C11.** *In one sentence:* something whose answer is simply written into one part can pass the tests but is not an explanation. *Example:* "it is red because it is red" passes but does not count. Your two-part sign stays an explanation on the reading that a part is "written in" only if it alone gives the answer everywhere asked, and stops being one on the other readings. *Seems to go against* S45: "Yes, take the test out"; and, on those readings, S44: "In either case, it is an explanation."

**C12.** *In one sentence:* anything that passes the tests is an explanation, even if its link to the thing was only declared. *Example:* the student who copies a working pendulum formula and writes "this stands for the pendulum" has an explanation. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2: "No, not if just declared".

**C15.** *In one sentence:* a question counts only if what it describes could be built in just one way; if several different set-ups would behave as described, nothing answers it. *Example:* "why is this lamp on at night and off at noon?" asked of any lamp that behaves so, when many different circuits do: on this reading no explanation of the lamp counts. On the reading where your sign is described only by what it shows (red on Mondays, blue on Tuesdays), several different signs would do that, so neither your two-part explanation nor any other explains it; the same for the weathervane. *Seems to go against*, on that reading, S44: "In either case, it is an explanation.", and S41 Q15: "Yes, it can be explained".

**C16.** *In one sentence:* a link found by trying things counts as found only if the trying says what the link was built from; otherwise it counts as simply declared. *Example:* a gardener tries many watering schedules and keeps the one that works, but never writes down what it is made of; on this reading her schedule is merely declared, and explains nothing. An explanation of your sign or your weathervane reached by trying and keeping what worked, with nothing stated about how it was built, would stop being an explanation. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2: "No, not if just declared" (the question you answered set a link "simply declared" against one "found by trial or worked out") [Xd2]; and, for such a history, S44: "In either case, it is an explanation." and S41 Q15: "Yes, it can be explained".

**C21.** *In one sentence:* a link counts as found by trial even when the answer was already written down in front of the person who picked it. *Example:* a student copies the pendulum formula from a textbook, checks it on one swing, and keeps it: on this reading that is an explanation. *Seems to go against* S41 Q2: "No, not if just declared".
