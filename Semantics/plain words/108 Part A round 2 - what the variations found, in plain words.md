## 3. What moved the definition of explanation

A candidate counts as an explanation when it meets the five requirements and its link to the thing was not simply declared.

An example first. One variation said a link counts as simply declared unless the story of how it was found says what it was built from. Under it, every link found by trying things, with no such note, stopped counting. That dropped between about 230 and 1,700 of the made-up candidates in each batch, and your sign and weathervane on such a story.

That pattern held across the round. The requirements themselves moved only where a variation rewrote what they look at. Whether a candidate counts as an explanation moved far more, and almost always through a reading of "simply declared": how far back to look, what counts as worked out, what to do when the story says nothing. ## 5. The alternative definitions you will be asked about

21 alternative definitions in all; 11 seem to go against something you decided earlier. None is applied. Each one below: a sentence, an everyday example, and the decision it may clash with. "On one reading" means your own case turns on how a word is read, and that reading is yours to choose.

1. Each part of an explanation must match what its piece does inside the whole thing, not the rule the piece follows alone. *Example:* a gear part may only say "the wheel turns at this speed with this rider". Your two-part sign stops counting. *May clash with* your "In either case, it is an explanation."
2. A question simply laid down, not found or worked out, has no answer. *Example:* a customer's bare "why is the sign red on Mondays?". *May clash, on one reading, with* your answers on the sign, the weathervane and the bridge.
3. A difference counts only when it comes with a change made to the thing. *Example:* "someone flipped the switch" explains a lamp being on; "it is night" does not. *May clash, on one reading, with* your "Yes, it can be explained" (the weathervane) and your answer on the sign.
4. Something whose answer is written into one of its parts is not an explanation. *Example:* a table that just lists the answers. *May clash with* your "Yes, take the test out"; and, on one reading, with your answer on the sign.
5. A link counts as found by trying even when nothing was tried, if nothing earlier already stood for the answer. *Example:* someone who has never seen a pendulum writes a formula on a napkin. *May clash, in part, with* your "No, not if just declared".
6. A worked-out link counts only if what it leaned on while being worked out passes the requirements. *Example:* a navigator works out a course with a method that fails at sea. *May clash, on one reading, with* your "No, not if just declared" and your answer on the weathervane.
7. Something with its answer written into one part can meet the requirements and still not be an explanation. *Example:* "it is red because it is red". *May clash with* your "Yes, take the test out"; and, on one reading, with your answer on the sign.
8. Anything that meets the requirements is an explanation, even with a declared link. *Example:* a student copies the pendulum formula from a textbook. *May clash with* your "No, not if just declared".
9. A question counts only if what it describes could be built in just one way. *Example:* "why is this lamp on at night?", when many different circuits behave so. *May clash, on one reading, with* your answers on the sign and the weathervane.
10. A link found by trying counts as simply declared unless the trying says what it was built from. *Example:* a gardener keeps the watering schedule that works but never writes down what it is. *May clash with* your "No, not if just declared"; and, for such a story, with your answers on the sign and the weathervane.
11. A link counts as found by trying even when the answer was already written in front of the person choosing. *Example:* a student copies the formula, checks it on one swing, and keeps it. *May clash, on one reading, with* your "No, not if just declared".
