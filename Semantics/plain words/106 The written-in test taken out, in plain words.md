## 1. Your shop sign, and what the theory now says about it

A shop sign is red on Mondays and blue on Tuesdays. Someone explains it: "there's a red part that switches on on Mondays, and a blue part that switches on on Tuesdays." You said this is "an explanation. Just not a good one", and of the theory's test that such an explanation is no explanation at all: "Yes, take the test out".

That test, the written-in test, is now out of the maths, the program and a new copy of the theory, the copy without the written-in test. The theory now says your sign explanation is an explanation. It gives no grade.

The program checked the sign in two forms:
- **With two parts, as you worded it.** It already counted under the program's usual reading, though not under other readings never chosen. Now it counts under every reading.
- **With one part that shows red on Mondays and blue on Tuesdays.** It did not count; now it does.

What this means for the idea: whether something is an explanation and how good it is are now separate questions. A written-in answer bears only on the second.

## 2. What stayed, and why

One part of the old requirement stayed: the answer must depend on the explanation's parts at all. Take away the parts of your sign explanation and nothing makes the colour change with the day, so it passes. This is not the written-in test: when nothing in the question changes, it fails a mechanism and a lookup alike.

Three other results stayed, because they rest on other requirements:
- **A bare table of the answers seen still fails:** for changes it has not seen, it gives the wrong answer.
- **Working backwards from a result to its cause still fails** on a question about how the result is produced.
- **An argument whose premise is its own conclusion still rules nothing out.** The theory also calls this "p because p".

The maths also names a part that on its own hands over the answer for one case, like your red part on a Monday: a pin. Criticism can point at a pin; it gives no grade and no count.

## 3. "p because p"

Here "p because p" means an explanation that just restates its answer: "the sign is red on Mondays because it is red on Mondays." The theory said this was no explanation; now it counts, as an empty one, unless its link was only declared, as you said in round 2: "No, not if just declared". Like every explanation, it fails when nothing in the question changes.

Of the program's 28 cases, ten moved from not counting to counting, among them the one-part sign, a weathervane that points north because someone turns it by hand, three lookups dressed up as mechanisms, and "p because p". Five more moved only under readings that were never chosen, your two-part sign among them. Thirteen did not move.
