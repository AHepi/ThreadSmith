## 2. The words used here

In your statement, "a problem" is what the candidates try to solve. Files 92 and 93 call that a **question**, and keep **problem** for something narrower, below; so here your word appears only inside quotation marks.

- A **candidate** is anything someone puts forward as an explanation for a question, true or false. The theory's word for putting forward is "offered".
- A **setting** is one change the question allows, made in given circumstances, such as a dry spring with the early variety planted. A question's **range** is the settings it covers.
- A candidate is an **account** of a question when it meets all four **requirements** of file 92 (section 4.3): matching the world, answering the question asked, not assuming the answer, and not dodging the question. "Account" is the theory's word for a real explanation of that question. Whether a candidate is one depends on the candidate, the question and the world, not on what anyone has found out.
- A **judge** is whoever is judging a candidate: a person, a system, the project.
- Something found out is **established** for a judge who holds evidence for it that they can still use. A candidate **fits**, for a judge, when nothing established for that judge shows it failing a requirement. "Fits" is the theory's nearest word to your "survive"; how near is the first choice (section 6).
- Two candidates **conflict** at a setting when they cannot both be right there (section 7). They are **rivals** when one is put forward in place of the other for the same question and they conflict somewhere. Two rivals that both fit pose a **problem**; what you call "the conflict" is taken here to be this problem.
- A candidate is **easy to vary**, on a question, when it has a rival that fits as well and conflicts with it only at settings outside the question's range. The rival is then easy to vary too.
- A **test** is finding out what actually happens at a setting where two rivals conflict.
- For the person who chooses I use your words, shortened: **the person choosing**. The theory has no word for such a person; the nearest is the judge.

2. **While both fit, nothing is solved.** "Both may survive, which means no resolution has been reached." In the theory a problem stands until what is established leaves at most one of the two fitting.
3. **A question can itself be faulty.** "The problem may, for whatever reason, be ill posed." The theory allows a question that asks for things that cannot all hold; showing that it is faulty is a different question.
4. **Stopping is a decision.** In your examples, the crop that needs harvesting, and the parents' “hurry up and clean your room”. The theory says that closing a stretch of inquiry is a decision, not a proof; nothing requires an inquiry to be finished, and a problem left alone still stands.

## 5. Does it go against anything in the theory?

**No, taken as your own words support.** Two places need care.

**Does choosing solve the problem?** In the theory, no: only finding something out solves it, such as a test in a dry spring with the early variety planted. Your "Resolution is up to the person and the person's choice." is taken here to mean that whether and how the person goes on, or stops, is theirs; your "yes, if resolution is the goal" fits that sense, and taken so, the theory agrees.

**The theory's one sentence about choosing between rivals.** Suppose a person has set herself a rule that must go on holding while she works, which the theory calls a **safeguard**: keep every rival that fits. If what she is trying to do can be done only by dropping one of two rivals that both fit, the theory calls her situation a **noticed difficulty**: a clash, which she is aware of, between what she is trying to do and a safeguard she has set herself. A **repair**, in the theory, is a change that does what she set out to do without breaking a safeguard. If she drops the rival anyway, all that follows is that her change is no repair by her own rules. The theory never calls a choice wrong, so "They're all valid choices." is untouched; that, like "Choice is always important", lies outside what the theory claims.

## 7. What "conflict" means, and the two other choices

You wrote: "I don't really understand the rest of the conflicts." File 93 calls its open points "choices"; in the theory "conflict" has one defined meaning, given first, and the two other choices follow.

### What "conflict" means

**The example.** In a dry spring with the early variety planted, the son's rescue says the north bed ripens first and the sister's says it does not. **Two candidates conflict at a setting when they cannot both be right there**: either their answers differ, as here, or they give the same answer but differ about how it comes about, as when two explanations of a lamp both say "lit" while one says a certain wire carries the current and the other says it does not. "A wet spring" and "a rainy spring" conflict nowhere.

**What hangs on it.** Whether two candidates are rivals at all, and what can solve their problem. If they conflict at a setting the question covers, a test there solves it whatever it shows. If they conflict only outside the range (say her question covers only the summers she has seen), nothing inside the question can solve it, unless one of them is shown failing for a reason of its own. A finer question, whose range also covers the dry spring, is a different question; a test there decides that question, not the first one.

### The second choice: is "offered" what you mean by "discovered"?

**The example.** The son and the sister each put their rescue forward separately, neither to replace the other's. The theory calls two candidates rivals when one "has been offered … in place of the other" and they conflict. Read narrowly, the sister would have had to put hers forward instead of the son's in particular, so they are not rivals. Read broadly, both were put forward for the same question, so they are rivals, and since both fit, they pose a problem. And that night the gardener thinks "or perhaps it was the warm wind", and tells no one. As the theory is worded, that rescue is nobody's rival. Your word was "discovered": "If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem."

**What hangs on it.** Whether such rescues pose a problem, and whether a variation only thought of can. No verdict on the project's cases changes either way, and your statement says nothing either way.

**The question, for later.** (a) Do two candidates put forward separately for the same question count as rivals, if they cannot both be right? (b) Does a variation the person choosing has thought of count as a rival, even if she has told no one?

### The third choice: should "easy to vary" point at one side?

**The example.** The tilt and the myth say the same for every season the Greeks knew; they come apart only far south of the equator, outside the Greeks' question. If the myth still fits for a judge (the first choice answered "no"), nothing on the Greeks' question can tell the two apart, and the theory calls each easy to vary against the other: the tilt too. The label says nothing about which is right; the tilt can be an account throughout, and the myth is not one.

**A correction to file 93.** File 93 called a finer question, covering the southern seasons, "the place where a test decides". But a finer question is a different question, and a test there decides that question only. On the Greeks' own question the label remains until the myth is shown failing on that question itself, for instance by the argument that it assumes its answer: the first choice again.

**What hangs on it.** Only the label, not which candidate is right. Pointing it at the myth alone would need a way to mark one of two fitting candidates as worse. Both outside readers argued that this would need a count of each one's versions, which your decision of 24 September set aside; and the theory grades nothing. Your statement does not mention the label, so this is best asked after the first choice; a "yes" there removes the example for any judge who holds the argument against the myth.
