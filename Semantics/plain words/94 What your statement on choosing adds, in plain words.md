## 2. The words used here

In your statement, "a problem" is what the candidates try to solve. Files 92 and 93 call that a **question**, and keep **problem** for something narrower, below; so your word appears only inside quotation marks.

- A **candidate** is an explanation someone puts forward as an answer to a question.
- A **setting** is one particular way things could be, such as a dry spring with the early variety planted. A question's **range** is the settings it covers.
- A candidate is an **account** of a question when it meets all four **requirements**: the theory's word for a real explanation of that question. Whether it is one depends on the candidate, the question and the world, not on what anyone has found out.
- A result is **established** for someone when they hold evidence for it that they can still use. A candidate **fits**, for someone, when nothing established for them shows it failing a requirement. "Fits" is the theory's nearest word to your "survive"; how near is the first choice (section 6).
- Two candidates **conflict** at a setting when they cannot both be right there (section 7). They are **rivals** when one is put forward in place of the other for the same question and they conflict somewhere. Two rivals that both fit pose a **problem**; your "the conflict" is this problem.
- A **test** is finding out what actually happens at a setting where two rivals conflict.
- For the person who chooses I use your words, shortened: **the person choosing**.

## 7. What "conflict" means, and the two other choices

You wrote: "I don't really understand the rest of the conflicts." File 93 calls its open points "choices"; in the theory "conflict" has one meaning, given first, and the two other choices follow.

### What "conflict" means

**The example.** In a dry spring with the early variety planted, the son's rescue says the north bed ripens first and the sister's says it does not. **Two candidates conflict at a setting when they cannot both be right there**: either their answers differ, as here, or they give the same answer but differ about how it comes about, as when two explanations of a lamp both say "lit" while one says a certain wire carries the current and the other says it does not. "A wet spring" and "a rainy spring" conflict nowhere.

**What hangs on it.** Whether two candidates are rivals at all, and what can solve their problem. If they conflict at a setting the question covers, a test there solves it whatever it shows. If they conflict only outside the range (say her question covers only the summers she has seen), nothing inside the question can solve it, unless one of them is shown failing for a reason of its own. A wider question that covers the dry spring is a new question; a test there decides the new question, not the old one.

### The second choice: is "offered" what you mean by "discovered"?

**The example.** The son and the sister each put their rescue forward separately, neither to replace the other's. The theory calls two candidates rivals when one "has been offered … in place of the other" and they conflict. Read narrowly, the sister would have had to put hers forward instead of the son's in particular, so they are not rivals. Read broadly, both were put forward for the same question, so they are rivals, and since both fit, they pose a problem. And that night the gardener thinks "or perhaps it was the warm wind", and tells no one. As the theory is worded, that rescue is nobody's rival. Your word was "discovered": "If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem."

**What hangs on it.** Whether such rescues pose a problem, and whether a variation only thought of can. No verdict on the project's cases changes either way, and your statement says nothing either way.

**The question.** (a) Do two candidates put forward separately for the same question count as rivals, if they cannot both be right? (b) Does a variation the person choosing has thought of count, even if she has told no one?

### The third choice: should "easy to vary" point at one side?

**The example.** The tilt and the myth say the same for every season the Greeks knew; they come apart only far south of the equator, outside the Greeks' question. If the myth still fits for someone (the first choice answered "no"), nothing on the Greeks' question can tell the two apart, and the theory calls each easy to vary against the other: the tilt too. The label says nothing about which is right; the tilt can be an account throughout, and the myth is not one.

**A correction to file 93.** File 93 called a finer question "the place where a test decides". A wider question covering the southern seasons is a new question, and a test there decides that question. On the Greeks' own question the label remains until the myth is shown failing on that question itself, for instance by the argument that it assumes its answer: the first choice again.

**What hangs on it.** Only the label, not which candidate is right. Pointing it at the myth alone would need a way to mark one of two fitting candidates as worse; the only ways anyone has found are counting versions, which your decision of 24 September set aside, and grading, which the theory does not do. Your statement does not mention the label, so this is best asked after the first choice; a "yes" there removes the example for anyone who holds the argument against the myth.
