## 1. What happened, and what it means for the idea

The two outside readers, Atria and Mimo, cross-examined the passages of draft 4 that no outside reader had yet seen, among them the paragraphs that write your decision on hard to vary into the theory, and tried to break them.

Your decision came through. Both readers judged every passage they were asked about faithful to what you said on 24 and 25 September. Neither found wording that brings back a list of rivals, a count of versions, a grade or a record. "A failed answer stays failed" was kept exactly as written.

What they found were slips at the edges: an example sentence that claimed too much, a restatement that dropped two words, a wrong letter, a proof that skipped a step. Seven small changes to the theory, and one to the sources note, make **draft 5**, dated 25 September. None changes the theory's verdict on any case.

Three choices are left for you (section 6). They are about where the theory draws fine lines, not about whether your position holds.

## 2. What was done

Take one passage first. The new paragraph on rivals went to each reader in a packet with the stretches of the theory it rests on, eleven cases it might affect, your words of 24 and 25 September, and what to ask: is it true, is it faithful to your decision, does it move any case, is anything lost? Each reader ended with one line: the passage stands, or it is disputed, with exact new words.

Eighteen passages were put to the readers this way (the project's papers call them items). They were the new paragraphs on rivals and problems and "A failed answer stays failed"; the sources note's new lines and three changes made with them; ten wordings corrected after an earlier cross-examination, which the readers had not seen corrected; and one proposed correction, the wrong letter in the pole sentence. They went out in eleven packets, each to both readers: 22 replies. The packets left out the drafters' reasons and expectations, so the readers judged from the text alone.

**The rule came first.** Before anything was sent, the project wrote down how the replies would be handled. No reply is opened until all are in. A reply is evidence, not a verdict, and silence is not support. Every passage a reader disputes, or on which the readers disagree, goes to its own **checker**: a fresh Claude assistant that did not draft the wording, did not write the packets, and had not read the replies. The checker sets out both readers' arguments, then decides with reasons whether the wording is kept, fixed or dropped. How many readers are on a side decides nothing. Where a point is a choice for you, the checker says only whether the present wording states it truly, and sets out both readers' reasons for you.

**All 22 replies came back**; a few needed a second try because the connection failed. Ten passages went to checkers: seven were fixed, three kept, none dropped. Eight were disputed by neither reader.

The fixes then made draft 5, and a separate assistant checked it (section 7).

## 3. Your decision on hard to vary

**Faithful, with nothing brought back.** Whenever asked whether a passage is faithful to your decision, both readers said it is. Each checker confirmed that its own fix brings back no list, count, grade or record. A search of draft 5 found none: every hit was a sentence denying such a thing, or an ordinary use of a word like "count".

**A failed answer stays failed.** Take Bruno's flood rule (file 92, section 8.3). On a day in March it gave an answer the stream did not give. Draft 4 says that any candidate giving that same answer for that day is no account of when the stream floods, whether it is Bruno's rule, the rule offered again, a rival, or a changed rule. Once someone has established what the stream did that day, that is established of every such candidate alike, from its own answer for that day. No record of who failed before is needed.

Both readers judged that this holds as stated. The checker kept it word for word, after going through every way a corrected mistake might come back. If the evidence for what the stream did lapses (a faulty flood gauge, say), it lapses for every such candidate alike, and none is thereby shown right. A narrowed question is a different question; the failure on the first stands. **The one real gap:** the same mistake can come back at another setting, where nobody has yet established what the world does. A corrected rule could repeat Bruno's error on a day in April that nobody has looked at: wrong in fact, but shown by nothing established until someone looks. What catches it is rivals, problems and tests; a record of past rescues would not. File 92 already names this limit, and the cross-examination found no other.

## 4. The seven changes to the theory, and one to the sources note

Draft 4 and draft 5 differ in seven lines of the theory, one for each change, and in nothing else.

**1. The example in the rivals paragraph.** File 92's lamp: two explanations both say "lit", but one says a certain wire carries current and the other says it does not. Whatever the wire does, at most one matches, so they conflict. Draft 4 illustrated this with "as when" two commitments tied to one anchor behave differently, as if such a clash were always enough. It is not: a candidate that says both things about the wire can never match, and by the old sentence it would conflict even with a rewriting of itself. Draft 5 reads "as none do when": the clash shows that no behaviour of the world lets both match, and each must still be able to match on its own.

**2. When a link has a miss: "actually occurring".** Rhea's footbridge swayed under a crowd that actually came, so her link has a miss (file 92, section 9.5). The theory gives a miss only at a setting that actually happens, but a sentence draft 4 added to restate this left those words out. Read as written, her calculation would have a miss for a crowd that never came, had it been wrong for that crowd. Draft 5 puts back the definitions' own words, "actually occurring".

**3. What a candidate is committed to.** Ines's spreading of the salt gives an input slot its value (file 92, section 4.4). Draft 4 said the commitments are the pieces the candidate offers as doing the work, and the rest is background, "including any piece that gives an input slot its value". Read one way, that made such a piece background even when offered as doing the work, so the sentence contradicted itself (file 92, section 14, lists this fork). Draft 5 says "any of them", meaning any of the pieces not offered, so the candidate's offer decides for every piece: Ines's spreading is a commitment if she offers it as doing the work, background if not. The salt case keeps its verdict either way.

**4. The wrong letter in the pole sentence.** The theory writes the pole's height with the letter H and the shadow's length with L. One sentence said that raising the real pole changes the world's shadow but not the calculation's H, where it should say the calculation's L. Draft 5 corrects it. Since the old sentence said something false, the correction counts as a change of claim, not a rewording.

**5. The proof that skipped a step.** Dov's copied footbridge swayed at a crowd outside its eighty years of use: a surprise, because his link was selected; Rhea's constructed link had only a miss. Draft 4 let every link have a miss and kept surprise for selected links, but the **proof on surprise** still began with the old definition, without "by a selected link", and so reached its conclusion through a step it never stated: that only a selected link has trials. Draft 5 puts those words into the proof; what it proves is unchanged.

**6. Which pieces a proof covers.** In the explanation of the shadow, the light piece is a commitment and "the pole as built" is background. The **proof that kinds add nothing** compares each commitment with its anchor, the light piece with the real sunlight, and says nothing about background. But the theory's sentence reporting this proof said it compares "a piece" with its anchor. Draft 5 says a commitment, as file 92 already put it (section 5.2).

**7. The order of definitions.** Whether the gardener's two rescues pose a problem rests on whether they are rivals and whether both fit; whether they fit rests on what she has established; and that rests on the evidence she holds. The theory's **order of definitions** says which definitions rest on which, and shows that nothing goes round in a circle. Draft 4 added rivals, problems and the rest without placing them in it. Draft 5 adds one sentence placing conflict, rivals, what is established, fits, a problem and easy to vary, which shows plainly that none of them rests on a list, a count, a grade or a record.

**And the sources note.** Take a system with a demand, to meet a deadline, and a safeguard, to keep a safety rule. It holds two ways to meet the deadline: a rushed way that breaks the safeguard, and a slower way that keeps it. The theory does not count that as a noticed difficulty, because what the system holds can meet the demand without breaking the safeguard (file 92, section 11.4). The sources note's summary dropped "what the system holds" and "only", and so would have counted it. It now uses the theory's own words. The theory itself does not change here.

## 6. Three choices for you

### First choice: does a failure found by examining a candidate count as established?

**The example.** The myth about winter assumes its answer: Persephone's yearly return is written into her bargain, so it fails the third requirement (file 92, section 4.7). That failure was found by examining the myth, not by looking at the world; nothing the Greeks saw goes against it. Set the myth against the tilt of the Earth's axis, each offered in place of the other on the Greeks' question, which covers only the seasons they knew. Everything turns on whether the myth **fits**.

- If a failure found by examining counts as established, the myth does not fit. The two pose no problem, and the tilt is not easy to vary against it.
- If not, the myth fits, and the two pose a problem outside the range: on that question each is easy to vary against the other.

Either way the theory does not prefer the myth: the myth is no account, and the tilt can be one.

**What the readers said.** This was asked in three packets, so there were six verdicts on it. Five said the theory leaves it open and should settle it so that such failures count; the sixth said the present definitions already count them. None said they should not count. Otherwise, they argued, a candidate that assumes its answer could still fit, pose a problem with a sound account and make that account easy to vary, and no test could ever catch the failure. They differed on how to say it: whether examining a candidate is holding a receipt, whether the sentence on shared blame applies, and where the words go.

**What the checkers found.** The present wording leaves it open and says nothing false either way. Settling it either way would be a change of claim, and the sentence on shared blame, worded for results found by looking or by experiment, would have to agree with it.

**Your choice:** *Should a failure found by examining a candidate itself, rather than by looking at the world, count as established?*

### Second choice: is "offered" what you mean by "discovered"?

**The example.** The gardener's two rescues were each put forward to save her explanation; neither was put forward to replace the other. The theory calls two candidates rivals when one "has been offered … in place of the other" and they conflict. Read alone, that could be narrow: one must have been put forward to replace that particular other, and the two rescues might escape being rivals. Read with the next sentence, "a candidate's rivals are among the candidates someone has offered", it is broad: both were offered for the same question and they conflict, so they are rivals. File 92 takes it the broad way.

**What the readers said.** Atria called "offered" slightly tighter than your "discovered", but no fault; Mimo counted it a loss. The checker found that the clause alone can be taken narrowly, the next sentence takes it broadly, and no case's verdict turns on the difference. Taken either way, a variation someone has thought of but not put forward is no one's rival.

**Your choice:** you said, "If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem." *Is "offered … in place of" what you mean by "discovered"?*

### Third choice: should "easy to vary" point at one side?

**The example.** The tilt and the myth give the same answer for every season the Greeks knew. They come apart only far south of the equator, where the tilt puts winter in the months of the Greek summer, while in the myth the whole world is cold at once. So, if the two are offered in place of each other and the myth fits (the first choice), they pose a problem outside the range of the Greeks' question, and each counts as easy to vary against the other. A finer question, covering the south, is a different question: there they pose a problem inside its range, and a test decides it (find out when winter comes in the south).

**What the readers said.** All four verdicts on it (two packets, both readers) called this harmless: "easy to vary" is tied to a question, applies to both rivals alike, and says nothing about which is right; the tilt stays an account throughout. Both readers argued that marking the tilt hard and the myth easy on the Greeks' question would need a count of each one's variations, which your decision rules out. The checkers found that the present wording states the matter truly.

**Your choice:** *Are you content that, on the Greeks' own question, the tilt counts as easy to vary against the myth for as long as the myth fits, with a finer question as the place where a test decides?*

**Checked.** A separate Claude assistant, which had drafted and decided nothing, checked draft 5 with computer programs: every fix stands exactly as its checker wrote it, nothing else changed, and the theory differs from draft 4 in exactly the seven lines above. It found no discrepancy.

- Two other sentences still say "every piece" where the proof that kinds add nothing covers only commitments; and the theory's word "active", used of commitments, is still undefined.

- "Conflict" is used in two senses, the defined one and an everyday one.
- The definition of a stated input does not yet rule out the standard of worth, which is a foundation.
