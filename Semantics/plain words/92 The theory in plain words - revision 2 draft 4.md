## 1. The theory in one page

The theory is called "Claude Fable Semantics". Its subtitle, "a structural class of explanatory creativity, with selected and constructed correspondence", says two things in plain words. First, the theory describes creativity at explaining by its structure alone, by what depends on what. (A "class", in the theory, is a set of systems, each described in the theory's terms, that meet stated conditions; section 13.4 lists them.) Second, the links between a system's ideas and the world they are about come about in two ways: shaped by selection, or made by the system itself.

The theory tries to say exactly what makes something an explanation, and what makes a system creative at making explanations, or questions, that did not exist before. It does so in claims that could be shown wrong, and that can be checked against **cases**: short everyday stories, each with a **fixed verdict**, what a careful person would say about it, written in advance (section 2).

Its first example: "The shadow is ten metres long because the pole is five metres tall and the sun is low." Raise the pole, and the shadow lengthens. Now fix the shadow's length directly: shine a floodlight on the ground so that the shadow ends wherever the floodlight's beam begins. The shadow is shorter, and the pole stays as it was. So the changes, not the words "pole" and "shadow", show that the dependence runs from pole to shadow. The theory judges every explanation this way: only by what happens when things are changed.

In outline:

- The theory describes the world, and every explanation of it, as a **set-up**. A set-up has slots that hold values (the pole's height, the shadow's length); pieces that tie slots together (in the world, the sunlight; in an explanation, a rule that stands for it, such as "light travels in straight lines"); circumstances held fixed (the flat ground); and changes that can be made (raising the pole). A question says which changes it covers and what it asks (section 3).
- An explanation offered for a question is a candidate. It counts, and is called an **account**, when it meets four requirements, all about how it behaves under the question's changes: its pieces behave like the world's, piece by piece and as a whole; it gives the answer to the question asked; it does not assume that answer; and it does not dodge, either by starting from a situation that cannot exist or by quietly leaving changes out (section 4).
- Some hold that each piece of an explanation must be paired with a piece of the world "of the same kind". The theory says there is nothing more to a kind than how a piece responds to changes, so no such rule is needed (section 5). It reports which pieces of an explanation do the work, without demanding that all of them do (section 6).
- Two candidates are **rivals** when one is offered in place of the other and they conflict somewhere. When nothing yet established (shown by evidence someone holds) decides between them, they pose a problem. A candidate is **easy to vary** when it has a rival that fits the evidence as well and conflicts with it only outside what the question covers. A village rule, "sow in October, after the first rains", is an example. At home, "in October" and "after the first rains" say the same; they come apart only in a land far to the south, which the village's question never covered. Nobody needs a list of every rival there could be; the rivals someone has offered are enough (section 7).
- A question narrowed after a failure is a different question. On the question where a wrong answer was given, any candidate that gives that same wrong answer at the same setting is no account, whoever offers it and however it has been changed. What can lapse is only the evidence that showed the answer wrong: if that evidence rested on, say, a sensor later found faulty, the failure is no longer shown, though nothing is thereby shown right (section 8).
- A **system** is anything the theory is applied to: a person, an animal, a machine or an institution. A **picture**, in the theory, is something in a system that stands for something else by matching it, as a key diagram stands for the key to be cut. The **link** between a picture and what it pictures has a history. It was shaped by selection, with nothing in that history picturing what the link is about; or it was **constructed** by the system through guessing and criticism; or it was merely written into a model. Creativity lives only in construction (section 9). A system originates something when it constructs it, the thing is new to the system, and the system uses it on a question. Finding a question counts too (section 10).
- Whether a fault was mended, who gets the credit, and whether knowledge was created are read from what actually happened. In "Two plumbers", the one who explained the leak correctly did not stop it, and the one who stopped it had no explanation (section 11). Whether a system has an ability is judged inside a stated **boundary**, the line that says what belongs to the system; and no system can do what the laws of physics rule out (section 12).

The theory's **central guess** is that creativity at explaining can be fully described with four things. (They are not the four requirements above.) They are:

- set-ups and the changes they allow (the pole, raised or not);
- links between set-ups, judged only by how they behave under change (the explanation of the shadow, matched against the real pole and sun);
- the history of those links, and of the questions they serve (shaped by selection, or constructed);
- and how all of this is carried by physical things (ink on a page, say).

An achievement these four cannot describe would count against the guess. So would something that has all four and plainly explains nothing.

The theory does not claim that matter alone fixes what a thing stands for (a history is needed too), or that predicting is explaining. It does not say what is beautiful or worthwhile, how probable an explanation is, or which thinker is better, and it does not share out credit beyond what the course of events shows. It defines classes of systems, up to the **universal class**: roughly, systems that could, with fair help, come to understand any explanation and make progress on any clearly stated challenge (section 12). It does not claim that any person or machine belongs to any of them.

The theory has two foundations: **the physics**, meaning whatever physical theory is adopted to say what matter can do; and, where a question is about worth, a standard of worth taken from outside. Everything else is defined from those two, together with two things a claim must state for itself: its **frame**, what it is relative to (such as which changes its question covers), and its **stated inputs**, facts it relies on and must state (such as, in the case "Four seconds" of section 11, which moments a promise to keep a tap running covers). The theory names five **main claims**, each with what would defeat it, and counts any mathematical error in its proofs as a further way to be shown wrong (section 13).

This document is also a progress report. Draft 4 has not yet been tried on the cases, and several verdicts are open (section 14). On one example, "The seasons and the sun god", one way of taking the text may go against the careful person's verdict (section 6). And the theory's own list of ways it could be shown wrong includes any mathematical error in its proofs: a review in September found such an error in one proof of the two earlier texts of the theory. Draft 4 corrects it, so the error counts against those texts, not against draft 4 (sections 7 and 13).

### 2.4 Cases, verdicts and rounds

A **case** is a short everyday situation with a **fixed verdict**: what a careful person would say about it, written in advance and never changed. The project keeps two case books. One holds 52 cases, on which the current text was tried. The other holds **proposed cases**, which no round has used yet. One more proposed case, "The discarded lamp controller", is kept on its own. (Section 19 gives the counts.)

A **round** is one complete run of a text of the theory against the cases. In a round, several readers each read a case against the text and give a **mark**:

- **agrees**: the text gives the fixed verdict;
- **silent**: the text does not reach the fixed verdict, because something it needs is missing;
- **split**: the text can be taken two ways;
- **disagrees**: the text gives a different verdict.

The project then checks each mark's reasons against the text and gives a **ruling**: its final judgement of what the text says on that case, in the same four words. A ruling is not a vote. It can stand against most of the marks when their reasons do not hold up, as in "Greta's dough" (section 8). In the round on the current text, the rulings were: agrees on 46 of the 52 cases, silent on 6, and disagrees on none.

Two computer programs that write and reason in language, from outside the project, are the **outside readers**. They cross-examine the project's rulings and its drafts.

Four judging words come up, and they are kept apart:

| Word | Given by | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| fixed verdict | the careful person, in advance | what should be said about a case |
| mark | one reader in a round | whether the text gives the fixed verdict: agrees, silent, split or disagrees |
| ruling | the project, after checking the marks | the final judgement, in the same four words |
| the theory's verdict | the theory itself | what the theory says about a claim; it can be **unsettled** when something it needs has not been stated (section 13) |

"Verdict" on its own means any judgement on a claim.

### 2.5 How each example is tagged

Each example is a case, unless it is marked as one of the theory's own examples or as this document's illustration. After a case's fixed verdict comes a **tag** in italics.

- *Ruled on the current text: agrees* (or *silent*). The round on the current text ruled it so, and draft 4 does not change what the text says on it. "Agrees" means the theory gives the fixed verdict; "silent" means it does not reach it.
- *Ruled on the current text: silent. Draft 4 not yet ruled.* Draft 4 changes the point, and what follows is the change list's view of how draft 4 handles it.
- *Not yet ruled.* A proposed case, or a point that draft 4 changes. What follows is the change list's view, unless it is marked *this document's view*. Neither is a ruling.
- One case, "The forbidden wire", carries rulings on both earlier texts: *ruled on the old text: disagrees; on the current text: agrees.*

Wherever a view is given, it ends by saying plainly whether, on that view, the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 2.8 Words

Each idea has one plain word, explained at its first use; the main words are gathered in section 16. Some everyday words are kept to one meaning here, sometimes narrower than in everyday speech.

- **Answer**: what a question reads off the target at a setting. A candidate "gives an answer" at each setting, but a candidate is never itself called an answer.
- **Reading**: a slot that reports another, as a thermometer reports a temperature. The number it shows is called "what the reading shows".
- **Test**: finding out what the target does at a setting where two rivals conflict (section 7).
- **Problem**: two rivals that both fit what is established (section 7). A problem in the everyday sense is called a **challenge**.
- **Kind** (section 5) and **new** (section 10).
- **Record**, as a noun: only your record, a record of past failures and of the changes made after each (section 7).
- **Trials**: the settings a selected link met while it was shaped (section 9).
- **Surprise**: a miss by a selected link at a setting outside its trials (section 9). This is narrower than the everyday word.
- **Possible**: allowed by the laws of physics. For a task, it means the task can be done as well as one likes, with no law putting a limit short of perfect (section 12). This is narrower than everyday speech, in which anything that has once happened is possible.
- **Task**: changing a physical thing from one stated property to another, such as water from cold to hot (section 12). Everyday tasks are called jobs.

Where a quoted case uses one of these words in its everyday sense, this document says so.

## 3. The world as a set-up, and questions about it

### 3.1 The fever and the thermometer

Hana says the child has a fever, since the thermometer shows thirty-nine. Later she says the thermometer's number is what makes the child shiver. Warm the thermometer in her hand: the number climbs, and the child shivers as before. Cool the child in a bath: the shivering eases. *Fixed verdict:* her first statement is a good reason to believe there is a fever; her second mistakes the instrument for the illness.

### 3.2 A set-up

Take the pole in the sun. The pole's height, the sun's height in the sky and the shadow's length are **slots**, places that each hold a **value**. The sunlight is a **piece**, a working element that ties slots together: it fixes the shadow's length from the other two. The flat ground belongs to the **circumstances**, held fixed while changes are made. Raising the pole is a **change**.

A **set-up**, then, has four things: slots, with the values they can take; pieces; circumstances; and **allowed changes**, the changes the set-up counts as ones that can be made. Changes can be made one after another. Making no change at all also counts as a change, so that "leave everything as it is" can be one of the situations a question covers.

Under each change and circumstances, each piece has a **behaviour**: the combinations of values it allows among its slots. For the sunlight, a five-metre pole with the sun low and a ten-metre shadow is a combination it allows; the same pole and sun with a two-metre shadow is not. A **state** gives every slot a value. The **consistent states** are those that every piece allows at once.

Two rules keep this description exact. (Section 18 adds three more, which a newcomer can skip.)

- A **removed piece** no longer restricts its slots at all. Take the sunlight away, and nothing fixes the shadow's length.
- A change that **sets** a slot fixes its value directly. It replaces the piece that used to fix that slot, rather than leaving the old piece in place beside a clashing second one. The floodlight is such a change. Once it lights the ground from a chosen point, the shadow ends there, and the sunlight no longer decides where the shadow ends.

Changes need not be physical. The seventh complaint says that mathematics has nothing you can change: nothing in it can be reached into and altered, as a pole can be raised. The theory replies that removing a starting assumption, dropping a condition, or changing a size (such as the number of rows in a grid of numbers) are changes too. Section 4.5 works one through.

### 3.3 Roles and direction

Roles are not labelled in advance; they come from the allowed changes.

- An **input slot** is a slot that some allowed change sets directly, such as the pole's height.
- An **output slot** is a slot that a piece fixes from its other slots, such as the shadow's length.
- A **reading** is a slot that reports another, where some allowed change alters the report without altering what is reported. The thermometer's number is a reading of the child's temperature: warming the thermometer in a hand alters what it shows, and leaves the child as she was.

**Direction** follows too. Raise the pole and the shadow lengthens; set the shadow's length directly with the floodlight and the pole stays put. So dependence runs from pole to shadow. That holds whichever way round the calculation linking them is written: "shadow from height" and "height from shadow" use the same numbers, but only one of them behaves like the world when things are changed.

### 3.4 A question

Take Hana's second question, "what makes the child shiver?" A **question** has five things.

- A **target**: the set-up in the world that the question is about. Here, the child, her temperature, the thermometer and what it shows.
- A **range**: the **settings** it covers, a setting being one allowed change made in given circumstances. Here, cooling the child in a bath, or warming the thermometer in a hand. The **untouched setting**, with no change made, in the circumstances the question states, is always in the range.
- **What is asked**: the exact thing read off the target at each setting. Here, whether she shivers.
- The demands and safeguards in play, which matter only when a question is about mending something (section 11).
- The history of its range: how the range came about (section 3.7).

The **answer** is what the target gives, for what is asked, at each setting. Here, she shivers at the untouched setting and when the thermometer is warmed, and shivers less after the bath. A candidate (section 4) also gives an answer at each setting, and it is right there when its answer is the target's.

A range holds the settings the question states, and no more. **The table with empty columns** shows this. Lea asks what happens when a freezer's two knobs are turned together. A colleague writes her question out as an everyday table, with a column for each knob and a row for each setting to be tried. Every row moves both knobs together; no row moves one alone. The case asks whether the table asks more than Lea did, since its columns could hold a row that moves one knob alone. *Fixed verdict:* "The table is faithful. Its columns could have carried single-knob rows, and none was added. A form that could ask more has not asked more." *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict: a range holds only the settings actually stated, however much more the form it is written in could hold.

The sort of question is fixed by what it reads and what its range holds, never by a label.

- A **producing question** asks what produces an outcome. Its range has settings that change what comes before the outcome. ("What makes her shiver?")
- A **pinning-down question** asks what a reading settles about a feature. Its range has settings that alter what the reading shows. ("The thermometer shows thirty-nine: has she a fever?", asked for each number it might show.)
- A **blocking question** asks whether one state can be reached from another at all, or whether something blocks the way. ("Why can no machine run forever?", section 3.6.)
- A **status question** asks what status something has under a **status rule**, a rule that gives things a status, as the rules of chess make a move legal or not. ("Is this move legal?") No case in the books is about one; section 5.3 says how a status rule behaves.
- A **purpose question** asks whether a purpose is achieved (section 11.8 says what that means).

A different range, or a different thing asked, makes a **different question**, and an answer to one is not an answer to another. A reading that settles an outcome, with a reliable forecast from it, answers the pinning-down question. Whether the thing read also produces the outcome is the producing question, and the first answer is not the second.

**Back to Hana.** *Ruled on the current text: agrees; draft 4 does not change it.* The thermometer's number is a reading: change only what it shows, and the child is unchanged; change the child, and what it shows follows. It answers "has she a fever?", a pinning-down question. "What makes her shiver?" is a producing question. Its range has settings that change her temperature, and there the thermometer's number does nothing. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 3.5 Pinning down and blocking: two of the theory's examples

**Two balances** (the theory's example; the numbers are this document's illustration). An object is weighed on two balances. Each shows the object's mass plus that balance's own unknown error. Say the object weighs 100 grams, balance A adds 2 grams and balance B takes away 1: A shows 102 and B shows 99. Now picture instead an object of 101 grams, with A adding 1 gram and B taking away 2: A still shows 102, and B still shows 99. Nothing the balances show tells these two situations apart, so what they show does not settle the mass. What it does settle is the gap between the two errors: in both situations A's error is 3 grams more than B's, as 102 minus 99 says. So the example explains a limit on what readings can settle.

In general, the **look-alike states** of a reading are all the states that make it show the same thing. A reading **pins down** a feature when some state makes it show that, and all its look-alike states agree on the feature. Here the mass is not pinned down, since the look-alike states include masses of 100 and 101 grams; the gap between the errors is. The theory's **proof on pinning down** shows that a feature is pinned down, whatever the reading shows, exactly when some rule gives the feature from what the reading shows alone. That sounds obvious, and the proof is short. Its use is that it gives a way to check: find such a rule, or find two look-alike states that differ on the feature.

Two things follow. Repeating the same weighing adds nothing, since it has the same look-alike states. A **calibration**, weighing something whose mass is already known, can add something: it can tell you a balance's error. And "take balance B's error to be zero, because that gives the mass I favour" is not drawn from the readings: its reasoning goes in a circle, even if the mass it gives happens to be right. Section 18 gives the exact statement.

**Twenty-three tokens** (the theory's example). Twenty-three whole tokens cannot be shared equally among three people. Handing a token from one person to another never changes the total, and three equal whole shares always make a total that three divides, which twenty-three is not.

An **unchanging quantity** is one that no allowed change alters, like the total here. The theory's **proof on blocking** shows that no chain of allowed changes joins two states where an unchanging quantity differs. The reverse does not hold: two states with the same total need not be **reachable** one from the other, reachable meaning that some chain of allowed changes leads from one to the other. For instance (this document's illustration), suppose the only allowed change were for the first person to hand a token to the second. Then the third person's share could never change, and a sharing that gives the third person more could never be reached, though its total is the same.

Allowing tokens to be cut makes different states, and so a different question. It does not refute a claim about whole tokens.

### 3.6 A case about blocking

**The machine that runs forever.** At a fair, visitors ask why no machine drives a water pump forever, with no fuel, no wind, no flowing water and nobody pushing it. Pia says hundreds of designs have been tried and every one has stopped, and perhaps someone will find one that works. Quentin says the energy anything gives out must be balanced by energy put in or used up, and that holds for any design, tried or not. *Fixed verdict:* only Quentin explains; Pia gives evidence and a hope, but no reason.

*Not yet ruled.* The change list expects the verdict to hold. It notes one risk: a reader might call Quentin's energy law the answer restated, which section 4 shows is not allowed. It expects the law to pass, since the law limits every design and the answer is only one of its consequences. *This document's view* of the reasoning: it is a blocking question. Quentin gives an unchanging quantity, energy, so no chain of changes leads to a pump that works with nothing used up, whatever the design. Pia gives a list of what was seen: designs tried, and all stopped. Nothing in the list responds when anything is changed, and section 4.4 shows why such a list cannot be an account. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 3.7 Where a range comes from, and faulty questions

A range has one of three histories.

- **Declared**: written in by the **modeller**, whoever writes the model down. ("The range is: raise or lower the pole.")
- **Selected**: shaped by selection, which section 9 describes: the survivor of a pool of ranges that were varied and weeded out, with nothing in that history picturing the range. The theory gives no example, no case in the books shows one, and nothing later in this document depends on it.
- **Constructed**: made by the system itself in an **episode**, roughly a stretch of inquiry from a noticed difficulty to a response (section 11.4 says what a complete one holds), with evidence of how it was made (section 10.3). Noor's question in "The rota" (section 10.5) is the example.

Judging whether a candidate is an account works the same way whichever history its range has. Only a claim that someone *found* a question needs a constructed range, with the evidence.

**Where the second compartment used to be.** A change removes a freezer's second compartment. Lea had asked for that compartment's temperature. After the change, a colleague answers with the temperature of the space where it used to be. *Fixed verdict:* "A meaning has been supplied, and the question now asks something. It is not what Lea asked." *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict, for the reason at the end of this section.

A **faulty question** is one of three sorts.

- It misses its target. On *this document's view*, once the compartment is gone, Lea's question no longer picks out anything at that setting.
- It assumes an untouched setting that cannot be: its starting point has no consistent state, as a question about a freezer both running and unplugged would.
- It asks for things that clash, such as one temperature that is at once the compartment's and the empty space's.

A faulty question was still asked. Its asking is part of what happened, so it can itself be criticized, and showing that it is faulty is a different question, with its own range. What is forbidden is changing the range, or what is asked, during an **assessment**, one act of judging a candidate with the range held fixed, without recording that the claim has changed. Supplying a meaning for a replacement question makes a different question; it does not answer the first one.

The theory gives no way of choosing the level of detail at which a question should be asked (section 4.3 explains level of detail).

## 4. When an explanation counts: the four requirements

### 4.1 The tide table with a real bell attached

Carla explains why the harbour water is high at noon today with two things. One is the printed almanac of high and low water for every day. The other is a real float that, rising past a line, tips a lever and rings the harbour bell. *Fixed verdict:* "Carla has explained the bell. She has explained nothing about the tide. The almanac is the answer written down."

### 4.2 A candidate

Take the explanation of the shadow as a candidate for the question "what fixes the shadow's length?", whose settings raise the pole and move the sun.

The candidate has its own set-up: slots for the pole's height, the sun's angle and the shadow's length, and a piece, "light travels in straight lines", which fixes the shadow's length from the other two. In the world the piece is the sunlight itself; in an explanation it is a rule that stands for it.

The candidate's **link** says which of the target's states, changes and circumstances go with which of the candidate's: raising the real pole goes with raising the pole in the candidate. The link **carries over** each setting of the target to the candidate's own setting that goes with it. A link always runs between two set-ups in this way. Here it runs from the world to an explanation; section 9 uses the same kind of link between a picture and what it pictures, and between two layers inside a system.

The link also ties each piece of the candidate to an **anchor**: the part of the target that the piece must behave like. Here the candidate's light piece is tied to the real sunlight. An anchor can be a **cluster**, a connected bunch of the target's pieces, because one piece of an explanation may stand for several working parts of the world at once. Here it is only one. A **slot pairing** says which slot of the anchor goes with which slot of the piece: the candidate's height slot with the real pole's height, and so on.

The **commitments** are the pieces the candidate offers as doing the work, whether or not anyone has described what work they do (section 6.3 has a case of a piece nobody described). Here, the light piece is the commitment. Everything else the candidate holds fixed is **background**: the values of its circumstances, and its other pieces, including any piece that gives an input slot its value. Here the pole's height is an input slot, and "the pole as built", which holds the height at five metres until a change sets it otherwise, is background.

In general, anything offered as an explanation for a question, true or false, is a **candidate**. An **account** is a candidate that meets the four requirements below.

### 4.3 The four requirements

A candidate is an account of a question exactly when it meets four requirements, each about how it behaves at the settings of the range. None looks at a label.

**1. Matching.** This has two halves, and both are needed.

- *Piece by piece:* at every setting of the range, each commitment allows exactly what its anchor allows, once the anchor's **hidden slots** are set aside. Hidden slots are slots inside the anchor that the commitment does not mention: the real sunlight has a brightness, for instance, which the light piece never mentions and which does not change where the shadow ends. The candidate's light piece gives the same shadow for each height and sun as the real sunlight does.
- *As a whole:* at every setting, the target's consistent states, carried over, are exactly the candidate's consistent states; and a chain of changes in the target carries over as the same chain in the candidate.

Why both? A candidate can get the whole right while splitting it into the wrong pieces. For instance, an explanation of a door hung on two hinges might say that the top hinge carries all the weight: the door still hangs straight, but that piece does not behave like the real top hinge. And pieces that each behave right can lose something that holds them together. For instance, two pieces might each describe one of two meshed gears correctly, in an explanation that forgets the gears are meshed; then the whole allows one gear to turn while the other stands still, which the world does not allow.

**2. Answering the question asked.** At every setting of the range, the candidate gives the target's answer, with what is asked held fixed. An account of a different thing asked is not an account of this one.

**3. No assumed answer.** The answer must follow by working out the candidate's set-up in its circumstances. This has two halves.

- *The answer is not written in.* The target's answer may not appear unexplained, as a value put into an input slot or as a piece, even one labelled "law". This is judged at the stated **level of detail**, how finely things are described, and by the statement's form at that level. It is not enough that two statements are equivalent in logic. In mathematics any true statement can be proved from any other, so if equivalence were enough, every proof would count as assuming its answer. A long working-out, step by step, is not the bare answer "this grid cannot be undone", though the two are equivalent (section 4.5).
- *Some commitments make the difference.* Take Ines's salt (section 4.4). The range has two settings: salt spread on the icy step, which is what happened, and salt swept off at once. The answers differ: the ice melts, or it stays. Now delete from the candidate the piece that says what salt does to ice. The candidate can no longer tell the two settings apart. That is what this half asks. In general: at some setting of the range, the answer must differ from the answer at the untouched setting; and some **group** of commitments must carry that difference, so that deleting the group leaves the candidate unable to tell the two settings apart, either giving the same definite answer at both, or no definite answer at one of them. (The theory also allows a second sort of difference, which no case here uses: the answer at that setting is not settled in the way the candidate claims.)

Both halves are about one thing: the answer must come from the commitments, not from what the candidate takes as given.

**4. No dodging.** A candidate must not dodge its question in either of two ways.

- *By starting from nothing.* The target must have at least one consistent state at the untouched setting. A question about a situation that cannot exist, such as a freezer both running and unplugged, gives nothing to be checked against.
- *By leaving changes out quietly.* The range is a stated selection from the **possible** changes, those the laws of physics allow. Any possible change the range leaves out must be left out by a stated **limit** (section 8), not silently.

Under this requirement the theory adds a point that is really about the third. Suppose a range holds only **renaming changes**, changes that rename things and alter nothing else (calling the pole "the post", say), or leaves out every change at which the commitments could matter. Then no setting has a different answer from the untouched one, the commitments have no difference to make, and no candidate can meet the third requirement on that range. So no account can be claimed on it.

**Back to Carla.** *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* Her only piece about the tide is the almanac, which is the answer written in, so she fails the third requirement. A real mechanism that answers another question beside it (why the bell rings) does not change this: deleting the bell loses no difference in the tide. Draft 4 also makes plain that both her pieces are commitments, since she offers both. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

**The domino that never falls.** A huge network of spring-loaded dominoes is built as a calculating machine: each falling domino knocks over its neighbour and springs back up, so waves of falls travel along branching lines and do arithmetic, very slowly. This network checks whether a number is prime. The number is set up as a row of exactly that many dominoes, and one particular domino falls only if the network finds a number that divides it exactly. It is set up for 641 and started, and that domino never falls. Explanation A: none of its neighbours ever falls at a moment that would knock it over, and so on back; nothing ever knocks it over. Explanation B: 641 is prime; the network hunts for divisors, and that domino falls only when one is found. *Fixed verdict:* B explains; A, though true, does not really.

*Not yet ruled.* A says what the observer already saw, "that domino never falls", only in terms of single dominoes: "nothing ever knocks it over" is the same fact, told one domino at a time. So A's only working piece is the answer restated, and A fails the third requirement. B has a piece that responds to a change: set the network up for 640, which has divisors, and the domino falls. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 4.4 What the requirements exclude, and what they do not

- A **table of observed answers** lists answers seen, with no piece that responds when the world is changed. It fails matching on any range with a setting that makes such a change. A **full response table** records how the set-up responds to every allowed change. It passes matching, and it is judged on the other three requirements like any other candidate. The word "table" settles nothing. (Carla's almanac lists answers too, but the ruling on it rests on the third requirement, above.)
- A **calculation run backwards** works an earlier quantity out from a later one, and offers that as what produces it. On the producing question it fails whole matching. In the backwards calculation, the pole's height is worked out from the shadow, so the shadow is where the working starts. Raise the real pole, and the real shadow lengthens. Raise the pole in the calculation, and nothing in it moves the shadow: the calculation's shadow stays as it was. The calculation may still match on the pinning-down question ("how tall is the pole, given this shadow?"), which is a different question.
- "It is so because it is so" fails the third requirement. So does a candidate whose only real piece restates the answer, whatever mechanism for another question sits beside it.
- A candidate fails the fourth requirement when the only difference it makes lies at a change that nothing possible could bring about. Such a candidate has, in effect, quietly left out every possible change at which it could be checked. For instance, an explanation of a pendulum's swing whose only working difference lies between gravity as it is and gravity switched off: nothing possible switches gravity off, and the possible changes that would check it, such as lengthening the string, have been left out without saying so.

**The planets tonight and a thousand years ago.** Priya calculates where the planets were a thousand years ago from where they are tonight, and says they were there *because* they are here tonight. *Fixed verdict:* the earlier arrangement, with the laws of motion, explains tonight's; not the reverse.

*Not yet ruled.* Take the forward explanation first: the positions a thousand years ago, with the laws of motion, give tonight's. Its commitments are the laws of motion. The old positions are background: they are the starting values, the values of the input slots, as "the pole as built" was. Delete the laws, and the explanation can no longer tell a change in the old positions from no change, so the laws carry the difference, and the forward explanation meets the third requirement. Priya's is a calculation run backwards, and it fails twice. Move a planet's position of a thousand years ago, and tonight's sky changes in the world, but her calculation, which starts from tonight, does not follow: it fails whole matching. And changes made tonight leave the past as it was, so at those settings the answer about the past never differs, and there is no difference for her calculation to carry: it fails the third requirement. *This document's view* adds that her "because" answers "where were they?", a pinning-down question, as the calculation run backwards above does. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

**Salt on the icy step.** Ines says the ice melted because she spread salt: spread it, and the ice melts within the hour; sweep it off at once, and the ice stays. She knows nothing finer. *Fixed verdict:* a genuine explanation, a coarse one. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* A coarse account answers the coarse question, and its strength is fixed by its range, not by what a finer range would add. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

Draft 4 states the reason more directly (*not yet ruled*). Whether salt is on the step is an input slot: spreading the salt and sweeping it off are the changes that set it. Whatever gives that slot its value (Ines's spreading, or the broom) is background, as the pole as built is. What the salt then does to the ice is the commitment. The difference between spread and swept starts in the input slot, but what carries it to the ice is the salt's action: delete that piece, and the difference is lost. So draft 4 gives the fixed verdict on firmer ground.

Nor do the requirements exclude a true mechanism guessed for bad reasons, or prefer an elegant account to a clumsier one that matches as well. A proof meets them for the question "why does this follow from these assumptions?" without answering "what caused this?". How deep an explanation goes, how far into finer workings, depends on the question, and the requirements do not measure it.

### 4.5 Odd-sized grids: a mathematical example

This is the theory's example, and it replies to the seventh complaint, that mathematics has nothing to change. A square grid of numbers can be used to turn one list of numbers into another. It can be **undone** when some other grid turns every result back into the list it came from. Take square grids with an odd number of rows, in which each number is the negative of its mirror image across the diagonal (so the diagonal is all noughts). No such grid can be undone.

Why? One number worked out from a grid, which mathematicians call its determinant, is nought exactly when the grid cannot be undone. The question's range drops the mirroring, or the oddness, or both, holding ordinary arithmetic fixed. The long working-out of that number, with the mirroring put in, meets all four requirements, so it is an account. Drop the mirroring, or drop the oddness, and a grid that can be undone appears, so the difference is carried by those two commitments. A three-line proof is shorter, but shortness is not a fifth requirement. Here the changes are dropped assumptions, which is the answer to the complaint. Section 18 gives the working.

### 4.6 Explanations of absence

**Denying the inner experience.** Wren says there are no inner experiences, and people are mistaken in thinking they have them. Yuri says the same, and adds that people looking inwards consult memories of what they have just perceived, through machinery that evolved to report experiences that never took place. *Fixed verdict:* Wren gives no reason why people believe they have inner experiences; Yuri gives one, though an incomplete one.

A question "why is there no such-and-such?" works like this.

- Its target lacks the pieces that the opposing view needs: here, the inner experiences themselves.
- Its range holds changes that would add those pieces to the set-up, as one might ask how things would go in a world that had them.
- An **explanation of absence** passes when adding those pieces would change its answer as it would change the target's, and leaving them out changes neither.
- The opposing view's pieces are tied to something the target does not have, as if to a removed piece. A removed piece restricts nothing (section 3.2), so a piece of an explanation that claims to do work there, by restricting something, cannot match its anchor.

Why the absent thing *seems* present is a different question. An account of the absence neither answers it nor needs to. A **bare denial** offers no piece that responds to a change in what produces the appearance, so it is no account of that second question.

*Not yet ruled.* Wren's is a bare denial. Yuri offers such a piece: machinery that reads memories and produces reports. Whether it is a good one, the question does not ask. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

*Departure:* Deutsch asks an explanation that denies something to explain why it seems to exist; here that is a different question.

### 4.7 Candidates and accounts

**A myth about winter.** A Greek farmer asks why winter comes every year, and is told the myth: Persephone must return to Hades for some months of every year, and while she is away her mother Demeter grieves and makes the world cold. Taken as it stood for the Greeks, the myth agrees with all they knew. *Fixed verdict:* it does not explain why winter comes every year; it is an explanation only in form, an attempt, and a false one.

*Not yet ruled.* The yearly return is written into Persephone's bargain, so the answer is assumed, and the myth is no account. A false explanation offered for a question is still a candidate, which ordinary speech may call an explanation. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict: a candidate, not an account.

Two **principles** of the theory stand behind the requirements.

- Whether a link matches is a fact, whether or not anyone accepts it. A system can be wrong about its links, its readings, its criticisms and its abilities.
- An explanation may hold a correct dependence alongside errors elsewhere. But an error in the very dependence said to do the work cannot count as an account. On *this document's view*, that already follows from matching, since such a candidate fails to match; the principle says it outright so that it is clear an account may still carry errors elsewhere.

In this document, "explanation" is an everyday word for anything offered as the reason something is so; "candidate" and "account" are the theory's terms. Whether an account is easy to vary is a separate matter (section 7).

*Departure:* Deutsch counts a false myth as an explanation; here it is a candidate.

## 5. Kinds are how pieces respond

### 5.1 The float that does two jobs

A float in a fuel tank turns a dial on the dashboard and, when it drops low enough, opens a valve to the reserve tank. Milan says the falling float opens the valve; Lena says the dial reaching "low" does. Bend the needle to show "full": the valve still opens. Hold the float up with a wire: the dial shows "full" and the valve stays shut. *Fixed verdict:* Milan is right; "The float is read by the dial and also does the work."

Two changes, not the names "float" and "dial", showed which piece does the work. That is the theory's whole view of kinds.

### 5.2 Response patterns and kinds

Across the two changes above, the float and the dial respond differently. Bending the needle changes what the dial shows and leaves the valve as it was. Holding the float up changes the dial and keeps the valve shut. A piece's **response pattern** is its behaviour at every setting of a range, taken together.

Two pieces are one **kind** on a range when their slots can be paired one to one so that their response patterns agree. So kinds depend on the range, that is, on which changes are allowed; a level of detail matters to kinds only through the changes it allows. Pieces that no allowed change tells apart are one kind, whatever they are called. They may come apart on a **finer range**, one with more settings. A response pattern is built from behaviour, not from the values the slots happen to hold, so pieces that differ only in their values are one kind.

The same comparison is made between a piece of one candidate and a piece of another, and between a commitment and its anchor.

**Two thermostats.** Two thermostats of one make are set to eighteen and twenty-two degrees. Rosa says they are one kind of thing in two states; Emil says they are two kinds, one keeping a warm room and one a cool one. *Fixed verdict:* Rosa is right about what the devices are; Emil's difference matters only when the question is why this room is warm.

*Ruled on the current text: agrees*, though several readers in the round marked the theory silent; the ruling weighed their reasons (section 2.4). Two points settle it, and draft 4 now states both outright. A change that sets a slot, such as turning a dial from eighteen to twenty-two, replaces only what fixes that slot's value; it does not change how the pieces that read the slot behave. And a change under which two pieces behave alike does not tell them apart. Eighteen and twenty-two are values of one slot, and both devices behave alike whatever it holds. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 5.3 Cause, measurement and status rule

Take the pole again. Set the shadow's length directly, with the floodlight, and the sunlight's hold on the shadow is replaced. Misread the shadow with a badly held ruler, and the sunlight is unaffected. Now the thermometer. Set the child's temperature directly, with a bath: the thermometer measures just as before, and the number it shows follows the child. Warm the thermometer in a hand: now its number no longer comes from the child at all, so the measuring itself has been altered.

What ordinary speech calls a cause, a measurement or a rule is a family of response patterns, not an extra fact.

- A **cause** is a piece whose behaviour changes when its output slot is set directly, or when the piece itself is replaced, and is unaffected when only a report of its output is altered. The sunlight is a cause of the shadow's length: the floodlight replaces its hold, and the misread ruler leaves it alone.
- A **measurement** is a piece whose behaviour is unaffected when the thing measured is set directly, and changes when the measuring itself is altered. The thermometer's measuring is one: the bath leaves it as it was, and warming the thermometer in a hand alters it.
- A **status rule** is a piece unaffected by changes to the world, which changes when the rule itself is edited. The rules of chess do not change when a move is made on the board; they change when the rule book is rewritten. A status rule answers a status question. Whether it governs a practice, or should, are different questions. A changed status rule counts as a changed piece, like any other.

So the theory never asks whether a piece "is" a cause. Anything that reports another thing, as the thermometer reports the child's temperature, has a measurement's pattern: change only the report, and what it reports stays as it was; change what it reports, and the report follows. Which of the two an explanation offers as producing an outcome is settled by the pattern, not by the explanation's wording.

**Back to the float.** *Ruled on the current text: agrees; draft 4 does not change it.* The dial has a measurement's pattern, and the float has a cause's pattern for the valve. So the theory gives the fixed verdict: Milan is right.

### 5.4 No requirement about kinds

Should each piece of an account have to be tied to a piece of the world "of the same kind"? Such an **anchoring requirement** would be redundant or empty. If the range has a setting that tells the two kinds apart, a piece tied to the wrong one already fails piece-by-piece matching, as holding the float up with a wire told the float from the dial. If the range has no such setting, the two are one kind on that range. There is no third case.

The theory's **proof that kinds add nothing** shows that piece-by-piece matching gives each commitment its anchor's response pattern, setting by setting. So "kind" can be dropped from the definition of an account without losing any case. That is the third of the five main claims (section 13.5). It would be defeated by a case in which a label told apart two accounts that no allowed change tells apart, and the difference did real explaining. The theory holds that no such case exists; to show it wrong, someone would have to find real explaining that the changes miss. Nobody has yet (section 14).

### 5.5 Three complaints

*"Without kinds you cannot tell a cause from a correlation."* You can, and only this way. A correlation has no piece that responds when its supposed input slot is changed; a cause has one. A label adds nothing. If some allowed change tells the two apart, matching already finds it. If no allowed change tells them apart, the label claims a difference that the level of detail does not have.

*"So a thing is whatever you can do to it."* No. A kind is only what the allowed changes at a level of detail can tell apart. The explanation itself keeps every one of its pieces, including pieces that no change at that level tells apart. Those are one kind at that level, and that is correct, not a loss. On a finer range, with more changes, they may come apart. So the claim is only that every claim about kinds is tied to a level of detail and its changes.

*"Kinds obviously exist. A rule is not a cause."* Agreed that a rule is not a cause, and the difference is one of response pattern, as section 5.3 shows. What the theory denies is that the difference exists before, or apart from, the allowed changes.

### 5.6 Matching over many steps

**Keeping count with string.** Two groups of goatherds keep count of their flocks with string. Nobody can measure string exactly: every length they measure out is off by up to a tenth of an inch. In the first group, as each goat leaves the pen in the morning, a herder reels out a length of string as long as that goat's body; in the evening, as each goat returns, its length is reeled back in, and when all the string is back, all the goats are home. In the second group, each goat stands for exactly one inch of string, and after every goat the herder trims or lengthens the string to the nearest whole inch. Both groups also add flocks together by joining strings, and split flocks by cutting a string in two. *Fixed verdict:* only the rounding method keeps count of a flock of any size. In the first, the error piles up with every goat, join and cut, until it is more than a goat's length, and then the string can no longer show whether a goat is missing.

Two proofs of the theory concern chains of changes like these.

- **Matching step by step.** If an explanation matches the world for each single change, it matches for every chain of those changes, so long as each step stays within the settings where its link applies. For instance (this document's illustration), a tally that matches the flock for each goat leaving and each goat returning matches it for any day's comings and goings. Nobody needs to check every chain. (Section 18 adds a related result, on matching in one direction or in both.)
- **Error build-up.** This is for candidates that match only approximately. It says how small errors add up over many steps.

The **proof on error build-up** works like this, in the goatherds' terms. Suppose each step of the candidate lands at most a small **step error** away from where the target's step lands: here, a tenth of an inch per goat, join or cut. And suppose there is a fixed **widening factor**: a number such that one step of the candidate never makes an existing gap more than that many times larger. Then, from a shared start, the gap after many steps is at most the step errors added up, each enlarged by the widening factor once for every later step. If there is no fixed widening factor, because a single step can blow a small gap up without limit, no ceiling on the gap follows at all. And coming close is not matching: a question that asks for matching is not quietly answered by a candidate that only comes close. Section 18 gives the proof exactly.

*Not yet ruled.* For the first group the widening factor is one: measuring, joining and cutting never make an existing gap larger. So the ceiling grows by a tenth of an inch for every goat, join or cut, without limit. That fits the first half of the verdict, but only loosely: the proof gives a ceiling, and does not show that the error must actually grow. The rounding method lies outside the proof altogether, since rounding removes the error at every step, which the proof does not describe. So the theory does not by itself reach this verdict. Its proof is consistent with the first half and silent on the second, which rests on the case's own reasoning.

## 6. Which pieces do the work

### 6.1 The seasons and the sun god

Tomas explains the seasons. The Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing the same way in space as the Earth goes round the sun, as a spinning top keeps its axis steady. For half the year each half of the Earth leans towards the sun, and for the other half it leans away. Each step can be checked on its own. Then he adds: "And the sun god, who approves of this arrangement, keeps the tilt steady." *Fixed verdict:* "Yes, Tomas explains the seasons. No, the sun-god sentence is not part of what explains them."

The theory's verdict here is not settled. Taken one way, the text gives the fixed verdict; taken other ways, it may not. Section 6.5 explains.

### 6.2 Supports and load-bearing groups

Tomas's explanation has several commitments: the tilt, the axis keeping one direction, the point about spinning bodies, and the sun god. Keep only some of them, with the background held fixed, and you have a **cut-down candidate**. Keep the first three, say. The way of cutting candidates down is stated in advance; the theory does not say by whom, or anything more about it (section 19 notes that it is not among the stated inputs).

A **support** is a group of commitments that by itself, with the background, makes an account. If Tomas's first three commitments make an account, they are a support. A **smallest support** is one from which nothing can be taken without losing it. The theory does not assume that adding a commitment to a support always leaves a support (section 6.4 shows it can fail). Nor does it assume that every support contains a smallest support.

A group is **load-bearing** in a support when the support stops being one without it. A group can be load-bearing though none of its members is alone. With two springs, each enough by itself to swing a door back (section 6.3), taking both away breaks the support, while taking either one away leaves the other doing the job.

Load-bearing is relative to the support being judged. And the supports judged are groups drawn from the commitments the candidate actually has, whether or not anyone has pointed to a particular group as a support. They are never groups that need commitments the candidate does not carry.

**A premise both routes use.** Two lines of proof lead to the answer (the case calls them routes; in this document's words they are supports), and both use premise P. The second line could be rewritten without P, but nobody has written that version. *Fixed verdict:* P is load-bearing in each line as written; that another line could be written without it is "a fact about a candidate nobody has written". *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict. Draft 4 adds a reason: a candidate nobody has offered is no one's rival (section 7.3).

Suppose three things: the candidate has a limited number of commitments, not endlessly many; adding a commitment never breaks a support; and the whole candidate is a support. Then the theory's **proof on smallest supports** gives two simple rules. A single commitment is load-bearing in some support exactly when it belongs to some smallest support. And it is **needed in every support** exactly when it belongs to every smallest support. So, under those assumptions, finding the smallest supports tells you which commitments do the work. Where the assumptions fail, as with interference (section 6.4), the rules do not apply. Section 18 states the proof exactly.

### 6.3 Two springs, and supports that run

**The spring nobody mentioned.** An explanation of why a door swings back holds two connected springs, each enough alone. The second is never described as doing anything, until the first is deleted from the explanation; then whoever gave the explanation says the second was always there. *Fixed verdict:* "It was already a route. That nobody said it did work does not make it a later addition." ("Route" is again the case's word for a support.)

*Ruled on the current text: agrees.* The two springs give **redundant supports**: each spring alone is a support, and neither is needed. The second spring was a support from the start, whether or not anything ever set it working. Draft 4 rests this on firmer text: commitments are the pieces offered as doing the work "whether or not anyone has described their work". So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

**The cable that used to be a spring.** After the first spring is deleted, whoever gave the explanation takes the piece that stood for the surviving spring, says it now stands for a cable, shows that the door still returns, and reports that the spring explanation survived. *Fixed verdict:* what survived is a replacement, and "the replacement's success is its own". *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* A piece tied to a different anchor makes a different candidate, judged on its own. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

A support is a group of commitments in an explanation. It **runs** in a real system when actual events carry it out. Open the real door, and the second spring stretches and then pulls the door shut: those pulls are events, and they carry out the support "the second spring swings the door back". Such events form a **route that ran**. Section 11.2 says why credit follows routes that ran, not the supports a candidate contains.

Two machines can give the same outputs, whatever goes in, and still differ in which supports run. The theory names parallel and priority wiring as its example, without details. In plain terms: in parallel wiring two circuits work at once, and in priority wiring one works while the other waits, and the two can give the same outputs. The theory's **proof that outputs do not settle accounts** shows what follows. When two systems give the same outputs, whatever goes in, but differ on a claim about an account, no judgement made from outputs alone can be right about both. The theory adds two everyday instances. Who wrote a text cannot always be judged from the text alone, since two writers can reach the same words by different routes. And whether something delivered was actually used cannot be judged from delivery logs alone.

That is why predicting is not explaining. A prediction looks only at outputs. An account is judged by its pieces and the supports that run, and two systems with the same outputs can differ there.

### 6.4 Interference and idle commitments

**One more commitment.** Every earlier addition to a finite collection of commitments kept its support. The newest, that the room was locked, conflicts with a premise every line of it uses. (The case gives no more detail; picture, say, an explanation of how someone got into a room, every line of which uses the premise that they came through the door.) *Fixed verdict:* the conclusion no longer follows; "additions could not always be made without loss". *Ruled on the current text: agrees.*

That is **interference**: adding a commitment breaks a support. The whole fails although a smaller group succeeds. Nor can whoever gave the explanation get round the failure by declaring, after the fact, that the troublesome commitment was never really a commitment. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

With endlessly many commitments, stranger things can happen: a whole endless group can do work that no single commitment and no smallest group does. The theory has a mathematical example of this; no case in the books is about it, and section 18 gives it.

None of the four requirements asks that each commitment do some work. Take a candidate that has at least one support. An **idle commitment** is one that makes no difference to any support: adding it to any support leaves a support, and removing it from any support leaves a support. (For a support that does not contain it, removing it changes nothing.) The candidate is then an account with it exactly when it is one without it, and it is load-bearing in no support.

Both halves are needed.

- The removal half keeps out a redundant spring. Take the support whose only spring is the second one. Remove the second spring from it, and no support is left. So the second spring is not idle.
- The adding half keeps out interference. Add "the room was locked" to a support, and the support breaks. So that commitment is not idle either.
- The definition applies only to a candidate that has a support. In a candidate with no support at all, even a wrong commitment would pass both halves, since there would be no support to break.

*Departure:* Deutsch counts superfluous details as a defect of an explanation; here nothing counts against a candidate for carrying one.

### 6.5 Back to Tomas

*Not yet ruled.* If the god sentence restricts nothing, because the spinning-top point already explains why the tilt stays steady, then it is idle and load-bearing in no support, and the fixed verdict follows: Tomas explains, and the sentence is not part of what explains. But the text can be taken two other ways, if the god is offered as a second piece that keeps the tilt steady.

- If that piece matches its anchor, it is a second, redundant support, like the second spring. Then it does some explaining, which goes against the verdict's second half.
- If it does not match, it is interference. The whole of what Tomas said then fails piece-by-piece matching, and so is not an account, though the part without the sun god still is one. Whether that still counts as "Tomas explains" is open.

Which way applies turns on what Tomas offers the sentence as doing, and the case does not say. So, taken the first way, the theory gives the fixed verdict; taken either of the other two, it may not.

## 7. Rivals, problems and tests: what "easy to vary" means here

### 7.1 The gardener's two rescues

This illustration comes from the project's note on rivals of 25 September; it is not a case.

A gardener says her south bed ripens first because the south wall gets the most sun. One summer the north bed ripens first. Two rescues are offered. One says the neighbour planted an early variety along the north side. The other says that in a wet spring the north bed ripens first. Both fit every summer she has seen. But they give different answers for summers she has not seen. In a dry spring with the early variety planted, the first rescue says the north bed ripens first, and the second says it does not.

This one story shows most of this section: two ideas offered against each other, both fitting everything seen, a summer that would decide between them, and no need for a list of every rescue anyone could invent.

### 7.2 Your decision

On 24 September you spoke to two points. On the set of all the versions of an explanation that would still fit, you said: "That assumes that the set even matters or that the creative agent can even list them. As far as I'm aware, that's not possible, even in principle. A variation is a competitor. Whether anyone can list all variations that still fit is beside the point. If two discovered variations fit, that constitutes a problem."

On whether the theory should grade explanations, you said that it "depends entirely on how error correction is handled", and then: "A record is redundant. Once the explanation is rescued, the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in." A grading scheme would have kept a record of past failures, and of the changes made to rescue the explanation after each; you judged such a record unnecessary. On 25 September you asked that this be logged, so that you would not have to correct it again.

Draft 4 writes your position into three paragraphs: "Rivals" and "Problems", in this section, and "A failed answer stays failed", in section 8. Your "variation", or "competitor", is a rival here.

### 7.3 Rivals

In general, two candidates for one question are **rivals** when two things hold. One of them has been **offered** for the question in place of the other, as each rescue is offered as the reason the north bed ripened first. And they **conflict** at some allowed setting of the target that both links carry over, inside the range or outside it. "Both links carry over" means that each candidate's link gives a setting of its own that goes with it, so that both candidates say something about it, as both rescues say something about a dry spring with the early variety.

Two candidates conflict at a setting in either of two ways.

- Their answers there differ. In a dry spring with the early variety, one rescue says the north bed ripens first and the other says it does not.
- Or their answers agree, but they differ about how the target's pieces behave there: each could match, and give the answer, under some possible behaviour of the target, but no such behaviour lets both. For instance, two explanations of a lamp might both say "lit" at some setting, while one says a certain wire carries current there and the other says it does not. Whatever the wire really does, at most one of them matches. (The theory's wording: two of their commitments tied to one anchor behave differently there.)

A candidate offered for a question claims all four requirements on the whole range, at every setting, tried or not. Outside the range it claims nothing on that question, though what it gives there can still conflict with what another candidate gives.

Not every pair of candidates is a pair of rivals.

- A candidate and a **rewriting** of it, renamed piece for piece with every connection kept and its answers left as they are, conflict nowhere. "A rainy spring" in place of "a wet spring" is no rival.
- Nor do two candidates, however differently they split the target into pieces, when some possible behaviour of the target would let both match, and give the answer, at every allowed setting both links carry over. Examples are a fuller and a leaner account of the same thing; a coarse account beside a finer one, such as Ines's salt beside a chemist's account of how salt melts ice (this document's illustration); a duplicate, the same piece stated twice; an idle addition, such as the sun god if it is idle (section 6.5); and a **blurred copy**, a less detailed version of the same account.

**What holds the universe up.** One story says an eternal dog outside the universe holds it up; another says a turtle; a third, turtles all the way down. Nothing inside the universe can touch or see the holder. *Fixed verdict:* none explains; and though the dog and the turtle differ in words, nothing anyone could see or do depends on which is true: "the difference between them is idle".

*Not yet ruled.* Dog and turtle conflict at no allowed setting, so they are not rivals. Since nothing inside the universe can touch or see the holder, no allowed change gets a different answer from the one story than from the other, and no possible behaviour of the holder tells them apart. They look like rival stories, but their difference is in words only, which is the verdict's "idle". And since no allowed change alters the answer, no group of commitments has a difference to carry, so none of the stories meets the third requirement. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

A candidate's rivals are among those someone has offered, and a candidate nobody has offered is no one's rival. No list of all rivals is supposed. As you said, as far as you know nobody can make one, even in principle.

### 7.4 What is established, and what fits

**The worn key and the diary.** Sam makes keys. His present key diagram, a drawing of how to cut a key for a lock, agrees with a worn key from an early attempt, found in his drawer. The claim at stake is when that early key was made, and so how the diagram grew. To date it, Sam wrote a diary last week, working the dates out from the stages visible in the diagram. *Fixed verdict:* "The worn key is evidence on its own. The diary is the diagram talking about itself." *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* The reasons are below; the theory gives the fixed verdict.

The theory's words for evidence, step by step, with the worn key:

- An **evidence item** is a pointer to an event, with what it is taken to show. The worn key points to an early key once cut to that shape.
- A **receipt**, in the theory's word, is a chain of reasoning built on evidence items: something a judge can hold and show, as one shows a receipt for a payment. Evidence for a claim is, by the same token, evidence against its denial. Where there is no receipt either way, the claim simply has none: missing evidence stays missing, and nothing fills the gap.
- Something written up from the claim it is meant to support is no receipt for that claim. Sam's diary was worked out from the diagram, so it is no receipt for the diagram's history. The worn key is.
- An argument is **usable** by someone when three things hold: they are entitled to use it (the theory says no more about what entitles them); it applies to the case in hand; and every **assumption** it needs is **still held**. Withdrawing an assumption takes away the entitlement, without making the conclusion false.
- **Blame is shared.** A failed prediction counts against the claim being tried together with the other assumptions behind the prediction and the instruments used, as a bundle. It does not by itself say which of them is at fault.
- A **judge** is whoever is judging a candidate: a person, a system, the project. A **result** is what looking or experiment shows about the target. It is **established** for a judge who holds a usable receipt for it.
- A candidate **fits** what is established for a judge when no result established for that judge shows it failing a requirement.

Both of the gardener's rescues fit: nothing she has established shows either failing.

### 7.5 Problems

Two rivals that both fit what a judge has established pose, for that judge, a **problem** for the question: a conflict between ideas that what is established has not settled. There are two sorts.

In a **problem inside the range**, the rivals conflict at a setting the range covers. Suppose the gardener's question covers every kind of spring and planting. Then her two rescues conflict inside its range, at a dry spring with the early variety. Whatever the target does there, at most one of them is an account, whether or not anyone can find out. Establishing what the target does there, by looking or by experiment, is a **test**: plant the early variety and wait for a dry spring. A test establishes the target's answer at that setting, and also, where the rivals' answers there agree, how the target's pieces behave. It solves the problem whatever it shows, since afterwards at most one rival fits. A rival's answer that the test refutes stays refuted on that question (section 8.3).

In a **problem outside the range**, the rivals conflict only at allowed settings outside it. Suppose instead that the gardener's question covers only the summers she has seen. Then her two rescues agree throughout the range, and conflict only at summers the question does not cover. No established answer inside the range tells against one without telling against the other. A test inside the range can refute one without the other only for a failure peculiar to that one. And where both meet the requirements, both are accounts of the question.

**The unused joint setting.** Two wiring arrangements agree on every setting that has been tried, and on almost every untried one. They differ at one setting of two switches together, which nobody has used and anyone could make by hand. *Fixed verdict:* they are two arrangements, and nothing tried so far has chosen between them; one reachable setting is enough.

*Ruled on the current text: agrees*, resting on a remark in the current text that a claim that two candidates really differ must name a change that separates them. Draft 4 narrows that remark to candidates that differ only in which piece is tied to which anchor (section 7.8). So on draft 4 the verdict rests on two other grounds (*not yet ruled*).

- The change list treats the tried settings like the trials of selection (section 9.6). Both arrangements come through every tried setting, and they differ at an untried one, so the tried settings leave that setting open: nothing tried so far has chosen between them.
- The joint setting can be reached by hand, so it is possible, so it is in the range unless a stated limit leaves it out. The case does not say that anyone offered one arrangement in place of the other. If someone did (two electricians, say, each claiming to know how the box is wired), the arrangements would be rivals that conflict at the joint setting, and both fit: a problem inside the range. Setting the switches together is a test that solves it either way: "one reachable setting is enough".

So on this view draft 4 gives the fixed verdict.

### 7.6 Easy to vary

A candidate is **easy to vary** when it and a rival pose a problem outside the range. On the question about the summers she has seen, each of the gardener's rescues is easy to vary: "a windy spring" would fit as well as "a wet spring", and nothing the question covers could tell them apart. *This document's view* of why the name fits: the candidate can be swapped for its rival, varied, and nothing on the question notices, since every answer in the range stays the same.

Is being easy to vary a fault? Not in itself, in this theory. The rival is then easy to vary too, and the words say nothing about which of them is right. Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates. What the theory does with the idea is this. A criticism that a candidate is easy to vary must supply such a rival: offering the rival is the criticism. And the cure is a finer question, on which a test can decide (section 7.7).

The theory defines only "easy to vary". It does not define "hard to vary", and it counts nothing. It does not say why it leaves "hard to vary" undefined. *This document's view* of one reason: "not easy to vary" would be true of any candidate nobody has challenged with a rival, however loose it is, so it would not pick out a merit (section 14).

**A farmer's rule carried south.** A village rule on the northern shore of the Mediterranean says: "Sow the wheat after the first autumn rains, in October, and it ripens in early summer, in June." Nobody knows why it works. A family moves to a land far south of the equator, where the cool rains come in April and May. *Fixed verdict:* the rule tells the father little he can rely on. Taken by its months it misleads; taken by its seasons it points to April or May; and it cannot say which to trust. He needs to know why it worked, "or else trial" (the case's word, in its everyday sense: trying it and seeing, which in this document's words is a test).

*Not yet ruled.* "Sow in October" and "sow after the first rains" are two ways of taking the rule. Once either is offered in place of the other, as the father must do when he chooses, they are rivals. At home they agree at every setting, and both fit generations of harvests; they conflict only in the south. So they pose a problem outside the home range, and each is easy to vary relative to the other. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict: the rule alone cannot tell him which way of taking it to trust.

**Your example.** You set "winter comes because Demeter grieves while Persephone is underground" against "winter comes because Persephone is underground", with no grief in it. On every season the Greeks knew, the two say the same. Are they rivals? Only if the world allows a change at which they come apart.

- In the grief story, the cold comes through Demeter's grief, and her grief stands for one thing in the world: how much warmth the Earth receives from the sun. In the other story, the cold comes straight from Persephone's absence.
- Now picture a change that cuts the warmth received while Persephone is above ground: a veil of dust dimming the summer sun. The grief story, whose grief is the warmth received, says it turns cold. The other story says it stays warm, since Persephone is above ground. So at that change the two conflict.
- The world allows such a change, so they are rivals. The Greeks never met it, so they conflict only outside the range of seasons the Greeks knew, and each is easy to vary relative to the other.

In a model of the world that allowed no such change, they would conflict nowhere, and would not be rivals.

Two partial explanations, each exactly right about a different step of a mechanism and both true inside the range, can still be rivals outside it. Then the finer question's test can show both wrong. The project's note on rivals of 25 September gives this only in general terms, without a worked story.

### 7.7 A finer question

If two rivals are both accounts on a range, they conflict only outside it. The reason is short. Both give the target's answer at every setting of the range, so their answers agree there. And both match the target there, so the target's own behaviour is one that lets both match. A claim that one is right and the other wrong is then a claim that some allowed change outside the range separates them, and it must name that change.

A finer range that holds a change at which the two conflict makes a different question. If both are offered for that question, and both still fit, they pose a problem inside its range, and a test can decide. On the original question nothing changes: both remain accounts there. The farmer's finer question covers sowing anywhere, the south included. A season's sowing in the south is a test that decides it: the verdict's "or else trial".

Whether a candidate is an account of a question is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world. It is not fixed by when anyone first asks the question, nor by whether anyone has checked the candidate against it.

**The tilt and the midnight sun.** An ancient astronomer proposes that the Earth's axis is tilted, to explain why the summer sun stands higher than the winter sun. It follows, though nobody works it out for centuries, that far north the summer sun never sets. *Fixed verdict:* the idea already accounted for the midnight sun (the case's words); no one, she included, had explained it before the connection was worked out.

*Not yet ruled.* The first half follows from the sentence just given: the idea's standing was fixed by the idea, the question and the world, so it already accounted for the midnight sun. The second half needs a distinction from section 10.5. An idea can be an account of a question whether or not anyone uses it; but explaining, as something a system does, needs the idea to have been used on the question, and nobody had used it on the midnight sun. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict on both halves.

A range narrowed to leave out the settings where two rivals conflict also makes a different question. It solves nothing on the first question, where the problem stands until what is established leaves at most one rival fitting.

### 7.8 Same anchors, one account

The theory's **proof on same anchors**, narrowed in draft 4, says two things. First, two candidates that both match, and both give the answer, on a range give the same answers there, since each gives the target's. Second, if in addition their commitments pair off, each pair tied to the same anchor through the same slots, then each pair is one kind, and, so far as matching and answering go, the two are one account. For instance (this document's illustration), two explanations of the shadow, one calling its piece "the light" and the other "the rays", both tied to the real sunlight through the same slots, are one account. Section 9.7 uses the proof on a harder example.

Without the same anchors, nothing more follows. Candidates that split the target differently are different candidates with the same answers. A claim that one way of tying pieces to anchors is "really" the right one must name a separating change, which makes a finer range, a different question. A blurred copy is not a rewriting (section 9.7).

The two earlier texts said more: that any two such candidates are one account, piece paired with piece. A review in September found that false as written, and the outside readers agreed. Draft 4 corrects it. The theory counts any mathematical error in this proof as a way it could be shown wrong, so by the theory's own rule the two earlier texts were shown wrong on this point; draft 4, with the corrected proof, is not (section 13.6).

### 7.9 Departures

- Deutsch judges ease of variation before any rival is offered, and would reject a bad explanation without experiment. Here it shows only when a rival is offered.
- He holds that an explanation which could easily explain anything explains nothing. Here a candidate with such a rival is still an account if it meets the requirements, and a finer question is where a test can decide between the two.
- Here "easy to vary" is relative to the question, and applies to both rivals alike. On the question of the seasons the Greeks knew, even the tilt of the Earth's axis, which he calls hard to vary, is easy to vary relative to the myth, when the two are offered in place of each other and the myth fits what is established. Does the myth fit? Section 4.7 found that it assumes its answer, which fails the third requirement. But that failure was found by examining the myth, not by looking at the world, and the theory does not say whether a failure found that way counts as established. If it does, the myth does not fit, and no problem arises. If not, the two pose a problem outside the range. Either way the theory does not prefer the myth: "easy to vary" ranks nothing, the myth is no account, and the tilt can be one. This point is open (section 14).
- Deutsch sets the Greek myth beside a Norse myth of the seasons about the god Freyr, and finds that they assert incompatible things, yet come down to one core explanation. Myths that differ only in their gods conflict at no allowed change, so here they are not rivals at all.
- His "reach", the power of an explanation to apply beyond the challenges it was made for, has no counterpart here: the word is not defined.

## 8. Narrowed questions, and why a failed answer stays failed

### 8.1 Greta's dough

Greta's rule says her dough doubles in two hours. It fails in January, and she restates it for rooms above twenty degrees, saying why: the yeast works slowly in the cold. "On days other than Tuesdays" would come with no such why. *Fixed verdict:* a legitimate narrowing; the limit follows from a piece of her explanation.

### 8.2 Limits

"For rooms above twenty degrees" is a limit: it says which possible changes Greta's range leaves out (cold rooms). A range is a stated selection from the possible changes, and a stated limit is what makes it one. An account at a stated limit answers the question at that limit. It does not answer a broader question that failed.

A **narrowing** is a limit added after a failure. It makes a different claim in a different **frame**. A frame is what a claim is relative to: its level of detail, its range, and, for a claim about a system's ability, the system's boundary and sameness rule (section 12). A frame is neither true nor false; it is chosen and stated.

What makes a limit right for the question belongs to the claim, and is open to criticism. The theory records the limit, and gives no way of certifying it as right. Meeting the requirements on the narrowed range does not certify it as right either. (The fourth requirement checks only two things here: that the target has a consistent state at the untouched setting, and that every possible change left out is left out by a stated limit.)

Where the claim states its reason for the limit, the theory judges the limit taking that reason as given: it does not certify the reason, but its verdict is a verdict given that reason. Where the claim states no reason, and a verdict turns on one, the reason is a **missing input**, and the verdict is **unsettled**. The theory never picks a reason to suit the verdict wanted.

**Back to Greta.** *Ruled on the current text: agrees*, though contested. Four of the five marks in the round were silent, one outside reader called the ruling wrong, and a fresh look at the texts upheld it, though not with full confidence (section 2.4 says how rulings are made). Greta states her reason, and it is tied to a piece of her explanation, the yeast; "not on Tuesdays" states none. Draft 4 now says outright that a verdict on a limit is given with the reason stated for it. So the theory gives the fixed verdict, given her reason. Her narrowed claim is still a different question, and the January failure stands on the first one.

A limit stated from the start is no narrowing. In **Two bakers**, Maya and Bea both explain why dough rises: yeast gives off gas, which the stretchy dough traps. Maya adds limits of 18 to 35 degrees, found by experience; Bea gives the same limits and also says why. *Fixed verdict:* both explain; Bea also explains the limits.

*Not yet ruled.* Maya's limits are an honest stated range. Bea's candidate is also an account of the further question why the rising stops outside the limits. The two conflict nowhere, so they are not rivals. That would change only if Maya's explanation said something outside her limits that conflicted with Bea's there. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

The fourth complaint says that if everything is relative to a range, nothing is objective. But the physics fixes what is possible, and within a stated range there is a fact about matching. A range that quietly leaves out changes to protect an account is caught by the fourth requirement, that every exclusion be stated, and then by any criticism that supplies the change left out. Objectivity lies in the physics and in matching. Honesty about limits lies in stating them openly.

### 8.3 A failed answer stays failed

**The forecaster who keeps finding honest limits.** Bruno's rule for when the village stream floods fails in March, and he limits it to months outside the thaw. It fails after a dam release upstream, and he limits it to days without one. It fails again, and he limits it to days with a west wind. Each limit is described in ordinary terms anyone can check, each narrowed rule has so far been right inside its limits, and he writes down every failure openly. *Fixed verdict:* "Bruno has no explanation of the floods. Each limit is honest taken alone, and the series as a whole is a retreat."

Draft 4 adds a paragraph, the theory's form of your "the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in". Take Bruno's first rule, on the question "when does the stream flood?", whose range has a setting on that day in March. On that day the rule gave an answer the stream did not give. Then any candidate that gives that same answer there is no account of that question. That holds whether it is the rule that failed, the same rule offered again, a rival, or a changed rule that keeps that answer on that day. It holds too on every question with the same target and the same thing asked whose range holds that day.

Once a judge has established what the stream did that day, this is established for that judge of every such candidate alike, from the answer each gives there. No record is needed of who failed before, or of how anything was changed.

Because blame is shared (section 7.4), the finding can **lapse**. Suppose an assumption behind the finding of what the stream did, about its background or the instruments used (a faulty flood gauge, say), stops being held. Then the finding that ruled those candidates out is no longer established, for all of them alike. None of them is thereby shown to be an account. What lapses is the showing, not the fact: the candidates may still be wrong; it is only no longer shown.

A range that leaves the failed setting out, or a change in what is asked, makes a different question, and the failure on the first question stands. **A claim keeps its frame**: it stays the claim it was, even when later thinking changes which range is in use.

**The test that fitted neither.** A remote expert sends a robot two diagnoses to choose between, and the one test that would separate them. The robot runs the test, and the result fits neither. It notices that the test assumed a pressure sensor was accurate, swaps to a second sensor, runs the test again, and finds a third fault that neither diagnosis named. *Fixed verdict:* the achievement is mostly the robot's. *Ruled on the current text: silent*, on "mostly" (section 11.6). On the test itself, *not yet ruled*: once the sensor is in doubt, the finding that ruled out both diagnoses lapses for both alike, and the rerun establishes a further result. So on the test the theory fits the case, and on "mostly" it does not reach the verdict.

**Back to Bruno.** *Ruled on the current text: silent. Draft 4 not yet ruled.* The first half of the verdict now follows. Each narrowed rule has a narrower range, so it is a different question, which does not answer when the stream floods, and the failures on that question stand: Bruno has no explanation of the floods. The second half does not follow. Nothing in the theory judges a series of narrowings. And Bruno's limits say where his rule applies, not why it fails outside them: the thaw and the dam release are where it failed, not a reason drawn from a piece of his explanation, as Greta's yeast is. So a verdict on his limits would need a stated reason for them, and he states none. So on this view draft 4 gives the first half of the fixed verdict, and does not reach the second.

So, on a fixed question, a correction sticks with no record of past narrowings, only a receipt for what the target did. That is how draft 4 renders your "the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in". It does not reach other settings: the same mistake at a neighbouring setting goes unnoticed until that setting is tried. Nor does it reach a series of narrowings, a change of level of detail, or a question that asks less exactly. The project's analysis of 24 September, on whether correction sticks, found that a record still does one job: telling a retreat like Bruno's from a limit that was honest from the start. Draft 4 does not settle whether that job is needed.

### 8.4 Fixed judging, moving questions

The tenth complaint says that holding the question fixed while judging, yet letting questions change, is having it both ways. It is, on purpose, and the two are kept apart. An assessment holds its range fixed. An episode of inquiry can replace a range with a constructed one, provided every change is recorded.

The theory's **proof that fixed judging and moving questions agree** shows these are consistent. "An account on the old range" and "not an account on the replacement range" are claims in different frames, and both can be true. **Moving the goalposts**, changing a claim's frame without saying so, is a failure to record the change, not something the theory permits.

*Departure:* both source books hold that a limit of scope needs a further explanation. Here a limit is recorded, and not certified as right.

## 9. Where links come from: selection, pictures and surprise

### 9.1 The discarded lamp controller

Each lamp controller has two switches, A and B. A sorting machine keeps a controller if, with B left off, its lamp is dark with A off and lit with A on; it discards the rest. Only two designs exist, and both can be made. The machine never tries the setting with both switches on, and the two designs behave differently there. One design also fails a setting the machine does try: its lamp stays dark with A on. It was discarded, and the other kept. Ivo reasons that since the two designs differ at the untried setting, and the machine never looks there, passing cannot tell you what a kept controller does there; he points to the discarded design as proof.

*Fixed verdict:* no. The discarded design failed at a setting the machine does try, so it says nothing about controllers that pass. Passing tells you which design you have, and that design does one definite thing with both switches on. Ivo would need two designs that both pass and still differ.

### 9.2 Carriers, contents and two layers

Sam's key diagram on paper, and the same diagram turned into raised marks that can be read by touch, are two carriers of one content. A **carrier** is a physical thing or event, at a place and time, that bears content. A **content** is what a carrier says: a set-up, together with what it commits to at the settings of its range. The diagram's content is a set-up of teeth and catches, committed to "a key cut this way turns this lock".

Carriers are not the same because they carry the same words: two copies of one page are two carriers. And contents are not the same because they give the same outputs: the parallel and priority wiring of section 6.3 give the same outputs with different supports running.

A **recoding** writes a carrier's content in another code, with the key to the code stated. It keeps the content when a reader using the key recovers every pairing of the original, such as which tooth of a key goes with which catch in the lock. In **Mirrored marks**, Sam's diagram is turned into raised marks by a reader who writes right to left, with the convention stated on the sheet. *Fixed verdict:* the content was kept, and anyone using the convention recovers every pairing. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

The theory describes a system as having at least two set-ups, called **layers**. Why two? Think of a dog watching a ball roll behind a sofa (this document's illustration). To look for it at the other end, the dog needs two things: a hold on the ball as a thing that goes on existing while hidden, and an expectation of where it will come out. The theory names a layer for each.

- In the **thing layer**, the slots are lasting things with edges and identity. The pieces are the **threads** that keep each thing the same thing from moment to moment, such as what ties where a rolling ball is now to where it was a moment ago. The allowed changes include **moving** a thing, **hiding** it, and **swapping** two things, each into the other's place. It is the layer at which there are things, rather than a blur of sensation.
- The **expectation layer** sits over it. Its pieces are dependences among things (a rolling ball keeps its speed, say), and it makes predictions: where a thing will be after a change.

The two layers are a description the theory uses to say what it would be for any system to have things and expectations. The theory does not claim that dogs, babies or any other real system has them.

To **picture** something is, roughly, for a carrier to stand for it by matching it, with a history of the right sort. The exact sense comes in section 9.4, after the three histories.

### 9.3 Three histories of a link

A link between set-ups of a physical system has exactly one of three histories, fixed by what physically happened.

- **Selected.** There is a **pool** of links to be selected from. There is **variation**, a physically possible process that makes fresh members of the pool. There are **trials**, the settings the link actually met while being shaped. And the surroundings decide what survives: a link **survives** when it is in the pool and matches at every trial. Nothing that takes part in the trials pictures the link being shaped, the trials, or what counts as surviving: selection is not aiming at anything. Which pool is meant is part of the claim: whoever says a link was selected must say from what pool. The pool is all the links that the physics and the **stated design** (how the members are made) allow, whether or not any of them was ever made.
- **Constructed.** The link is made in an episode of inquiry (section 11.4) that prepares it, as its construction evidence shows (section 10.3). In that episode the system pictures the link, or what the link leads to. Episodes of inquiry hold criticism, so a constructed link has both a pictured target and criticism in its history.
- **Declared.** Written into the model by the modeller. The theory allows this only as a convenience for writing models, and no claim of creativity may rest on it.

Selected links are to be expected at the thing layer and constructed ones above it, though this is not required. Construction can work on selected material, and selection can go on beneath it. Neither history reduces to the other: a selected link has no pictured target and no criticism in its history, and a constructed one has both. Creativity lives in construction; selection supplies its raw material.

**Dark moths.** Where smoke has blackened the tree bark, moths become mostly dark-winged over generations, as birds eat more of the pale ones and moths inherit their wing colour. *Fixed verdict:* the wing colour, inherited and put there by selection, tells the bark's colour, though no moth has any idea of it. *Not yet ruled.* The change list expects this to hold under draft 4's wording of selection, which says only that selection's history holds nothing picturing its target: selection put the wing colour into what the moths inherit, and nothing in that history pictures the bark. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

Two complaints are answered here. To the ninth, that calling a link "selected" is just laying down a label, like calling something "a cause": selection is something that physically happened (a pool, variation, trials and survival), and whether it happened can be checked, at least in principle; a label claims nothing. To the sixth, "you have replaced explanation with evolution": selection appears once, at the bottom, producing the thing layer that explanation works on, and every claim of creativity needs construction.

### 9.4 Pictures

Take the dark moths again (this document's illustration). Their inherited wing colour is a carrier. At the level of detail of "dark or pale", the set-up it embodies (dark wings where the bark is dark) has a link to the content "the bark here is dark". That link matches on the content's range, blackened bark or clean, and its history is selection. So the wing colour pictures the bark's colour.

In general, a physical thing **embodies** a set-up when, described at some level of detail, it has that set-up. A carrier **pictures** a content, at a level of detail, when the set-up it embodies at that level has a link to the content that matches on the content's range, with a history of selection or construction. So what a carrier stands for is a fact about matching with a history, not something given.

- A system can picture a false explanation: the link from carrier to content matches, while the content's link to the world fails.
- A declared link makes no picture. It makes only a modeller's claim that there is one.
- A carrier keeps its history when access to it is lost. Anything later worked out from it is no second, independent witness to that history (Sam's diary, section 7.4).
- Which set-up a physical carrier embodies is the one thing here taken from outside: the physics supplies it.

**The card nobody can read.** A forecast card's ink has faded, and the one person who could read its raised marks by touch has died. A copy made earlier gives its content. *Fixed verdict:* the marks still say what they said; the card's history is intact, and access to it is not. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

**Half from the source, half from the shelf.** A copier fills one damaged patch of a diagram from an old stored diagram that happens to agree with the source exactly there. *Fixed verdict:* the rest of the copy depends on the source; that patch's agreement is luck, and it is known which part is which. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* Whatever is filled into a carrier from another source keeps that source's history. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 9.5 Expectation, miss and surprise

**Two footbridges on opening day.** Rhea designed her footbridge from a worked-out theory of sway, calculated for crowds of the size that came. Dov copied an eighty-year-old village footbridge, not knowing it had never carried more than a few dozen people at once. Under large opening-day crowds both bridges sway badly. *Fixed verdict:* the swaying went against what both expected, but contradicted only what Rhea had reason to expect.

The theory's words for this. For a link into the expectation layer, at a setting that actually happens:

- the **expectation** is what that layer gives there;
- a **miss** is the link failing to match there;
- a **surprise** is a miss by a selected link at an **untried setting**, one not among its trials.

With no link, or with trials that cover the whole range, there can be no surprise. A constructed link that fails has a miss, not a surprise, in the theory's sense, though its owner may well be surprised in the everyday sense. A miss that the system pictures can be a noticed difficulty (section 11.4).

*Not yet ruled.* Rhea's link was constructed, and its range covered that crowd, so she has a miss that contradicts her grounds. Dov's copy is taken as a link shaped by the village bridge's eighty years of use, which serve as its trials; that is why it counts as selected, not constructed. The large crowd was a setting outside those trials, so Dov has a surprise, contradicting nothing he had grounds for. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict. The change list flags the Dov half as watched: it depends on taking Dov's link as selected, and a reader might not. *This document's view* of why it can be so taken: Dov made no join of his own; he passed the old design on, which is relay (section 10.3), and relay keeps the design's inherited history, here the eighty years of use.

A miss by a selected link can be met by **re-tuning**: adding the setting to the trials, and letting variation adjust the link within its pool. Any miss can be met by a **response by construction**: making a fresh set-up or link. For instance (this document's illustration), a weather rule of thumb, shaped by years of seasons and nudged after a bad miss to fit the new year, has been re-tuned; a forecaster who instead works out a new account of the winds has responded by construction. Only the second can originate anything (section 10.5).

### 9.6 What survival leaves open

The theory's **proof on what survival leaves open** says this. Suppose some other member of the pool also survived the trials, and behaves differently at an untried setting. Then the trials leave the selected link's value there **left open**: surviving the trials cannot choose between the two there. An untried setting alone does not show that such a survivor exists: it must be in the pool, and it must survive. Where there is none, the pool itself fixes the value.

So a selected link matches where it was tried and, wherever its pool allows an alternative, is unconstrained elsewhere. That is why the thing layer can be wrong, and why surprise can happen. The older blanket claim, that every untried value is open, is given up (section 13.6, "The forbidden wire").

Rivals are not a pool. What the trials leave open is fixed by the pool, whatever rivals anyone offers (section 7.3). So a merely imagined alternative cannot reopen what the pool has settled, as in "The forbidden wire".

**Back to the lamp controller.** *Not yet ruled.* The pool is the two designs, and the trials are the settings the machine tries. The discarded design failed a trial, so it did not survive. "Both on" is not left open: passing tells you which design you have. In the terms of section 7, the discarded design does not fit what is established, so no problem arises. Whether one could arise is a fact about the pool, not about which designs anyone points to. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict: no.

This answers the third complaint, that selection makes truth a matter of survival. Survival is how a link got there; whether it matches at changes it never met is a fact about the link and the world. The theory's **proof on surprise** adds that a system can be surprised only if it holds a selected link whose trials fall short of the possible settings. Surprise is not a feeling added to the theory. The theory calls re-tuning learning and a response by construction creating, and tells the two apart by their evidence, not their outcomes.

A natural thought is that if a programmer designed the pool, success at untried settings reflects the programmer's knowledge. The theory has no way yet to say this (section 14).

### 9.7 The hidden thing: the theory's worked example

Picture a strip of squares on a screen, like a row of lights, and two things moving along it, each at its own speed. The allowed changes are: move a thing, set its speed, cover a square so that what is under it cannot be seen, and swap the two things. What the system senses is only which squares are lit, not which thing lights them.

An expectation layer was selected on trials of moves and speed changes, but never of covering. It predicts which squares will be lit from which were lit just before. Then a thing passes under a cover and keeps moving. The layer expects nothing there, and misses when the thing comes out where its speed puts it: a surprise, since covering was never among its trials. Re-tuning fails if every member of the pool predicts from lit squares alone: no member survives the wider trials, so the fault is in how the whole pool is made, not in any one member.

Construction makes a fresh layer, with a **tracker** for each thing: a piece that carries the thing's position and speed through the time it is hidden, linked to that thing's thread. The fresh layer matches on the wider range, and by the proof that kinds add nothing, the trackers have the response pattern of things. Nobody declared them "objects" (the theory's word); they count as things because they respond as things do. If the system made the layer in a stretch of events it owns (section 12.2), with a real join of tracker to thread (section 10.3), and the layer was new to it, then it originated the layer. Section 11.7 returns to this example, as created knowledge.

Now the swap. Swapping the two things changes nothing the senses show. The theory draws a lesson from this in five steps.

1. Take a range that allows each change for both things alike.
2. Make a second link from the first by swapping which thread each tracker is tied to: tracker one is now tied to thing two's thread, and tracker two to thing one's.
3. The two things are alike, and so are their trackers, so this swap is only a renaming. The theory's **proof on rewriting** says that renaming everything piece for piece, with every connection kept, keeps matching and answers (and with them accounts, originations, repairs and created knowledge). So the second link matches wherever the first does, and gives the same answers.
4. Now pair each tracker in the first link with the tracker in the second link that is tied to the same thread. Each pair has the same anchor, so by the proof on same anchors (section 7.8) the two links give, so far as matching and answering go, one account.
5. So "tracker one is really thing one" would need a change that tells the two links apart, and on this range there is none. At this level of detail, which thing is which is nothing more than which path each follows.

The proof on rewriting does not cover **blurring** (describing with less detail), changed boundaries, or descriptions that lose track of which event is which (a summary that merges two events into one, say). *This document's gloss:* the proof matters because it is what lets the theory say that how a candidate is written makes no difference, only its structure.

The example shows that the theory's definitions can all hold at once. It is not a claim about any infant, animal or program.

**Nadia and the cards she has never seen.** Nadia learns a card game only by watching cards played. For weeks she predicts the next card from those just played, and often guesses wrong (the case says she "is often surprised"). Then she starts keeping a tally of cards "waiting somewhere", and her predictions improve sharply. *Fixed verdict:* "Nadia built something new: a part that stands for cards she has never observed. That is construction, and it is her own." *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* The tally is a response by construction. A first picture can be constructed from a challenge, without ever observing what it pictures. The change list counts her early wrong guesses as surprises in the theory's sense too, since she predicted with a link shaped by the cards she had seen and met cards outside them; but the verdict turns on construction, not on surprise. "Her own" is taken up in section 12.2. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 9.8 Departures

- Deutsch calls criticism and experiment a sort of selection; here selection has no pictured target.
- For Deutsch a problem is a situation in which conflicting ideas are experienced, and it can arise with no observation. Here that idea is split three ways. Surprise, which needs an observation at an untried setting, is kept for selected links. A problem for a question, in section 7's sense, is narrower than his: two rivals that both fit what is established. And a problem in his wider sense, a clash of ideas that can arise before any observation, can be a noticed difficulty (section 11.4).

## 10. Building something new: understanding, construction and finding questions

### 10.1 The apprentice and the third lock

On Monday an apprentice tries keys from a ring until one opens the lock. On Tuesday, at another lock, she feels where a blank catches and files it until it turns. On Wednesday, at a third lock, she picks a key from the ring at once, saying it looks like Tuesday's. *Fixed verdict:* "Monday is blind fitting. Tuesday is making something new. Wednesday is neither: she is reusing what Tuesday gave her."

### 10.2 Understanding

**The phrasebook.** After a month abroad, Kofi can order the same dishes with his phrasebook shut. Asked for a sentence that was never in the book, he has nothing to say. *Fixed verdict:* "Kofi has kept a narrow skill by his own effort. He has yet to understand the language." *Ruled on the current text: agrees.*

A system **understands** a content when three things hold. It holds a picture of the content. It uses that picture in its work on challenges. And it keeps that use, able to put the picture to work again and again (section 12.3 says exactly what "lasting" means), for a stated **use task**: a stated way of putting the content to use, by which the claim of understanding is judged. A use task is a task in the physical sense of section 12.3. For Kofi, ordering those dishes is one use task, and he keeps it. Making sentences that were never in the book would be another, and he does not have it. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

A system's **stock**, at a moment, is the contents it understands for some real use. A system may understand a false explanation. A narrow lasting use is just that: it shows neither a wider understanding, nor that the wider one can never come.

### 10.3 Construction

Take the apprentice's Tuesday. She feels where the blank catches, forms a picture of what the lock needs, and files the blank to suit it. That stretch of events is hers. It makes ready a set-up she pictures (the shape the key must have), so that she can explain why a key turns. And it holds a real **join**: a connection the system itself makes between things it pictures, here between her picture of the lock and the shape she files.

In general, **construction** is an actual **stretch** of events, with a start and a stated end, owned by the system (section 12.2). It makes ready a pictured set-up, so that the system can explain with the content. It holds a real join relevant to that use. And it is not merely a chain of passings-on that keep the content as it was.

**Construction evidence** names the processes the system controlled, the carriers that came in, the joins made and the picture that came out. **Reconstruction**, working received content out afresh, is construction. **Relay**, passing content on without making any join, is not. A small join made inside received content is construction of that join, and the rest keeps its **inherited history**.

**The parrot in the lecture hall.** A parrot repeats a professor's sentences word for word, answers questions about them with another sentence or a squawk, and would have repeated recipes as readily. A student learns the ideas by working out what the parrot's sentences mean. *Fixed verdict:* the parrot has only the sounds; in the student's learning it was a carrier. *Not yet ruled:* the parrot relays, since its responses follow wording, not content; the student reconstructs. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

**The one tooth.** A learner receives a full key diagram from Sam, finds a catch it never covered, files one tooth for it, and keeps the rest as given. *Fixed verdict:* the tooth is hers; the rest is relay. *Ruled on the current text: agrees*, on the sentence about small joins, which the current text added. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 10.4 Unspoken pictures

**The order of adjectives.** Ngozi always says "a lovely little old green French knife", never another order, even in phrases she has never heard. She cannot state any rule, and nobody taught her one. *Fixed verdict:* she knows the rule tacitly, and formed her grasp of it herself.

The theory's illustrations are like this: a pianist imagines a passage without playing it; a geometer handles a spatial relation without naming every piece; an investigator notices an inconsistency before she can say what it rests on. An **unspoken picture** is not an absent one. Picturing, understanding and construction ask for no written form. They ask only whether the distinctions and moves are there, with matching and history, and are used where use is asked.

A picture can be partial, spread out, or drawn out over time. It can be kept in memory, imagery, rehearsed action, or a notebook.

So construction evidence may pick out a join by its use. With Ngozi, for instance:

- her responses keep what the join ties together: size comes before age, whatever the words;
- she treats the same content alike in another code: the order is the same whether she speaks or writes;
- a change in content brings the change the join calls for: an added word of colour takes its place after age and before origin;
- and the use lies on a route that ran: her grasp of the order actually produced her sentences.

That is how using an objection is judged too (section 11.3). But use alone does not construct. Received content used as it was received keeps its inherited history, and passing it on without such use is relay.

*Not yet ruled.* Ngozi was given examples, never the rule, and her use of it on phrases she has never heard picks out the join. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict. (Section 14 notes how the outside readers took this passage.)

### 10.5 New, origination and finding questions

On Wednesday the apprentice picks a key that works like Tuesday's. Two contents are **equivalent**, at a level of detail, when links run both ways between them, each matching on the content's range. That is sameness of structure, not of wording or looks. A content is **new** to a system when nothing already in its stock is equivalent to it.

A system **originates** a content for a question when three things hold: it **used** the content **on the question**, the content was new to it, and it constructed the content. What is originated can be a set-up, a link, or a range with what it asks; each can be treated as a content.

**Back to the apprentice.** *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* Monday is selection: a pool (the ring), trials (each key tried), and a surviving key (the one that turns). She may have ideas about the lock, but they play no part: she tries keys in turn and the lock decides, so nothing in the process pictures the lock. Tuesday is construction, a real join made to her picture of what the lock needs, and the key is new to her, so she originates it. On Wednesday the key is equivalent to one in her stock, so she originates nothing. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

**"The seal was tight."** An investigator's notes mention in passing that the seal was tight. Later she measures the seal's compression as something that varies, and finds it matters. *Fixed verdict:* she added a dimension (the case's word for something that can vary: in this document's words, a slot), by measuring it, not by mentioning it. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* A matter mentioned in passing becomes a slot of the account only when the account allows changes to it, and adding it is construction. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

**The rota.** A team argues for weeks about who is lazy, until Noor asks which jobs have no owner (the case says "tasks", in the everyday sense); the arguing stops, and the work gets done. Nobody had said that every job should have an owner. *Fixed verdict:* "Noor found a better question, and its merit is real, even though the need it met was only recognised afterwards."

Originating a range, with what it asks, is **finding a question**. A question can itself be described as a set-up. Take Noor's question. Its slots are the settings it covers: which jobs there are, and who owns each. Its pieces settle which settings belong. And its own allowed changes add or remove a setting, or alter what is asked, as Noor altered "who is lazy?" to "which jobs have no owner?". The theory's **proof that a range is a set-up** says this can always be done. So construction and newness apply to questions as to any content, and finding one can, where the rest holds, create knowledge (section 11.7).

Finding the right question is creative on the same footing as answering one, and a description of creativity that takes questions as given cannot say so. The theory's opening Part puts it strongly: finding questions is "at least half of creativity". The phrase is a figure of speech, not a measurement: the theory measures nothing here, and the phrase only says how much weight it gives to finding questions.

*Ruled on the current text: silent*, on the merit only. If Noor constructed the range, it was new to her, and she used it, then she originated a question. Whether it was better is a claim of worth, which needs a standard of worth from outside, and the case supplies none. On draft 4 (*not yet ruled*), the last clause of the verdict, "the need it met was only recognised afterwards", is in line with the theory's episodes: the difficulty the team pictured was that the work was not getting done, and the demand her question met was stated only afterwards. So the theory gives the verdict that Noor found a question, and does not reach its merit.

That questions can be found is the fifth main claim. It would be defeated by a showing that treating a range as constructed, new and originated either cheapens creativity (for instance, by counting as creative every question a system constructs, finds new and uses, however trivial) or misses a real case of finding the right question.

*Departure:* Deutsch holds that grasping any idea, even one received from someone else, takes a guess, so that every acquisition is creative. Here working a received idea out afresh is construction, and merely passing it on is not. The likenesses to Deutsch on relay and on unspoken knowledge are named as likenesses, not claimed as borrowed.

## 11. Criticism, repair, credit and created knowledge

### 11.1 Two plumbers

Ana inspects a leak, says the washer has cracked, and explains why that lets water past. Before she can replace it, Ben tightens a nearby joint at random, and the leak stops, because the tightening shifted the washer back into place. *Fixed verdict:* "Ben's act stopped the leak. Ana's explanation is correct and produced nothing. Nobody here made a repair that came from an explanation."

### 11.2 Routes that ran

**The route that started and was stopped.** (Ana and Ben again, in a different story.) Ana's bypass and Ben's valve begin to move at the same moment. The bypass jams a quarter open; the valve closes, and the flow stops. The operating log notes it all before any meter is read. *Fixed verdict:* Ben stopped it; Ana's route started and did no stopping; the log says so without the meter. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* The point about the meter is that who stopped the flow is read from what happened, as the log shows it, not from the outcome a meter would show. So the theory gives the fixed verdict, for the reasons below.

The **course of events** is everything that actually happened, as events in an order of before and after, in which no event comes before itself. The physics says which processes took place and which connections were really made. A **route that ran** is a connected cluster of actual events with three features:

- it joins something the system pictures to an outcome;
- each part of it behaved as the physics says such parts behave (valves closed as valves do, water flowed as water does);
- along it, the outcome really depended on the pictured difference: had that difference been otherwise, among the alternatives the claim states, the outcome would have differed.

A route that ran is made of events. A support (section 6) is made of commitments, and it runs when events like these carry it out. A route that started and did no work, or had already stopped when the outcome came, did not run for that outcome. This is read from the course of events, not from the outcome.

**The diagram in his hand.** Sam holds his old key diagram while cutting a blank, but takes every measurement from the lock itself. *Fixed verdict:* the diagram did no work; had it said something different, he would have followed the lock. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* A change in the diagram would have changed nothing Sam did, so no route ran from it. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

Credit for a repair follows the routes that ran, not the supports a candidate contains (section 11.6).

### 11.3 Criticism

Three principles of the theory come first. An idea may be entertained before anything justifies it. A criticism is aimed at something, and is itself a **guess**, an idea put forward without proof. And a system may act on a judgement without first proving it.

**Two students, ten drafts each.** Jana hands each draft to her teacher, gets back a bare grade, and in the end hands in the best-graded draft. Kasia shows nobody; after each draft she writes down its faults, such as a claim with nothing to back it, and fixes them in the next. The final essays are about equally good. *Fixed verdict:* both created what is good in their essays; for Jana, the teacher chose which draft was handed in.

Take one of Kasia's notes, "this claim has nothing to back it" (this document's illustration). A **criticism** has four things:

- a **criticized item**: here, the claim in her draft (in general, a candidate, question, range, link or method);
- an **alleged fault**: that the claim is unsupported;
- **grounds**: she looked, and her draft gives no evidence for it;
- a **connection**, the argument from grounds to fault: no evidence is given, so the claim is unsupported.

A criticism **bears on** its item when its connection, taken as a candidate for the **fault question**, is an account. The fault question is: does the item have that fault, in respect of the original question (here, the essay's)? So a criticism is judged by the same four requirements as any candidate. A criticism can exist without bearing on anything.

Here is Kasia's note worked through (this document's illustration). The fault question's target is her draft. Its range adds evidence for the claim, or leaves it out. What is asked is whether the claim is supported. The connection is a small candidate with one commitment, "a claim with no evidence given is unsupported".

- Matching: add evidence to the draft, and the note's piece says "supported", as the draft now is; leave it out, and it says "unsupported", as the draft is.
- Answering: at each setting it gives the draft's answer.
- No assumed answer: delete the commitment, and the connection can no longer tell the two settings apart.
- No dodging: the draft exists, and the range says openly what it covers.

So the connection is an account of the fault question, and the note bears on the claim.

A **warning sign** is something that tells against an item. It is not a criticism until something pictures it as grounds for an alleged fault.

*Not yet ruled.* Kasia's written faults are criticisms. Jana's bare grades allege no fault, so they are at most warning signs. And the teacher, who pictures what she wants as she chooses among Jana's drafts, is not doing selection in the theory's sense (section 9.3), since selection has nothing in it that pictures its target. This keeps "the teacher chose" from being read as selection. So on this view the theory fits the verdict's point about the teacher's choice.

An **objection** is a guess that something is wrong. The theory uses the word for what a response in an episode responds to, and does not define it further, or say how it differs from a criticism. Kasia's note serves as both: it is a criticism of her draft, and the objection her next draft responds to (this document's illustration).

A response **uses an objection** when the objection shapes it in the right way and it lies on a route that ran. "In the right way" has three parts. With Kasia's note, for instance:

- each role in the objection keeps its role in the response: the claim criticized is the claim she fixes;
- the objection put in other words brings the same response: "no evidence for this" would lead to the same fix;
- a change in what the objection says brings the change that the responder's own way of deciding calls for, whatever that way is: had the note said "this claim is false", she would have cut the claim rather than backed it.

Using an objection that is not valid does not make it valid.

Keeping a criticized item in view can mean being able to bring back the distinction that matters, rather than storing every word: Kasia need not keep her old draft, only the point her note made about it.

### 11.4 Episodes and noticed difficulties

**The novelist's two demands.** Odile wants two things from every chapter: rich, lyrical prose, and a story that keeps readers turning the pages. Planning chapter five, before writing it and before anyone has read it, she sees that a long description of a valley at dawn would stall the story at its tensest point. Her first draft cuts the description to one plain sentence. Her second has the heroine flee across the valley at dawn, described through what she sees as she runs: rich and fast at once. *Fixed verdict:* she had a problem as soon as she saw the clash, before anyone read the chapter; only the second draft solves it. ("Problem" is the case's word, in its everyday sense: in this document's words, a challenge.)

Three neighbouring words need keeping apart here.

- A **challenge** is a problem in the everyday sense: anything a system is trying to solve or advance. Odile's chapter is one.
- A **problem**, in the theory's sense, is two rivals that both fit what is established (section 7.5).
- A **noticed difficulty** is defined just below: a failed demand, or a clash between demands and safeguards, that the system pictures.

The theory speaks of an episode loosely, as a stretch of inquiry from a difficulty to a response, and defines only a complete one. A **complete episode of criticism** holds four things: a noticed difficulty; an item that was there before it was criticized; an objection; and a response that depends on what the objection says.

A **noticed difficulty** is one of two things, when the system pictures it: a failed demand; or a clash in which what the system holds meets one demand only by breaking a safeguard (section 11.5 explains demands and safeguards). A problem that a system pictures can be a noticed difficulty too: for instance, when a system has stated, as a safeguard, that it keeps every rival that fits, and one of its demands could be met only by dropping one of two rivals that fit equally well.

A **creative episode** is an episode of criticism holding an origination connected to its inquiry. Closing an episode, deciding that the inquiry is over and the difficulty dealt with, is a decision, not a proof.

*Not yet ruled.* Her two stated demands clash in the chapter before anyone reads it, which is a noticed difficulty. It needs no reader and no observation. *This document's view* is that either sort fits, depending on how her demands are stated: in her plan the story's pace fails, which is a failed demand; or, if her rich prose is something she will not give up, a safeguard, then in her plan the pace could be met only by breaking it. The first draft meets one demand by breaking the other, so it is no repair. The second draft is a repair. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 11.5 Repair

**Written on the job sheet.** The job sheet for a plumbing repair states in advance that the kitchen tap runs at all times during the work. During the work the tap stops for four seconds while the mains is switched over; nobody draws from it. *Fixed verdict:* the safeguard was broken, for four seconds, and calling it incidental changes nothing. *Ruled on the current text: agrees*, because the moments it covers were stated. So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

A **contribution** is a stretch of events, together with the changes it makes to what the system holds. **Demands**, what is to be repaired, and **safeguards**, what must go on holding, are **stated inputs**: the theory records them without supplying them, and stating them claims nothing about their worth. Each is stated with the **occasions** it covers ("at all times during the work"), and a safeguard is lost exactly when it fails on one of them.

A contribution makes a **repair** when three things hold:

- some demand that failed before holds after;
- every safeguard that held before held on every occasion it covers in between, not only at the end;
- and the repair was **produced by** the contribution, meaning that a route that ran joins the two.

The theory ranks no repair above another. Losses outside the safeguards must be exposed openly.

**Four seconds.** The same stop happens, but the case names only "the protected tap". *Fixed verdict:* the safeguard held in every way that mattered. *Ruled on the current text: silent.* Whether the safeguard covers every moment, or only moments when someone draws water, is not stated, so the verdict turns on a missing input. So the theory does not reach the fixed verdict: it leaves it unsettled.

### 11.6 Credit

**Two valves in one second.** Ana and Ben each close a different valve in the same second, and either alone would have stopped the leak. *Fixed verdict:* both did something that would have stopped it; neither can claim it alone; there is no way to say whose water stopped. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict, for the reason below.

**Credit** is which contribution an achievement is attributed to. The theory credits each contribution whose route ran to the achievement, as the course of events shows. Where two contributions that were each enough both ran, both are credited. It gives no **sharing of credit** that the course of events does not contain.

Three attributions stay apart: a correct account that produced nothing (Ana, among the plumbers); an act that repaired without an account (Ben); and a repair produced through the use of an account (nobody there). *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So on "Two plumbers" the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 11.7 Created knowledge

Return to the hidden thing (section 9.7), the theory's own example; it is not a case, and has no ruling. Suppose the system originated its new layer, with its trackers, and that the layer met the demand "hold, and be able to use, an account of how things reappear after being hidden", while keeping the safeguard "predict moves correctly". Then it made a repair. And since the layer is an account on its range, and the system understands it, it created knowledge.

Among the plumbers, by contrast, nobody created knowledge: Ana holds a correct account that produced nothing, and Ben repaired without one. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So on the plumbers the theory gives the fixed verdict.

A **demand for knowledge** is a demand that a correct account be understood, or that some way of using something be corrected through such an account. **Created knowledge** needs all eight of the following. Each is shown with the hidden thing (this document's mapping).

1. A creative episode: the system noticed a difficulty (its misses at the cover) and responded with an origination.
2. A contribution in it that makes a repair: the stretch of events that made the new layer.
3. Among the demands repaired, a demand for knowledge, which failed before and holds after: "hold, and be able to use, an account of how things reappear".
4. A content the system originated, for some question, by the end of the episode: the new layer, for the question of where a hidden thing will reappear.
5. That content is an account of its question, on the range fixed when it was originated: the layer matches on the wider range.
6. It is among **what the contribution prepared**, the contents the contribution made ready by its end: the layer is one of them.
7. The system understands it afterwards: it holds the layer, uses it in predicting, and keeps that use.
8. Its join lies on the route that ran to that repair, so that the repair was **produced through the account**: the join of tracker to thread is what did the repairing.

A later narrowing of the range, made to save the account, is a different claim and does not count.

### 11.8 Worth

Noor's question stopped the arguing, and the work got done (section 10.5). Whether it was a *better* question is another matter. Repairing a demand shows that it was repaired; it does not show that the demand, or the question behind it, was worth having.

Where a claim invokes **worth**, the theory takes the **standard of worth**, one of its two foundations, from outside, and flags the place. The eighth complaint asks where beauty and art come in. The theory keeps three things apart in the artistic case:

- a work's **effect**, what it does in a context;
- its **purpose**, what it aims at;
- and its **artistic value**, taken from the standard of worth, with its **reasons of taste**, whenever artistic value is claimed.

**Achieved purpose** is defined from the first two: a work achieves its purpose when it has some effect, and every effect falls within the aim. That is strict: any effect outside the aim means the purpose, in this sense, is not achieved. None of the three is defined as another. The theory does not pretend to know which reasons of taste are true, and no view of beauty follows from achieving a stated effect. Whether Odile's rich prose is beautiful, for instance, is a claim of artistic value, which needs the standard of worth.

*Departure:* Deutsch holds that there are objective truths about beauty; the theory abstains.

## 12. Owning an ability: boundaries, tasks, barriers and the universal class

### 12.1 The technician's whisper

An assessment is stated at the start as robot-only. Midway, a technician tells the robot which gauge to read, and the robot completes the diagnosis; the log notes the instruction. *Fixed verdict:* the boundary was robot-only, and the instruction crossed it; "The diagnosis is a team result." *Ruled on the current text: agrees*, as on the old text. So the theory gives the fixed verdict: the instruction came from outside the stated boundary (section 12.2).

### 12.2 Owning

A stretch of events is the system's **own** when its processes run inside the **boundary** stated for the system, the line that says which processes and resources are the system's, and within its stated **resource terms**, which say what resources the work may draw on. (The theory names both, and does not spell out the difference further.) Work supplied from outside the boundary, such as a diagnosis, a decisive question, or an instruction about what to read, stays an **outside contribution**, however it is carried out inside.

A process run inside the boundary is the system's own, whoever wrote it. Where the boundary is drawn decides, not where the process sits in the casing. Credit for content and owning a process are different things: a routine written outside and run inside is the system's process, and its content is still its writer's contribution.

Three more cases are *ruled on the current text: agrees*, and in each the theory gives the fixed verdict.

- In **The routine uploaded that morning**, a robot compares pressures using a routine a remote person uploaded that morning. The comparison is the robot's work; who wrote the routine is a separate fact.
- In **The module in the casing**, a bought module sits inside a controller's shell but outside its stated boundary. It is not the controller's.
- In **The robot's log and the maker's manual**, the log shows which routine, running inside the robot, produced today's diagnosis, and the manual says the routine is the robot's own "because the robot can diagnose". Owning rests on the log, which shows the routine running inside. It never rests on the ability being attributed, as the manual's sentence would have it.

An ability is attributed under a stated boundary and a stated **sameness rule**, what makes the system the same system through change. Both are stated before the attribution, not chosen after it. In **New board, old memory**, a robot's controller board is replaced partway through its work, and its memory and routines, stated at the start as its sameness rule, are copied across. *Fixed verdict:* the same robot finished. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

Where the boundary is not stated outright, two sorts of statement are told apart.

- A statement that names the system and says whether a process runs inside it or outside it states the boundary for that process. "The robot compares the pressures itself" says the comparison runs in the robot.
- A statement that a process is the system's own, without saying where it runs, is the attribution itself, not the boundary. "The routine is the robot's own" is one.

Nadia's case (section 9.7) states no boundary outright. But "she starts keeping a tally" names her and places the process in her, which under draft 4 states the boundary for that process (*not yet ruled*). So on draft 4 "her own" rests on stated words, and the theory gives the fixed verdict. On the current text, the project's readers in the round had noted a doubt there, since the ruling read "her own" in the ordinary sense.

### 12.3 Tasks, and what is possible

Take a kettle (this document's illustration). Boiling water is a task: a physical thing, the water, goes from a starting property, cold, to an end property, hot, using stated resources (electricity), with stated side effects (warm air). The kettle is the doer. Switching it on and waiting is the procedure. One boil is a carrying-out. The kettle is ready when it is plugged in, holds water and is switched off; after each boil it switches itself off and is ready again.

The theory states its physics in terms of tasks. (The sources note adds that this way of stating physics, by which changes can and cannot be brought about, comes from Deutsch's 2013 paper "Constructor Theory"; the paper is not one of your two source books.)

- A **physical thing** has **properties**, each a set of its states: "hot" is the set of all its hot states.
- A **task** changes a physical thing from a stated starting property to a stated end property, with its resources and side effects stated.
- A task is **possible** when no law of physics puts a limit, short of perfection, on how well it can be done and how reliably the doer can be kept ready: the laws allow it to be done as well as one likes. This is the one meaning of "possible" here, allowed by the laws of physics. It follows that one success does not make a task possible. One success shows the task done once, to some degree, not that it can be done as well as one likes, again and again.
- A **doer** performs a task by a stated **procedure**, and is left able to do it again. A **carrying-out** is one run of the procedure.
- In a **lasting performance**, every carrying-out finishes with a right end property and leaves the doer ready again. That holds from any start where the doer is ready, for anything within the task's starting property, and with the stated **aids**, the help given for the task (for the kettle, its power supply). A kettle that never switches off is **getting stuck**, and getting stuck is not success.
- **Staying able** is the widest set of ready states from which every carrying-out finishes and brings the doer back into that same set. For the kettle, it is every state in which it is plugged in, holds water, is switched off, and is in good enough order for a boil to finish. The theory's **proof on staying able** shows that such a widest set always exists.

An **ability** is an owned lasting performance, or an owned and possible way of coming to have one in a finite number of steps, under the same sameness rule and resource terms. A modeller's description of a procedure is not the system's having it.

A system is **able to advance** a challenge when, from each starting point in a set stated in advance, every carrying-out ends with the challenge advanced and the system still in shape to go on. A first discovery cannot be repeated: Nadia's first idea of hidden cards can happen only once. So what must last is the set-up that lets the system go on inquiring, not the discovery.

The physics also supplies **degrees of accuracy**, ever more demanding and short of perfect, for how exactly a task is done and how reliably the doer is kept ready. At each degree, what a system can do stays within what the physics lets be done at that degree. A task that a system can do at every degree is possible. But an ability at some degree does not make a task possible. For instance (this document's illustration), take the task of cutting a sheet exactly along a line. A machine might cut to within a millimetre of the line. If the laws of physics forbid ever cutting closer than some finer limit, the task is not possible in the theory's sense, though the machine does it well to within a millimetre: it can be done well, but not as well as one likes.

### 12.4 Criticism at any depth

Kasia criticizes her draft. She could also criticize the way she criticizes, say her habit of looking only for missing evidence, and then criticize that criticism in turn. A principle of the theory: any aspect of a system's practice (a question, a range, a link, a method, what it attends to) can be criticized, with an outcome able to change how the system goes on.

An aspect is **reviewable** when the system has some way of carrying on, possible and owned by it, in which the aspect becomes something the system pictures, is criticized, and can change how it is used. For Kasia, her habit of looking only for missing evidence is reviewable if she has some way of going on, of her own, in which the habit becomes something she pictures and criticizes, and which can change how she criticizes. Ranges and links are among the reviewable aspects. A system has **criticism at any depth** when, for every finite chain of such items (a criticism of a criticism of a method, and so on), it has such a way to carry on, owned by it.

### 12.5 Barriers and the universal class

**Two sealed sorts of matter.** A universe has two sorts of matter that pass through each other without effect: nothing made of one sort can push, see, signal to or record anything made of the other. Each sort's people have built a computer for any calculation their sort allows, and some calculations are possible only in one sort. Asha says the two computers together make a machine for any calculation the laws allow; Bram says none can exist, since a question written in one sort can never be read by the other's computer. *Fixed verdict:* Bram is right, and his reason largely explains why, together with the stated fact that each sort allows calculations the other cannot.

An **aid** is help a system may be given in doing a task (section 12.3). A **fair aid** is help that the physics allows and that does not assume what is to be shown. For instance (this document's illustration), giving a student a textbook is a fair aid for learning to solve equations; giving her the answers is not, if the claim is that she can solve them.

A **barrier** is a field of tasks, described in its own terms rather than by a list of anyone's failures, for which every fair aid leaves the ability unavailable. For a system made of one sort of matter in the case above, the calculations only the other sort allows are such a field. A finite list of failures proves no barrier. And a narrow lasting use, like Kofi's phrasebook (section 10.2), shows no lasting inability to go further. A **way round** refutes a proposed barrier.

A principle of the theory is that any sort of matter may carry any set-up, and that every claim that a physical thing has a set-up must agree with the physics. Draft 4 adds a limit. This holds only so far as the physics lets contents pass between sorts of matter. Where two sorts cannot exchange what they carry, what only one sort can carry is a barrier for a system made of the other.

*Not yet ruled.* For a system of either sort, the calculations only the other sort allows are a barrier, so no system there is in the universal class (below). Bram's reason explains this, together with the stated fact that each sort allows calculations the other cannot. Asha's claim, as the case states it, that the two computers together make one machine for any calculation, is wrong for that reason. Taken more weakly, as saying only that every calculation can be done by some machine somewhere, it is true, but it answers a different question, so taken that way she and Bram are not rivals. So on this view the theory gives the fixed verdict: Bram is right.

The **universal class** is the set of systems, each described with its boundary and sameness rule, that have three things. They have criticism at any depth. From their starting state, with some fair aid, they can come to understand every explanatory content, by gaining the ability to do its use task. And with some fair aid they can advance every **well-posed challenge**, one stated clearly enough for progress on it to be judged. The collection of all explanatory contents, and the collection of all well-posed challenges, are both fixed in advance, without reference to the system being judged.

On *this document's view*, nobody has to write these collections out as lists; the theory needs only that they are fixed in advance. How they are fixed, the theory does not say (section 19). Whether that sits well with your view that nobody can list all the versions of an explanation is open.

Criticism at any depth does not bring membership. A growing history does not certify it, and a finite run of successes does not establish it. The theory shows no person, machine, institution or line of descent to belong.

*Likenesses and departures:* draft 4's limit on what sorts of matter can carry is like Marletto's principle of exchange between sorts of matter, a likeness named, not claimed as borrowed. Deutsch holds that people are universal explainers; the theory abstains. Marletto defines knowledge as information that can keep itself embodied in physical things; the theory leaves that idea out of its classes, and if it matters, it belongs to the physics, which the theory takes from outside.

## 13. What the theory rests on, and how it could be refuted

### 13.1 The uncited textbook

An explanation builds up the ideas it uses, step by step, from a starting base. It justifies every idea that way except one, which it justifies by a later theorem; but the proof of that theorem, as the explanation gives it, uses that same idea. A separate proof of the theorem exists in a textbook the explanation does not cite. *Fixed verdict:* as written, the order has a circle in it; the textbook proof would break it, and has not been used; the order is not settled until it is. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict, for the reason in section 13.3.

### 13.2 Foundations, frames and stated inputs

Recall "Four seconds" (section 11.5). Whether the safeguard was kept turns on which moments it covers, and the case does not say. The theory does not fill that in; it leaves the verdict unsettled. That is how the theory treats what a claim takes as given.

The theory has two **foundations**.

- **The physics**: whatever physical theory is adopted. It says what states and properties physical things have, which processes are possible, which processes count as a system's own controlled actions (construction evidence names them), what resources are, the degrees of accuracy, and which set-up a physical carrier embodies at a level of detail.
- **The standard of worth**. It is used only where a question invokes worth, is taken as given, and is never derived.

Every claim also has a frame and may have stated inputs, and the two are different. A **frame** is what a claim is relative to: its level of detail, its range, and, for an ability, the system's boundary and sameness rule (section 8.2). It is neither true nor false. A **stated input** is a fact the claim relies on and must state; the theory records it and does not supply it. The stated inputs are:

- a repair's demands and safeguards, with their occasions;
- a range's limit, and what makes the limit right;
- an attribution's boundary and sameness rule;
- a weighting of credit beyond what the course of events contains.

Some things are both. The robot's boundary is part of the frame of "the robot can diagnose", and it must also be stated. The same holds for a sameness rule and for a range's limit. The occasions a safeguard covers, as in "Four seconds", are a stated input but not part of a frame.

A verdict that depends on a stated input, or on the standard of worth, is given with it. Where the stated input is missing, the verdict is unsettled, and the theory says so rather than choosing the input to suit the verdict wanted. No probability of truth, merit score or ranking of thinkers is supplied, and a claim that needs one is unsettled.

### 13.3 Everything else is defined from these

Roles come from allowed changes; kinds from response patterns; pictures from matching and history; histories from what physically happened; accounts from matching under change; and from these, everything else, up to criticism at any depth and the universal class. Nothing rests on a basic term meaning "really explains", "is a cause" or "is knowledge". The theory's **proof that nothing rests on "really explains"** follows each definition down to confirm this. The foundations are a theory of matter (the physics) and, where needed, a standard of worth; neither is about explanation.

The **order of definitions** says which definitions rest on which.

- Frames and stated inputs rest on nothing in the theory; they are stated, not derived.
- Kinds rest on set-ups and ranges; accounts on matching; a criticism's bearing on accounts.
- Pictures rest on matching and physical history; understanding on pictures and lasting performance.
- Owning rests on the course of events and a boundary; an ability on owning, lasting performance and a sameness rule.
- Construction rests on owning and accounts; origination on understanding and construction.
- Repair rests on origination, accounts, understanding, routes that ran, and the stated demands and safeguards with their occasions; created knowledge on all of those and on repair.
- Criticism at any depth and the universal class rest on all of the above.

The order has **no circles**. A picture justified only by the construction that made it, or an owning and an ability justified only by each other, has no place in it. A separate proof counts only once the explanation uses it, as with the uncited textbook.

Pictures and construction can look like a circle: a constructed picture rests on construction, and construction makes ready a pictured set-up. *This document's view* is that the order runs through time. A construction uses pictures the system already had, which came from earlier constructions or, at the bottom, from selection, which needs no picture at all. What the theory forbids is a picture justified only by the very construction that made it.

**Owned means capable.** A routine is defined as the robot's own when it can run it, and runnable when it is its own. *Fixed verdict:* the definition goes in a circle, and only the histories of what happened give the distinction meaning. *Ruled on the current text: agrees.* So the theory gives the fixed verdict.

### 13.4 Classes

The theory's definitions leave places to be filled for any particular system: its set-ups, links, histories, boundary, and so on. A **filled-in model** is those places filled in for a real or imagined system. A filled-in model of the apprentice, for instance, would say what her set-ups are, which of her links were selected and which constructed, and where her boundary lies (this document's illustration). A **class** is a set of filled-in models that meet stated conditions. The theory's classes are these.

- The **base class**: every open place filled with something of the right sort (a set-up where a set-up is needed, a history where a history is needed), and every physical claim in agreement with the physics.
- The **creative-episode class**: with an origination connected to an episode of criticism.
- The **knowledge-creation class**: with created knowledge.
- The **criticism-at-any-depth class**.
- The **universal class** (section 12.5).

The creative-episode class lies inside the base class, and the knowledge-creation class inside the creative-episode class. The universal class lies inside the criticism-at-any-depth class. The others are not ordered. A single origination does not put the system that made it in the universal class.

The classes are how the theory states its conclusions about a system. To say that a system, with its stated boundary, is in the creative-episode class is to say that its course of events holds a creative episode; to put it in the universal class is the strongest claim the theory can make. The theory defines the classes, and does not claim that any real person or machine belongs to any of them.

### 13.5 How the theory could be refuted

The five **main claims**, in order of how much would fall, each with what would **defeat** it. None is protected by the theory's notation, or by the name of any draft.

1. **The four requirements are enough.** Defeated by a candidate that meets all four on a range of possible changes, with a link that is not merely declared, and plainly explains nothing. "Not merely declared" means a link that selection shaped or that some system constructed, not one simply written in by the modeller; section 4 did not need a link's history, but this claim does, so that, on *this document's view*, nobody can defeat it with a link merely made up. The known tricks each fail one requirement (section 4.4). A full response table passes matching; like any other attempt, it would defeat this claim only if it met all four and still explained nothing. An outside reader tried to build such a candidate, and offered none (section 14 notes one boundary case).
2. **They are needed.** Defeated by a real explanation whose set-up no link can match on any range of possible changes. Explanations of absence are the most exposed place (section 4.6).
3. **Kinds can be dropped.** Defeated by a label that tells apart two accounts which no allowed change tells apart, where the difference does real explaining (section 5.4).
4. **The two histories, selection and construction, are kept apart.** (The third history, declaration, supports no claim of creativity.) Defeated by any of three things.
   - A selected link whose value at an untried setting is fixed by its trials, although its pool holds a survivor that differs there. The proof on what survival leaves open says this cannot happen, so finding one would show that proof wrong. (A pool with no such survivor is a condition the proof itself states, not a refutation.)
   - A showing that every construction can be rewritten as a selection without loss.
   - A showing that explanations do not in fact work on a layer of lasting things, as the theory says they do.
5. **Questions can be found.** Defeated by a showing that treating a range as constructed, new and originated cheapens creativity, or misses a real case of finding the right question (section 10.5).

A mathematical error also counts. That means a counterexample, under their stated assumptions, to one of eight proofs: the proof on smallest supports (section 6.2), the proof on pinning down (section 3.5), the proof on blocking (section 3.5), the proof on error build-up (section 5.6), the proof on staying able (section 12.3), the proof that kinds add nothing (section 5.4), the proof on same anchors (section 7.8), and the proof on what survival leaves open (section 9.6). The theory does not say why it lists these eight and not its other proofs. Section 14 notes that this lets a gap found in the proof that a range is a set-up go uncounted.

A case whose verdict turns on one of the stated inputs listed in section 13.2, or on the standard of worth, when the case does not state it, has a missing input, and is not a refutation.

### 13.6 Results so far

**The forbidden wire.** The case gives no more setting than this: a stated pool of devices, some settings that were tried, and one that was not, as with the lamp controller of section 9.1. The only arrangement that would respond differently at the untried setting needs a wire between two terminals, and every device in the stated pool is made without that wire. *Fixed verdict:* "Then there is no alternative in that population. The different response is imaginable and is not available." *Ruled on the old text: disagrees; on the current text: agrees.*

The old text's proof on what survival leaves open said that the trials always leave every untried setting open. This case refuted that, and the theory's list of defeats counted it. The current text corrected the proof by requiring a differing survivor in the pool, and draft 4 keeps that, adding that rivals are not a pool, so a merely imagined arrangement reopens nothing. So the theory now gives the fixed verdict. The cost: the corrected claim is now close to true by definition. It says that where another survivor differs, the value is left open, which is nearly what "left open" means. So it says less than it did, and is hard to defeat. The theory does not say how much that matters.

Three more defects, found in September, are corrected in draft 4.

- The old proof on same anchors was false as written, so by the theory's own rule the two earlier texts were shown wrong on it (section 7.8).
- The proof on error build-up lacked assumptions it needs (section 5.6).
- "No assumed answer" left open which pieces are commitments (section 4.2).

On the cases, the current text agreed with 46 of the 52 fixed verdicts, was silent on 6, and went against none. On two cases it came closer to the fixed verdict than the old text had: "The spring nobody mentioned" and "The forbidden wire".

### 13.7 Three older views

The fifth complaint says the theory is three older views renamed. The first holds that what a sign stands for comes from a history of selection. The second, that science captures the structure of the world. The third, that a mind is defined by what it does, not by what it is made of.

The theory takes from each and differs from each. Links have a history, but selected and constructed histories are kept apart, with creativity only in construction. Structure is kept, but piece by piece and under change, and the theory does not require an explanation to copy the world's whole structure at once. Any sort of matter may carry a set-up, but every claim that a physical thing has one must agree with the physics. Whether the mix is new is a question for scholars.

- Whether a failure found by examining a candidate alone, rather than by looking at the world, counts as established is not said. Whether the myth about winter, whose gods stand for nothing in the world, and the tilt of the Earth's axis pose a problem turns on it (section 7.9).
- If a programmer designed a pool, success at untried settings seems to reflect the programmer's knowledge, which the theory cannot yet say (section 9.6).
- "Any piece that gives an input slot its value" (section 4.2) can be taken two ways: either every such piece is background, never a commitment; or such a piece is background unless the candidate offers it as a commitment. The known verdicts are the same either way.

**How the outside readers took three passages.** On an earlier draft, one outside reader (of two) said that the text's reasoning on three cases was a move toward the fixed verdict, that is, that it made the theory likelier to give the careful person's verdict: "A myth about winter" (section 4.7), "The seasons and the sun god" taken the first way (section 6.5), and "The order of adjectives" (section 10.4). On the order of adjectives, the other reader called the passage faulty. The project kept the text in all three.

**A boundary case for the first main claim.** An outside reader, trying to build a candidate that meets all four requirements and explains nothing, noted that a candidate which is simply a copy of the target, linked to it piece for piece, passes all four requirements trivially. The reader did not offer it as a failure, since such a copy arguably explains trivially rather than not at all. The project has not ruled on it.

**Main claims no case yet aims at.**

- That kinds can be dropped (section 5.4). An outside reader tried to break this claim and could not.
- That absence is handled rightly (section 4.6).
- That construction cannot be rewritten as selection, and that explanation works on the thing layer (section 9).
- On finding questions (section 10.5), no case yet aims at whether treating a range as constructed, new and originated cheapens creativity; counting every constructed, new and used question as creative may do so. An outside reader called the proof that a range is a set-up "a sketch, not a proof". The project agreed that a step is missing, and counted no defeat, since the list of defeats names only the eight proofs listed in section 13.5. That list is the theory's own, and it does not say why these eight.

**The proof on what survival leaves open**, with its pool requirement, is now close to true by definition, and hard to defeat (section 13.6).

**Words the theory leaves undefined, or unplaced.** A short list is in section 19, for checking against the theory.

**For the owner: a slip in draft 4's text.** In the theory's worked example of the pole, one sentence says "the calculation's height" where it should say "the calculation's shadow": raising the pole changes the world's shadow but not the calculation's shadow, as the theory's other statement of the same point says. ## 15. What draft 4 changed

Draft 4 is the current text with 57 changes (section 19 gives the counts). The main ones, one line each, by the section of this document they touch.

**Questions (section 3).** Only one change touches the theory's text on questions: a verdict on a limit is given with the reason stated for it (section 8).

**The four requirements (section 4).**

- The commitments are named: the pieces offered as doing the work, whether or not anyone has described their work. The rest, including any piece that gives an input slot its value, is background.
- The second half of "no assumed answer" now asks that deleting a group of commitments lose a difference between two settings. The current text asked instead for a change in the range that itself removes commitments; taken one way, it found no account where the fixed verdict finds one, as with the salt on the step.
- Whether a statement is the answer written in is judged by its form at the stated level of detail, not by treating all mathematically equivalent truths as one.
- A full response table passes matching, and is an account only if it meets the other three requirements. The current text said outright that it is an account.
- On absence: why the absent thing seems present is a separate question, and a bare denial is no account of it.
- The principle on errors now says that "explanation" there means an account, and that a false explanation offered for a question is a candidate.

**Kinds (section 5).**

- It says how a piece of one candidate is compared with a piece of another.
- Response patterns are built from behaviour, not from values, and a change that sets a slot replaces only the piece that fixes it.
- The proof on error build-up now states the assumptions it needs; the current text left them out, a drafting slip.

**Which pieces do the work (section 6).**

- The current text compared changed forms of an account by how many "explanatory jobs" they still did (the jobs were never defined), and used the word "reach" without defining it. Draft 4 drops both, so nothing counts, grades or ranks candidates.
- The idle commitment is added, with its definition applied only to a candidate that has a support.
- A cut-down candidate's commitments are the ones it keeps, and the supports judged are groups of the written commitments.
- A support is told apart from a route that ran, and credit follows routes that ran.

**Rivals and problems (section 7).**

- The two paragraphs "Rivals" and "Problems" replace the current text's paragraph on hard to vary, which was the job-counting comparison just described.
- The proof on same anchors is narrowed: it now needs the same anchors.

**Narrowed questions (section 8).**

- Meeting the requirements on a narrowed range does not certify the limit as right.
- A verdict on a limit comes with the reason stated for it; where none is stated, the verdict lacks a stated input.
- The paragraph "A failed answer stays failed" is new.
- The list of defeats now says that a case with a missing stated input is no refutation.

**Where links come from (section 9).**

- Surviving the trials is defined: being in the pool and matching at every trial.
- Expectation and miss now cover every link into the expectation layer, whatever its history. The current text gave a failed constructed link no miss.
- The pool is all the links the physics and the stated design allow, not only ones that were made.
- The swap in the hidden thing now runs through the proof on rewriting and the narrowed proof on same anchors.
- The theory's opening Part no longer calls selection "blind". It says only that selection's history holds no pictured target, as the body of the theory does.

**Building something new (section 10).**

- Unspoken pictures, and joins picked out by their use, are added, with the guard that use alone does not construct.
- "Equivalent" is defined.

**Criticism, repair and knowledge (section 11).**

- The fault question and a criticism's connection are spelled out, and so is when a warning sign becomes a criticism.
- A noticed difficulty and a contribution are defined.
- A safeguard must hold on every occasion it covers, not only at the end.
- "What the contribution prepared" and "produced through the account" are defined.
- The standard of worth is a foundation; the current text had called it a stated input.

**Owning an ability (section 12).**

- The current text said a process run inside the boundary is the system's own "today". Draft 4 drops "today", so that the rule agrees with the wording of the theory's Part on the physics; the change list gives no other reason for the word.
- Credit for content is told apart from owning a process.
- It says how a boundary is stated when it is not stated outright.
- Degrees of accuracy and fair aid are defined.
- The principle about carriers is limited to what can pass between sorts of matter.

**What the theory rests on (section 13).**

- Everything else is defined from the two foundations, the frames and the stated inputs; the current text said only that everything else is defined.
- Frames and stated inputs are told apart.
- A weighting of credit joins the stated inputs.
- A missing standard of worth leaves a verdict unsettled, as does a claim that needs a probability of truth, a merit score or a ranking of thinkers.
- The order of definitions now places owning, abilities and repair.
- A case with a missing stated input is no refutation.
- The full response table is no longer set outside the list of defeats: like any candidate, it would defeat the first main claim if it met all four requirements and still explained nothing.

## 18. The mathematics, for readers who want it

**Three further rules about set-ups (section 3).** Values need not be numbers: they may be paths, proofs or histories. Pieces may depend on one another in a loop. And where several states are consistent, the theory keeps them all rather than picking one.

**Pinning down, for sums (section 3).** Suppose each reading, and the feature asked about, is a sum of the unknowns, each unknown multiplied by a fixed number. Then the feature is pinned down exactly when every shift of the unknowns that leaves all the readings unchanged also leaves the feature unchanged. For the two balances, the shift "one gram more mass, one gram less error on each balance" leaves both readings unchanged and changes the mass, so the mass is not pinned down. The same shift leaves the difference of the two errors unchanged, and every shift that leaves both readings alone is a multiple of it, so that difference is pinned down. Repeating a weighing adds a reading with the same multipliers, so it rules out no shift. A calibration, against something already known, can rule one out.

**Odd-sized grids (section 4).** Take square grids of numbers with an odd number of rows, where each number is the negative of its mirror image across the diagonal. The question is why none of them can be undone. The range drops the mirroring, drops the oddness, or drops both. It holds fixed ordinary arithmetic, and the tie between being undone and the grid's **determinant**, one number worked out from the grid that is nought exactly when the grid cannot be undone.

- The full working-out of the determinant, with the mirroring put in, matches: each in-between step of the working-out is a smaller piece of the same calculation, and it matches once the numbers it uses but does not report are set aside.
- It gives the answer: in the family of grids asked about, none can be undone, and under each dropped assumption the family holds one that can.
- It assumes none. Without the mirroring, the three-row grid with ones down the diagonal and noughts elsewhere can be undone. Without the oddness, the two-row mirrored grid with a one above the diagonal and a minus one below it can be undone.
- It does not dodge: a mirrored grid of odd size that is not all noughts exists, so the untouched setting has a consistent state.

So it is an account. A three-line proof is shorter, but shortness is not a fifth requirement.

**Matching in one direction or both (section 5).** Treating two set-ups as equivalent needs matching both ways: each side can always follow the other's changes. Matching one way, the candidate following the target, is enough for a simplified copy. Two links chained one after the other keep both directions when each covers what the next needs.

**Error build-up (section 5).** Choose a way of measuring the distance between states. Suppose that at every state within stated bounds, one step of the candidate lands at most a step error away from where one step of the target lands, carried over. And suppose one step of the candidate never makes the distance between two states more than a fixed number of times larger: that number is the widening factor.

Then, from one shared start, as long as the target's states stay within the bounds, the gap after a number of steps is at most this: the step error once for the latest step, plus the step error times the widening factor for the step before, plus the step error times the widening factor times the widening factor for the one before that, and so on back to the first step. With a widening factor of one, the ceiling is simply the step error times the number of steps. If there is no fixed widening factor, no ceiling follows.

**Smallest supports (section 6).** Suppose three things: the commitments are finitely many; adding a commitment to a support always leaves a support; and the whole candidate is a support. Then a single commitment is load-bearing in some support exactly when it belongs to some smallest support, and it is needed in every support exactly when it belongs to every smallest support. Where adding can break a support (interference), the second assumption fails, and the rule does not apply.

**Spread-out support (section 6).** Take endlessly many commitments: the first says a quantity lies within one of nought, the next within a half of nought, the next within a third, and so on. Any endless selection of them puts the quantity at exactly nought; no finite selection does. So no single commitment and no smallest group does the work; the whole group does, together. For the same reason, with endlessly many commitments, commitments that are each idle can together be load-bearing.

**Staying able (section 12).** Call a set of the doer's states "safe" when every carrying-out from a state in it finishes and returns the doer to a state in it. Put all the safe sets together, and the result is again safe. So there is always a widest safe set, and that is staying able.
