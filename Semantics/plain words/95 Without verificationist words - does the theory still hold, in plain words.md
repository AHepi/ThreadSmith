## 1. The short answer

**Yes, with repairs, and on one condition that is yours to decide.**

What this means for the idea: the theory never needed a word saying that an explanation had earned something by surviving. Wherever it said an explanation "fits", it only ever used the other side of it: nothing has ruled the explanation out yet. So all of that can be said with just two phrases. An explanation is **ruled out** for someone, or it is **not ruled out**, and it gains nothing from not being ruled out. Everything the theory does still works in those words: what an explanation is, rivals, problems, tests, "easy to vary", and a failed answer staying failed.

The condition is in section 5. One thing in the theory involves no person at all. If you count it as the kind of absolute you want removed, the theory as built does not hold.

## 2. One sentence, before and after

First, three words the sentence needs.

- A **candidate** is anything someone offers as an explanation for a question.
- The **four requirements** are the theory's test of whether a candidate explains its question: it matches the world piece by piece, it gives the answer to the question asked, it does not assume that answer, and it does not dodge the question. A candidate that meets all four is an **account**.
- An **assessor** is whoever is looking at a candidate: a person, a system, or the project. Files 92 to 94 called this the "judge". This file uses the new text's word, because a judge's word is final and nothing in the new text is.

Before, in draft 5 (square brackets mark where the theory's shorthand is put in plain words):

> A candidate **fits** what is established for an assessor when no result established for that assessor shows it failing [one of the four requirements].

After, in the scrubbed text:

> A candidate is **ruled out** for an assessor when an argument usable by that assessor rules out that the candidate meets [the four requirements] … A candidate is **not ruled out** for that assessor when no argument usable by that assessor rules it out.

The old sentence gave the assessor a positive holding, "what is established", and the candidate "fits" when that holding says nothing against it. The new sentence has no positive holding at all. An argument rules the candidate out, for someone, for as long as that person can still use the argument, and without such an argument the candidate is simply not ruled out. Both sentences pick out the same candidates. What is lost is only the suggestion that surviving earns something.

"Argument" is now used only in your sense. The new text opens with your words: "reasons why this and not that", "something that can be strung together into a coherent structure to decide why this and not another". Next to it stands the one other word you asked about. To **tentatively accept** a claim is a person's choice to go on with it, "for whatever reason", as you put it on 25 September. It can always be dropped, and the theory never defines it by the arguments someone has.

A second example. Before: "Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof." After: "Closing an episode is a choice, not an argument." (An episode is one round of a difficulty, a criticism and a response.) One reader noticed that this could be read as saying arguments play no part in closing, and proposed: "an argument can rule out some ways of closing it, but no argument makes the choice."

## 4. What still works

Take the gardener from file 92. Her son says the neighbour planted an early variety along the north side, and her sister says the north bed ripens first in a wet spring. Before, the theory said both "fit" what she has seen. Now it says that neither is ruled out for her. They are still **rivals**: one is offered in place of the other, and the two give different answers somewhere. They still make a **problem** for her, which in the theory means two rivals, neither ruled out for the assessor. Suppose a test can be made where they give different answers. Then an argument from what that test records rules out at least one of them for her, for as long as she can still use that argument. That works because, where two candidates give different answers, at most one of them can match the world, whether or not anyone looks.

"A failed answer stays failed" still works at two levels. At the level of the world, a candidate whose answer differs from the world's somewhere in its question is not an account, and no one has to record that. At the level of the assessor, the candidate stays ruled out only while the argument that rules it out can still be used. If that argument lapses, no candidate becomes an account because of it.

The ten numbered arguments go through with every step as it was. Each is now headed "Why this and not its denial", after your words. Of the 20 cases read again, 19 come out as before. The twentieth is in section 6.

## 5. The condition: this one is yours

Take one candidate and one question. Whether the candidate meets the four requirements on that question depends on three things only: the candidate, the question and the world. No person appears in it. The scrub removed the old words for this ("true", "fact"), but the idea is still there, and the theory needs it. It is what makes a test able to separate two rivals: where they give different answers, at most one can be an account.

Your statement on choosing says of science: "It must match reality, but not by some fixed infallible metric." Claude reads that as keeping this idea. The relation is to the world, and no one has a way of reading it off that cannot be in error, since every assessor reaches it only through arguments that can lapse. That reading is Claude's, not yours.

If you count this idea as one of the absolutes your instruction removes, the theory as built does not hold. "Account" would mean only what someone tentatively accepts. A test could no longer decide between two rivals, and the world's half of "a failed answer stays failed" would go.

## 6. What changed beyond the words

Some things could not be said in the new words without changing what the theory claims.

- **Your open question from file 94 is now answered, without your say.** The new definition lets an argument's starting points be stated assumptions and definitions as well as records of what happened, so that the theory's ten numbered arguments count as arguments. With that, someone who sees by argument alone that the myth about winter assumes its own answer has an argument that rules the myth out for them. Draft 5 left this open, and it is your question. Your own definition of argument says nothing about records, and your statement bears on it only for questions that are not scientific. The report proposes marking the place as provisional until you answer. A related gap: as written, someone could rule a candidate out just by assuming the opposite of what it says. One sentence closes that.
- **"Knowledge."** Draft 5 had an idea called "created explanatory knowledge", with no one's state of mind in it. It is renamed, for now, "created explanation", and the place is marked for you. Your sources use the word for something that keeps itself in being, not for a state of mind. So the other choice is to keep their sense and add that condition, not the bare word. Until you choose, one case, the burned notebook, gets no answer. The same question applies on a smaller scale to "surprise" and "understanding", each defined in the theory with no attitude in it.
- **Worth is gone.** The theory now speaks only of someone's appraisal. That leans toward values being personal, where your sources treat them as open to criticism like anything else.
- **Exclusions are no longer permanent for a person.** Draft 5 said a refuted answer "stays refuted". Now it stays ruled out for as long as the argument can be used, as your instruction asks.
- **"The right question" is now "a new question".**

## 7. What broke, and can be repaired

The main break: the new text says what one step of an argument rules out, but never what a whole argument rules out. One added sentence fixes that: a whole argument rules out any claim that cannot stand together with its conclusion.

The smaller ones:
- A few sentences point to definitions that no longer say the same thing.
- In six places, a thing (a case, a test, a condition) still "rules out" by itself, with no one who can use an argument. That is the absolute sense you asked to remove.
- Two words of command are left: "forbids" and "prohibited".
- Three sentences still have the shape "an argument that such-and-such", which reads as the "reasons for" you excluded.

Each of these has a new wording in the full report. None needs the old words back.
