## 1. The short answer

**The theory no longer ties explanation to what physics allows, and it now says what your two footnotes say.** Physical possibility means whether something can exist or be done in the physical world. In the repaired copy of the theory, it bears on whether an explanation can be held, copied, taught, tested, built or performed. It does not decide what counts as an explanation. And a claim such as "perpetual motion is impossible" can now conflict with an explanation, with no test.

What this means for the idea: an explanation of a melody, a mathematical structure or a philosophical claim comes under the same rules as an explanation of a garden. The rules look at the thing explained, not at what anyone could physically do to it.

Two readers went through the repair. They found breaks, each with new wording, and no case moved away from the answer fixed for it beforehand. Their repairs are now made; no one has read them yet.

## 2. Perpetual motion: a conflict with no test

Someone explains how a water wheel works: the falling water turns the wheel, and the wheel drives a pump that lifts the same water back to the top, so the wheel turns forever. No one needs to build the wheel to find the trouble. Follow the explanation step by step and it implies perpetual motion. You put it this way:

> "If it can be shown that your explanation implies perpetual motion, then that's a conflict."

The repaired copy now says exactly this. First, some words. A **candidate** is anything someone offers as an explanation. An **argument** is, in your words, "reasons why this and not that". A **conflict** is a place where two things cannot both stand: two candidates, or a candidate and a claim. A claim is **ruled out** for a person while an argument that person can use rules it out; otherwise it is **not ruled out**, and it gains nothing from that.

So the water wheel conflicts with the claim "perpetual motion is impossible". For a person who tentatively accepts that claim (goes on with it, for whatever reason, open to dropping it), the argument rules the water wheel out. There was no test: no one looked at a wheel.

Two limits come with it, both yours. The bare claim is enough for the conflict: the person does not need the whole explanation of why perpetual motion is impossible. And, in your words, "It is not enough for a creative agent to do anything about it though." The conflict does not say where the wheel's explanation is in error, or how to repair it. It does not even say which to drop, the wheel or the claim. That stays the person's choice.

One care the readers added. Suppose a question asks what a pendulum would do with no friction at all. The answer that question asks for is that it would swing forever. "Perpetual motion is impossible" is a claim about the world as it is; it says nothing about that imagined pendulum. So in that case the conflict is still there, but the claim rules out no answer to the question, unless someone adds that the claim also speaks of the imagined pendulum.

## 3. A melody: what a question asks about

Take a question about music: why does this run of chords come to rest on its last chord? A candidate says that one note in the second-to-last chord sits just below the home note and leans toward it, and the last chord gives it that home note.

Every question comes with a **range**: the changes it asks about. Here the changes are changes to the music. Change that leaning note, and does the sense of rest go? Replace the last chord, and does it go? A candidate meets the theory's four **requirements** when it answers those changes as the music itself does, gives the answer to the question asked, does not assume that answer, and does not dodge the question.

Draft 5 said that a question's range is a set of "physically admitted changes". Read as written, that left a question about a melody, or about a mathematical structure, with no range at all: changing a note in a piece of music, or a starting assumption in mathematics, is not something physics admits or refuses.

The repaired copy says instead: the range is the changes the question asks about, in whatever the thing explained is, whether a garden, a mathematical structure, a melody or a philosophical claim. Whether anyone could play the changed chord matters for performing it, not for the question. A part that needs twenty fingers is still a change the question can ask about.

## 4. Where physical possibility still comes in

You wrote, "Well not strictly nothing. But", and then, of Claude's sentence "Physical possibility comes in only when something is instantiated or transformed", you wrote: "This is correct." Instantiated means carried by something physical; transformed means changed from one form or place to another. The repaired copy keeps physical possibility in two kinds of place.

- **Where an explanation is carried or changed.** To exist for anyone, an explanation of the seasons has to sit in ink, a brain or a file. Teaching it, copying it, playing a melody, building a bridge from a design: each changes physical things, and physics decides whether it can be done.
- **Where a claim about what is possible is content.** "Perpetual motion is impossible" is a claim a candidate can conflict with, as in section 2.

A **test** is the first kind: it changes the thing explained, as planting and waiting changes a garden. So a conflict between two **rivals** (two candidates each offered in place of the other) at the Earth standing upright is still a conflict, although no one can tilt the Earth to test it. The readers found one gap here, now repaired: saying that an explanation of the Earth and Sun allows the change "axis upright" is not a claim that anyone could carry it out, so it does not clash with physics.

The first version of the repair said physical possibility enters "in two ways only". Your "This is correct." answered a sentence with "only" in it, and then your footnote added a second way; you never closed the list at two. So the word is gone, and whether there are other ways is left open.

## 5. Using a claim you have not thought through

A **premise** is a claim an argument starts from. Suppose a student was told in school that perpetual motion is impossible, took it without a thought, and now uses it to find the flaw in the water wheel. You wrote:

> "Creative entities can do creative work without ever containing the entire contents of a explanation it uses to find errors. It need not. But that property alone is a costly gamble for all creative agents."

The repaired copy now says that a premise may be a claim taken as given: tentatively accepted, for whatever reason, even with no thought given to it, by someone who has no explanation of it. Nothing in the theory asks a person to hold the whole explanation first. The theory names the gamble in your words and puts no measure on it. A system that must hold the whole explanation first can be designed; that is, in your words, "a detail that exists outside the process".

The readers found a serious hole in the first version here. It counted a claim as usable by a person whenever the person had not dropped it, so a claim someone had never even heard of counted too. Then any claim and its opposite could be ruled out for everyone, and "not ruled out" never happened. Now a claim can serve a person as a premise only once that person has taken it up, tentatively, and not dropped it.

One limit stays. An argument that simply starts from its own conclusion, "the candidate fails because the candidate fails", rules nothing out: it goes in a circle. A person can still drop the candidate; that is the person's choice, not a ruling out.
