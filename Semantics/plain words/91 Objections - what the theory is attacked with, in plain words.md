# Objections to the theory, and how it answers them

*Written for the owner on 25 September 2026 from file 11, the draft and the project's checks. Each special word keeps one name throughout; see the word list.*

## What "the theory" is

A pole casts a shadow. "The shadow is ten metres long because the pole is five metres tall and the sun is low" is an explanation. The theory says what makes it one: a set of parts that depend on one another, each linked to a piece of the world, and judged only by what happens when something is changed. It never asks whether a part "is the same kind of thing" as a piece of the world, only whether it responds to changes as that piece does. An explanation that passes four tests on the question asked is an **account**:

1. **The parts match, change by change.** Raise the pole: in the explanation, as in the world, the shadow lengthens. Each part, and the whole, must respond as its piece of the world does.
2. **It answers the question asked.** A thermometer tells you who has a fever, not why.
3. **It does not assume its answer, and its working parts make a difference.** "The syrup puts you to sleep because it has a sleep-making power" fails.
4. **It does not dodge.** Leaving awkward changes out of the question is allowed only if it is said openly.

The theory also says where links come from: shaped by trial and survival (**selection**), built by guessing and criticism (**construction**), or simply written in by the modeller (**declaration**). It puts creativity in construction, and counts finding a new question as creative too.

**Where the objections come from.** File 11's opening section answers eleven expected complaints ("grievances") and names five weight-bearing claims, (A) to (E), "where to attack this". Part XV says what would defeat each, plus a mathematical error in its proofs. Others are answered in passing. Of some forty, the eight that go deepest are below, including the case the theory itself names as exposed (item 3).

**The checks the answers are judged against.** The *case check* (round S81) ran file 11 on 52 cases, each with a thoughtful person's verdict fixed in advance: file 11 gave that verdict on 46, stayed silent on 6, went against it on none. The fixed verdicts and the rulings were Claude's; only the outside readers, Atria and Mimo, read from outside. The *defect check* (round S88) settled three faults in the theory. The *source check* (S89) compared it with Deutsch's and Marletto's books. Then there is your decision on hard to vary (S20). A fourth draft restating it is under way and not finished; this note does not use it.

### 1. Passing every test while explaining nothing

**An example.** Carla explains why the harbour water is high at noon with two parts: the printed tide table, and a real float that rings the harbour bell. Take the bell part away and her explanation changes (it no longer says the bell rings), so a working part seems to make a difference.

**The objection.** A clever explanation might pass all four tests and still explain nothing. This is attack (A), which the theory says would do the most damage.

**The theory's answer.** It names the test each old trick fails. A table of observed answers fails the first: nothing in it responds when the world is changed. A calculation run the wrong way (the pole's height worked out from the shadow) fails the first too, when the question is what makes the shadow. Assuming the answer fails the third, and so does Carla: her only part about the tide is the answer written down, and a real mechanism answering another question, set beside it, does not repair that. A table of the response to every change in the range is, file 11 says, an account, not a trick.

**Does the answer hold up?** Partly.
- The case check gave the thoughtful person's verdict on Carla and on a similar syrup case, both written as hard cases. No record shows an explanation that passes all four and still explains nothing; Atria tried to build one and offered none.
- The defect check found a hole in the third test: file 11 never says which parts are the "working parts". On one reading, verdicts the theory needs flip (Greta's dough in item 8, and the pole and shadow). Both outside readers agreed in part, and the fault was narrowed. Mimo added that, on one reading, an idle part could pass the test through a combined change.
- The draft rewrites the test: the working parts are those the explanation offers as doing the work; delete some of them and see whether the difference asked about is lost. The outside readers found no case it breaks; one sentence changed after they read it.
- The draft narrows the claim about the full table: it is an account only if it passes the other tests too, and, like any new attempt, it would count against the theory if it passed all four and still explained nothing.

### 2. The tests let an explanation carry idle parts

**An example.** Tomas explains the seasons by the tilt of the Earth's axis, then adds: "And the sun god, who approves, keeps the tilt steady." Strike that sentence out and nothing changes.

**The objection.** A good explanation is hard to vary: every detail does a job. That is Deutsch's central idea. No test asks every part to work, so an explanation can carry an idle part and still pass.

**The theory's answer.** On purpose. The tests ask whether the parts match the world, not whether the explanation is economical: they do "not prefer an elegant account to a less elegant one", and a shorter proof "is not a fifth condition". Hard to vary is kept apart: the theory reports which parts are needed, and proves that the more questions an explanation must answer, the fewer versions of it still pass (or at least no more). But counting the questions answered, it adds, proves nothing ("is not a warrant").

**Does the answer hold up?** As design, yes; against its source and your decision, no.
- The source check called this "the widest real divergence from the main source": Deutsch counts useless details as a defect.
- The draft says it openly: the tests have no condition that each part work, and how hard an account is to vary "grades nothing".
- Your decision goes to the root. The theory's measure counts passing versions out of a set declared in advance. You hold that no one can list all rivals, even in principle; a rival is discovered, and two discovered rivals that both fit make a problem. So the measure rests on a reading of hard to vary that you do not hold, as the project's lesson on it records. (My illustration, in no text: "the moon goddess keeps it steady" fits just as well, which makes a problem for the god sentence; the tilt, as far as I know, meets no rival that fits.)
  - *Note, 25 September:* this illustration was written before draft 4 was finished, and it does not match draft 4. There, a moon goddess in place of the sun god changes only a name: no change could get a different answer from the one explanation than from the other, so the two are not rivals and make no problem. A sun god that does no work is caught another way instead, as an idle part (see sections 6 and 7.3 of file 92, the theory in plain words).
- The restatement through rivals is under way in a fourth draft, not finished. Tomas's case waits for the next case check; its fixed verdict is that Tomas explains the seasons and the god sentence is no part of the explanation.

### 3. Explanations of absence: the case the theory names as exposed

**An example.** An old house creaks at night, and the family says it is haunted. A builder says: "There is no ghost. The timbers shrink as they cool, and that makes the creaks."

**The objection.** The tests match parts to pieces of the world, but some good explanations are about what is not there, like "there is no ghost". Their key part has nothing to match, so the tests may reject real explanations. This is attack (B); the theory names explanations of absence as its exposed case.

**The theory's answer.** "Why is there no ghost effect?" is about a house without ghost parts, with a range that includes adding them. The explanation passes if adding a ghost part would change the answer in the explanation as in the house, and leaving it out leaves both unchanged. The ghost the family believes in is linked to a deleted piece, which limits nothing. The theory calls this adequate and says plainly that it may not be.

**Does the answer hold up?** Unknown; no case has tested it.
- The case check had no case of this sort. Atria tried this attack in the defect check and offered no counterexample.
- The source check found the books' absence examples (a machine that runs forever) mostly test something else, what is impossible, which the theory treats in a part it calls exact. The sharper challenge is Deutsch's: to deny something exists, explain why it seems to, or "anything at all could be denied by that method".
- The draft separates rather than meets this: why the creaks sound like footsteps is a separate question, which an account of the absence need not answer, and a bare denial ("people are mistaken") is no account of it. It records this as a deliberate difference from Deutsch.
- A case built on Deutsch's example waits for the next case check.

Whether "a deleted piece that limits nothing" captures a good explanation of absence is unsure.

<small>Where to find it: Part 0, "Where to attack this"; Part VII, "Explanations that remove structure"; Part XV (B). File 11, lines 63, 335–337, 530.</small>

### 4. Getting rid of "kinds"

**An example.** Two identical coffee cups sit on a tray. While you are out, someone may have swapped them. Nothing you can do tells them apart. Which is really yours?

**The objection.** Some things just are causes and others measurements, whatever we can do to them. Keeping only how parts respond to changes, the theory cannot tell a cause from something that merely goes along with it, and shrinks to "a thing is whatever you can do to it" (grievances 1, 2 and 11). Surely some case exists where a thing's kind does work no change could show: attack (C).

**The theory's answer.** A kind is how a part responds to the changes in the range. Push a barometer's needle by hand and the weather does not care: that is how a measurement responds, and no label is needed to see it. Where no change in the range separates two parts, they are one kind at that level; whoever says they "really" differ must name a separating change, like a lipstick mark, which means a finer level, with more changes in the range, and so a new question.

**Does the answer hold up?** The central claim does; a proof beside it did not.
- No check faulted the central claim: requiring each part to be tied to a part "of the same kind" adds nothing to the first test. Atria tried to break it and could not.
- The defect check found the neighbouring proof ("indistinguishable is identical") false as written: two explanations can pass while cutting the world differently, and then nothing pairs their parts. Both outside readers upheld this. The theory's defeat list counts an error in this proof against the theory, so that entry stands triggered in file 10 and file 11.
- The draft narrows it: one account only where both tie their parts to the same pieces of the world. Explanations cut differently still give the same answers, but the theory no longer says when they are the same account.
- I found no case aimed at attack (C).

<small>Where to find it: grievances 1, 2, 11; Part II, "Kinds are edit-signatures"; Part V, "no anchoring condition"; Part XV (C); Derivations 1, 2, 10. File 11, lines 39, 41, 59, 113–129, 281–283, 532, 544–558, 620.</small>

### 5. Survival is not truth

**An example.** Newly hatched chicks peck at small round grains, because chicks that did so survived. Offer small round plastic beads and they peck those too. The habit survived; it is wrong about beads.

**The objection.** If links are shaped by trial and survival, truth has become whatever survived (grievance 3). And the theory must say what survival leaves open at changes never tried. This is the first part of attack (D).

**The theory's answer.** Survival is how a link got there; whether it matches on changes it never met is a separate fact. The chick's habit can fail on beads because beads were never among its trials. File 11 adds a condition: an untried change is left open only if another member of the pool survived the same trials and would behave differently there. If none did, the pool, not the trials, fixes the outcome.

**Does the answer hold up?** Mostly, at a cost.
- File 10 said flatly that the untried is always open, and gave the wrong verdict on the forbidden-wire case (the only different design needs a wire no design in the pool has). File 11's condition was its one declared change, and all five readings of file 11 gave the thoughtful person's verdict. Atria argued, unprompted, that file 10's version was false.
- The planning notes admit the cost: with the condition, the claim is close to true by its own words, so it is hard to defeat.
- The source check raised a question the draft leaves open: if a programmer designed the pool, success on untried changes is the programmer's knowledge, and the theory cannot yet say so.
- A case aimed at the condition's weakest sentence (a discarded lamp controller) has a verdict from authors who never read the theory, and waits.

<small>Where to find it: grievance 3; Part IV, "Three provenances" and "Expectation, surprise, violation"; Part XII, "Selection"; Part XV (D); Derivation 3. File 11, lines 43, 63, 197, 217–225, 473, 534, 560–566.</small>

### 6. Is construction just faster selection?

**An example.** On Monday an apprentice tries keys from a ring until one opens the lock. On Tuesday, at a new lock, she feels where a blank key catches and files it until it turns. Is Tuesday just Monday's trying, done faster?

**The objection.** Guessing and criticism may be only trial and survival inside a head; Deutsch himself calls criticism a kind of selection. "Selected" may be just a label (grievance 9). If every construction could be rewritten as selection, the theory's place for creativity collapses (grievance 6; the second part of attack (D)). The third part, that explanation does not work on the layer of lasting things selection builds, is left out here.

**The theory's answer.** The two are told apart by history, not result. In selection nothing pictures the target and nothing criticises; in construction the target is pictured and criticised. Monday's trying pictures no lock; Tuesday's filing works to a picture of what the lock needs. Whether selection happened is a physical fact, not a label. What would defeat the theory: showing every construction can be rewritten as selection without loss.

**Does the answer hold up?** On the cases tried, yes.
- The case check gave the thoughtful person's verdicts on the apprentice and on a card player who invents a part for cards she has never seen.
- The source check found a clash of words, not substance: Deutsch's "selection" keeps the target in view, so it does not defeat the theory. But file 11 had dropped the word "blind" from its opening; the draft now says selection's history holds no picture of the target, and records the difference.
- The draft adds that a picture need not be spelled out, as when a pianist imagines a passage.
- Kept on purpose: Deutsch holds that grasping any idea takes guessing; the theory says not all learning is construction, and someone who only passes an idea on constructs nothing.
- I found no record of an attempt at the rewriting that would defeat the claim.

<small>Where to find it: Part 0, "What this document claims"; grievances 6, 9; Part IV, "Three provenances"; Part X, "Construction"; Part XV (D). File 11, lines 15, 49, 55, 193–203, 401–403, 534.</small>

### 7. Finding a question

**An example.** A team argues for weeks about who is lazy. Noor asks: which tasks have no owner? The arguing stops. A teammate asks "which tasks were done on a Tuesday?", which is new too.

**The objection.** Counting a new question as creative either cheapens creativity, since any new question counts, or misses some real case of finding the right question: one that starts, say, as a vague sense that something is wrong, before anyone can say what the question is. This is attack (E).

**The theory's answer.** A question (target, range, and what is asked) is treated like a set of parts itself: it counts as found only if the finder built it, it was new to her, and she used it on the problem. A matter mentioned in passing becomes part of an account only when changes to it are allowed, and adding it is construction. Whether a question was worth finding is separate, and the theory does not judge it.

**Does the answer hold up?** It does what it says; that may be the cheapening feared.
- The case check: the theory stayed silent on the verdict's "its merit is real", since worth comes from outside it. So the Tuesday question also counts as found, if built, new and used; its worth lies outside the theory.
- The draft helps: an old question reworded is not new, and a picture need not be spelled out ("an investigator can notice an inconsistency before articulating its premises").
- My reading, in no text: your decision, that two discovered rivals that both fit make a problem, could give finding a question a sharper starting point. No finished draft connects the two.
- No case was aimed at this attack. Atria called the short proof behind it "a sketch … not a proof", though "Nothing here is false"; the project agreed a piece is missing, and counted no defeat, since the defeat list names only Derivations 1 to 3.

### 8. Everything depends on the chosen range

**An example.** Bruno's rule for when the stream floods fails in March; he limits it to months outside the thaw. It fails after a dam release; he adds "no release". Then he adds "west wind". Each limit is stated openly, and each restated rule has been right since.

**The objection.** Every verdict is tied to the range a question covers, and the explainer chooses it, so nothing is objective and any failing explanation can be saved by narrowing the range (grievance 4). Fixing the question while it is judged, yet letting questions change over time, is having it both ways (grievance 10).

**The theory's answer.** Physics fixes which changes are possible; a range is a stated part of those, and within it there is a fact about matching. A quiet narrowing fails the fourth test and invites criticism. A narrowing after a failure is a new claim, not a rescue, and changing the question unannounced is "a failure of the record". Whether a limit is appropriate is open to criticism; the theory has no rule certifying it.

**Does the answer hold up?** Not by its sources' standard; the source check ranked this gap first to act on.
- Bruno's verdict: no explanation, and the series is a retreat. The theory stays silent, in file 10 and file 11. Both source books hold that a limit needs its own explanation.
- Greta limits her dough rule to warm rooms and says why: yeast is slow in the cold. Swap her limit for "not on Tuesdays" and her explanation has nothing to say. The ruling agrees with the thoughtful person, but four of five readings of file 11 found it silent and Mimo called the ruling wrong; a fresh re-reading upheld it at medium confidence. The draft now says a verdict on a limit comes with the reason the explanation states.
- The draft adds no test, so Bruno stays silent; and a case turning on an unstated input the theory lists now counts as a missing input, not a defeat.
- Your decision: "A record is redundant. Once the explanation is rescued, the mistake shouldn't be able to creep back in." The theory's answer leans on a record. The fourth draft is to say a correction sticks on a fixed question; how that treats Bruno is open.

<small>Where to find it: grievances 4, 10; Part III, "Scope, and a question that can be wrong"; Part V, non-vacuity; Part XIV, "Declared inputs"; Derivation 7. File 11, lines 45, 57, 159–163, 259, 514, 592–598.</small>

## Objections left out

- **"Old theories with new words"** (grievance 5): whether the mix is new is for scholars to judge.
- **"In mathematics nothing can be changed to see what follows"** (grievance 7): removing a starting assumption of a proof is a change, so the tests apply to proofs.
- **"Where is beauty?"** (grievance 8): worth comes from outside; the theory does not know which reasons about beauty are true.
- **"A mathematical error"** (Part XV): triggered by the proof in item 4; a rule on piling-up errors also lacked conditions, corrected in the draft.
- **"Prediction is explanation"**: identical outputs through different inner routes still count as different.
- **"What a thing stands for comes from matter alone"**: the theory claims no such thing; standing for something needs matter plus a history.
- **"A rough explanation should fail"** (Part V): it answers a rough question.
- **"A list of failures proves something can never be understood"** (Part XIII): it does not; one way round shows there is no such limit.
- **"Being able to examine your own thinking without end means you can understand anything"** (Part XIII): it does not.
- **"Owned because it can, and can because owned"** (Parts XII, XIV): resting on each other, neither is supported.

## Word list

- **Theory:** the written claim about what makes an explanation; file 11 is in force, and file 10 is the version before it.
- **The draft:** the third draft of the second revision; read by the outside readers, not yet tried on cases, not frozen.
- **Explanation:** anything offered as the reason something happens.
- **Account:** an explanation that passes all four tests on its question.
- **Test:** one of the four requirements for an account (the theory's "condition").
- **Question:** what is explained (its target), the range of changes covered, and what is asked.
- **Range:** the changes a question covers (the theory's "contract").
- **Change:** anything alterable: a push, a setting, a removed rule or starting assumption.
- **Level:** how finely a situation is described.
- **Part:** a piece of an explanation or of the world (the theory's "component").
- **Working parts:** the parts an explanation offers as doing the work.
- **Idle part:** a part whose removal changes nothing.
- **Link:** which part of an explanation stands for which piece of the world (the theory's "transport").
- **Match:** a link matches when both sides respond alike to every change in the range.
- **Picture:** something inside a system standing for its target.
- **History:** how a link came about.
- **Selection:** making a link by trial and survival, with no picture of the target in its history.
- **Construction:** building a link by guessing and criticism, with the target pictured.
- **Declaration:** writing a link into a model; no creativity claim rests on it.
- **Pool / trials / survive:** the alternatives selection picks from; the changes a link met while shaped; being in the pool and passing every trial.
- **Kind:** what sort of thing a part is; for the theory, how it responds to the changes in the range.
- **Label:** a name given to a part, like "cause".
- **Version:** the explanation with a detail changed, from a set declared in advance; the theory's measure of hard to vary counts the versions that pass.
- **Hard to vary:** said of an explanation whose details cannot be changed without it failing.
- **Rival:** a discovered competing explanation that also fits.
- **Problem:** in your words, two discovered rivals that both fit.
- **Limit:** a stated narrowing of a range.
- **Grievance / attack / defeat list:** the eleven expected complaints; the five claims (A) to (E); Part XV.
- **Case / thoughtful person's verdict:** a short situation; what a careful person would say, fixed in advance.
- **Outside readers:** Atria and Mimo, models outside the project that try to overturn its rulings.
