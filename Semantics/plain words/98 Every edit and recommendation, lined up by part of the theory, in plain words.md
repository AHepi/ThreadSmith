## 1. What was done, and how to use it

**Every change ever made to the theory's text, and every change anyone proposed for it, is now collected and lined up under the sentence of today's theory that it touches.** That is 1,894 records, from the first standalone text to the last cross-examination: 865 edits that were made, and 1,029 proposals, whether taken, turned down, replaced by a later wording, or still waiting for you.

**Only the sentences are copied, word for word, never the reasons given for them.** Each record names the file and the place it came from, and that is the one way back to the reasons.

**To use it,** open the front page, pick a part of the theory, and read down. Each part has its own page, which goes through it sentence by sentence, in the order of the text. Under each sentence stands everything that ever happened to it, oldest first: what it said before and after, whether the change was made, and whether the new words are still there today. Untouched sentences are shown too, so each part reads whole.

## 3. How the groups were chosen

Once everything had been collected, two ways of grouping were tried on the same data: by the idea a change carries, for example everything about rivals, and by where it sits in the text.

Both ran into one snag. A change often reaches several sentences, and a sentence often has several changes, so filing whole changes would have scattered the history of about half the sentences that have one (299 or 268 of the 540 there were then). Filing by sentence scatters none. So the thing lined up is the sentence of today's theory, and a change that reaches several sentences is shown in full once and pointed to from the others.

The sentences keep their order and fall into the theory's own parts, and the groups carry the theory's own part titles, from "Organizations and their changes" to "What would rule this class out". There are fourteen of those, and two more: "The document as a whole", for the opening pages, and "Vocabulary across the text", for word swaps that run through the whole theory.

The opening chapters and the worked results at the end mostly treat matters that another part owns. Each such section is filed with that part. The list of objections the theory expects at the start, for instance, is split up, and most objections go with the part that answers them.

A proposal that never reached the text, or an edit whose sentence was later removed, stands at the end of its section. The grouping by idea was kept as a cross-index of twenty ideas.
