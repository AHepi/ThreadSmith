## 2. Rules for every writer

1. **The word sheet binds.** One plain word for each thing, never a synonym. The four conditions of an account are **requirements**; **test** means only finding out what the target does at a setting; components are **pieces**; "Part" names only the theory's divisions.
2. **Example first.** Every section opens with its first worked example, told as a short story, before any general statement. Every idea after it gets a concrete instance first (from the example, from the theory's own examples, or a one-line everyday illustration), then the general statement.
3. **No codes in the body.** No Part numbers, line numbers, case ids, decision numbers, round numbers, equation tags, symbols, Greek letters or formulas in the body text. Cases are named by title ("the fever and the thermometer"). The owner's decisions are named by date and subject ("your decision of 24–25 September on hard to vary"). Each section ends with one small line, "Where to find it:", giving the Parts, the draft-4 line numbers, the Derivations and the case ids, as the objections note does.
4. **Say which kind of verdict you report.** For a case from the book of 52, say what the ruling in the round on file 11 found (agrees, silent), and, where draft 4 moves the reason, say that this is draft 4's view, not yet tried in a round. For a proposed case (N-cases and the lamp controller), say plainly that no round has used it and that what you give is this plan's view of what the text says. Never present a view as a ruling.
5. **Say plainly what is unsure.** Each section has a short "What is not settled" part (a heading or a closing paragraph). Do not soften it and do not inflate it.
6. **What changed from file 11.** Each section has one or two plain lines saying what draft 4 changed in this ground, taken from the plan below. Say "file 11" and "draft 4"; do not list change numbers.
7. **The owner's decision on hard to vary** (decision S20 of 24–25 September) is to be reflected exactly, and nowhere contradicted: nobody can list all rivals, even in principle; a rival is a competitor someone has offered; two offered rivals that both fit make a problem; a corrected mistake cannot creep back in, and no record of rescues is needed. Never describe hard to vary as counting versions or variants. Say plainly that the theory now defines only **easy to vary**, and that it does not define "hard to vary".
8. **Names.** The theory's title may appear once, in section 0. No model names anywhere: say "the outside readers" for the two cross-examiners, and "the ruling" (never whose ruling). Do not name the project's own tooling or agents.
9. **Mathematics in words.** The two balances, the twenty-three tokens, the odd-sized tables and the build-up of errors are told in words, with at most one number sentence each, and no symbols.
10. **Short paragraphs, headings, few lists.** Lists only for the four requirements, the kinds of question, the three histories, the main claims, and "What is not settled". Address the owner as "you" only when naming the owner's own decisions.
11. **The source books.** Where this plan names a departure from the source books, give it in one or two sentences, marked as a departure. Do not open the third book named by the owner; it is not a source.
12. **Render the theory text only.** The revision note, the sources note and the record are not part of the theory; use them only for the "what changed" and "departure" lines.

## 3. The sections

### Section 0 — What this theory is for, and how to read this (800 words)

**Covers.** The title (L1–3, the name given once). Part 0, "What this document claims" (L9–17) in outline, and "What this document does not claim" (L19–27) as a short list. Part I, the six principles (L65–77), one sentence each with a pointer to the section that uses it. Reading guide: the word sheet; the pattern of each section; the status of draft 4; the case books and the round on file 11; the source books.

**Ideas, in order.**
1. Open with the pole and the shadow (Part VII, L325): "The shadow is ten metres long because the pole is five metres tall and the sun is low." Raise the pole and the shadow lengthens; shade part of the ground with a board and the pole does not grow. The theory judges explanations only by what happens when something is changed.
2. What the theory is for: to say exactly what makes something an explanation, and what makes a thinker creative at explaining, including at finding questions, so that each claim can be checked against cases and could be shown wrong.
3. The central guess (L17) in one paragraph: four things are enough to describe explanatory creativity — set-ups and the changes they allow; links between set-ups, judged only by how they behave under change; the history of those links and of the questions they serve; and how all of it is embodied in matter. A real explanatory achievement these cannot describe, or something meeting all four while plainly explaining nothing, would count against it (section 11).
4. What it does not claim (L21–27): that matter alone fixes what a thing stands for (it needs matter plus a history); that prediction is explanation; that it supplies a standard of beauty, a probability of truth, a merit score, a measure of worth or a ranking of thinkers; that anyone belongs to its classes.
5. The six principles of Part I, one sentence each: matching is a fact whether or not anyone accepts it (section 2); a theory can be partly wrong and still explain, but not when the error is in the very dependence said to do the work (section 2); ideas may be entertained without proof, criticism is itself a guess, and action need not wait for proof (section 9); any aspect of a system's practice can be reviewed, with effect (section 10); any sort of matter may carry a set-up, as far as the physics lets contents pass between sorts (section 10); selection and construction are two different histories (section 7).
6. How to read: the word sheet, and each section's pattern (example, ideas, what the theory says on cases with fixed verdicts, what changed from file 11, what is not settled, where to find it). Status: draft 4 is file 11 with 57 changes, 51 of them changes of claim; it is not frozen; the new paragraphs on rivals, problems and failed answers have not been seen by the outside readers; draft 4 has not been tried on the cases. The round on file 11 gave the fixed verdict on 46 of 52 cases, was silent on 6, and went against none. The theory has two source books, and departs from them on purpose at places each section names.

**Example.** The pole and the shadow, the theory's own (Part VII). No case-book example is needed here.

**Changed from file 11.** Mention only that Part 0 now says a claim of worth takes the standard of worth from outside, and a claim needing a probability, merit score or ranking is unsettled (L25); details in sections 9 and 11.

**Not settled.** The status of draft 4 as above; the rest is gathered in section 12.

### Section 1 — The world as a set-up, and questions about it (1,500 words)

**Covers.** Part 0 L11 (first two sentences) and complaint 7 (L49, mathematics has changes too). Part II, "Organizations" (L83–105) and "Roles are derived" (L107–109). Part III, "Contracts" (L133–147), "The respect is the query" (L149–151), "Contracts have provenance" (L153–155), and the second paragraph of "Scope, and a question that can be wrong" (L161). Part VII, "Production and direction" (L323–325), "Identification" (L327–331), "Obstruction" (L333–335). Part III's first scope paragraph (L159) goes to section 6.

**Ideas, in order.**
1. The fever and the thermometer, told first (example 1).
2. A set-up: pieces, slots and their values, circumstances, and allowed changes; each piece allows some combinations of values; the consistent states are those every piece allows. Use the pole, the sun and the shadow.
3. Three facts about changes: a removed piece stops restricting its slots; a change that sets a slot replaces the piece that used to fix it, rather than adding a second, clashing rule beside it; a changed status rule is a changed piece. Values can be paths, proofs or histories; set-ups may loop; several consistent states stay several.
4. Roles come from the allowed changes, not from labels: an input is a slot some change sets directly; an output is fixed by a piece from its other slots; a reading reports another slot and can be altered without altering it. Direction comes from the allowed changes: set the shadow's length directly and the pole and the sun stay as they were. "Pole" and "shadow" as words fix nothing.
5. Mathematics has changes too (complaint 7): removing a starting assumption, dropping a constraint, changing a dimension.
6. A question: its target, its range (the settings it covers, including the untouched setting), what is asked, the demands and safeguards in play, and the history of its range. The answer at each setting.
7. What is asked fixes the sort of question, not a label: producing, pinning-down, blocking, status, purpose. The same target with a different range or a different what-is-asked is a different question, and an answer to one is not an answer to another. A reading that pins something down, with a reliable forecast from it, answers the pinning-down question; whether the thing read also produces the outcome is the producing question.
8. Pinning down, with the two weighings (L331) in words: two readings each give a sum of an unknown mass and a balance's error; they pin down the difference between the two errors and not the mass; repeating a weighing adds nothing, and a reading against a known weight can. "Take balance B's error to be zero because that gives the mass I favour" is not an inference from the readings.
9. Blocking, with the tokens (L335): twenty-three whole tokens cannot be shared equally among three people, because passing tokens from hand to hand never changes the total, twenty-three, and no three equal whole shares make twenty-three; an unchanging quantity blocks every chain of allowed steps. Equal values of such a quantity do not show that a state is reachable. Allowing tokens to be cut changes the states and does not refute the claim about whole tokens. Then example 2.
10. A range has a history: stated by the modeller, selected, or constructed. To claim that someone found a question, the range must have been constructed, with evidence (pointer to section 8).
11. A question can be faulty: it can miss its target, assume an untouched setting that cannot be, or ask for things that clash. Exposing that is a different question with its own range. What is not allowed is changing the range or what is asked during an assessment without saying the claim has changed. Giving a replaced question a meaning makes a different, coherent question; it does not answer the first.

**Worked examples.**
- **The fever and the thermometer** (O6). *Situation:* Hana says the child has a fever because the thermometer reads thirty-nine, and later that the reading is what makes the child shiver; warming the thermometer in her hand raises the reading while the shivering is unchanged, and a cool bath eases the shivering. *Fixed verdict:* the first statement is a good reason to believe there is a fever; the second mistakes the instrument for the illness. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees. The thermometer's reading is a reading: change only the reading (warm the thermometer) and the child stays as she was; change the child (the bath) and the reading follows. The reading answers the pinning-down question "has she a fever?", whose range holds changes to the reading; "what makes her shiver?" is a producing question, whose range holds changes to her temperature, and there the reading does nothing. An answer to the first is not an answer to the second. Unchanged in draft 4.
- **The machine that runs forever** (N8, a proposed case). *Situation:* asked why no machine drives a pump forever with no fuel, Pia says every design tried so far has stopped and perhaps one will work; Quentin says the energy anything gives out must be matched by energy put in, for any design, tried or not. *Fixed verdict:* only Quentin's answer explains. *What the theory says (no ruling; this plan's view of the text):* the question is a blocking question. Quentin gives an unchanging quantity, total energy, that no allowed step alters, so no chain of steps reaches a state where the pump has run on for nothing, for every design at once. Pia gives a list of failures: it has no piece that responds to any change, like a table of observed answers (section 2), and a finite list of failures does not show that something cannot be done (Part XIII, section 10). Say that one source book draws the same line between "cannot happen" and "has not happened yet".

**Changed from file 11.** None of these paragraphs changed. (Draft 4's change to Part III's limits belongs to section 6.)

**Not settled.** The theory does not say how to choose the right level of detail for a question (a point the comparison with the source books raised). It gives no rule for telling that a question is faulty: exposing a fault is itself a different question. The machine case has not been tried in a round.

**Where to find it.** Part 0 L11, L49; Part II L83–109; Part III L133–155, L161; Part VII L323–335; cases O6, N8.

### Section 2 — When an explanation counts: the four requirements (1,700 words)

**Covers.** Part I, explanatory realism and fallibility (L67–69). Part IV, "Transports" (L181–189), to introduce the link and the anchor. Part V, the candidate, its commitments and background (L231), the four requirements (L233–265), and "What (E) excludes, and what it does not" (L267–277). Part VII, "Explanations that remove structure" (L337–339) and "Odd-order skew-symmetric matrices" (L341–343). Part 0's central guess (L17) as a pointer back to section 0. Part V's "Why there is no anchoring condition" (L279–281) goes to section 3; non-vacuity's detail on stated limits is recalled in section 6.

**Ideas, in order.**
1. Carla's tide table and bell, told first (example 1), left open until requirement 3.
2. A candidate: its own set-up; a link from the target (which state goes with which, which change with which, which circumstances with which; and each of the candidate's pieces tied to an anchor in the target, with a slot pairing); and its commitments, the pieces it offers as doing the work, whether or not anyone has described their work. The rest is background.
3. Requirement 1, matching, in two halves: piece by piece (each commitment behaves like its anchor at every setting of the range, with the anchor's hidden slots set aside) and whole (the whole candidate's consistent states are the target's, carried over; and chains of changes carry over as chains). Why both: a whole that matches can hide a wrong split into pieces; pieces that each match can hide a lost tie between them.
4. Requirement 2, answering the question asked: the same answer as the target at every setting of the range, with what is asked held fixed; an account of a different what-is-asked is not an account of this one.
5. Requirement 3, no assumed answer: the target's answer must not appear, at the level of detail, as an unexplained input or as a piece; moving it into a piece called "law" does not help; sameness is judged at the level of detail, not by treating all equivalent mathematical truths as one. And there must be a setting and a group of commitments such that the answer there differs from the answer at the untouched setting (or is not fixed there in the way claimed), and removing the group loses that difference. Return to Carla.
6. Requirement 4, no dodging: the target has a consistent state at the untouched setting; the range is a stated selection from the possible changes, and anything possible it leaves out is left out by a stated limit. A range of mere renamings, or one that leaves out every change where the commitments could matter, fails requirement 3 and so is no range on which an account can be claimed.
7. What the four rule out: a table of observed answers (fails matching when the range holds a change to it); a full response table is not such a table (it matches, and is an account if it meets the rest); a calculation run backwards (fails whole matching on the producing question, and may match on the pinning-down question, which is a different question); "it is so because it is so"; an explanation whose only real piece restates the answer, with a genuine mechanism that answers another question placed beside it; a contrast that no possible change brings about.
8. What they do not rule out: a true mechanism guessed for bad reasons (the reasons are judged elsewhere, section 9); a less elegant account with the same matching; a coarse account (example 2); a proof, which answers "why does this follow under these rules?" and not "what caused this?". Depth is relative to the question, and the requirements measure none of it.
9. A worked piece of mathematics (L343), in words only: "why does every square table of numbers with an odd number of rows, where each number is minus its mirror image across the diagonal, have no inverse?" The range: drop the mirror rule, drop the oddness, drop both, with arithmetic held fixed. The long expansion of the table meets all four requirements, with a plain example for each contrast. A three-line proof is shorter, and shortness is not a fifth requirement.
10. Explanations of absence (L337–339): "why is there no such effect?" is about a target without the pieces a rival would need, with a range that includes the changes that would add them. The account passes if adding them changes the answer in the account as it does in the target, and their absence leaves both unchanged; the rival's imagined structure is tied to a removed cluster, which restricts nothing. Why the absent thing seems to be there is a different question, which an account of the absence neither answers nor needs to; a bare denial is not an account of that other question. The theory itself names this as a place it may fail (section 11).
11. The two principles behind the requirements (Part I): whether a link matches does not depend on anyone accepting it; a theory can hold a correct dependence on its range and errors elsewhere, but an error in the very dependence said to do the work cannot count. A false theory offered as an answer is a candidate, which ordinary speech may still call an explanation; whether an account is easy to vary is a separate matter (section 5).

**Worked examples.**
- **The tide table with a real bell attached** (O2). *Situation:* Carla explains why the harbour water is high at noon with the printed tide table plus a real float that rings the harbour bell; take the bell mechanism away and her explanation changes, since it no longer says the bell will ring. *Fixed verdict:* she has explained the bell and nothing about the tide; the table is the answer written down. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees. Her only piece about the tide is the answer she was asked for, written in, so she fails the first half of "no assumed answer"; a real mechanism that answers a different question (why the bell rings) placed beside it does not repair this. In draft 4 her candidate has no support at all (section 4), so the question whether the bell is idle does not arise; the verdict rests where it did.
- **Salt on the icy step** (O7). *Situation:* Ines says the ice melted because she spread salt; spread salt and the ice melts within the hour, sweep it off at once and the ice stays; she knows nothing finer, and no instrument is involved. *Fixed verdict:* a genuine explanation, a coarse one; the salt produced the melting. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees. The range holds the two changes; the salt is a commitment whose removal loses the difference between the salted and unsalted settings; and a coarse account answers the coarse question, its strength fixed by its range, not by what a finer range would add. Draft 4's rewording of "no assumed answer" states this case's reason more directly (the change list counts it as a move toward the fixed verdict).

**Changed from file 11.** Draft 4 says which pieces are the commitments (those offered as doing the work, whether or not described) and that the rest is background; it restates "no assumed answer" as the removal test on a group, with sameness judged at the level of detail; a full response table is an account only if it also meets the other requirements; the absence paragraph adds the separate question of why the thing seems present, and the bare denial; Part I now says "explanation" there means an account, and a false theory offered as an answer is a candidate.

**Departures.** Deutsch counts a false myth as an explanation; here it is a candidate. Deutsch asks an explanation that denies something to explain why it seems to exist; here that is a different question.

**Not settled.** Nobody has yet built a candidate that meets all four requirements and still plainly explains nothing (an outside reader tried and offered none); that would defeat the claim that the four are enough. The treatment of absence is offered as adequate and may not be; no case in a round has tested it (a proposed case, "Denying the inner experience", is built on exactly this and waits). The text uses "active" for pieces without defining it. The phrase "including any that assigns an input" can be read two ways; the known verdicts are the same on both.

**Where to find it.** Part I L67–69; Part IV L181–189; Part V L229–277; Part VII L337–343; cases O2, O7 (and N22 named).

### Section 3 — Kinds are how pieces respond (1,300 words)

**Covers.** Part 0 L11 (last two sentences); complaints 1 (L37), 2 (L39) and 11 (L57). Part II, "Kinds are edit-signatures" (L111–127). Part V, the last sentence of L245 and "Why there is no anchoring condition" (L279–281). Part VII, "Constitutive rules" (L345–347). Part VIII, "Functional transport", "Relational transport", "Approximate transport" (L353–363). Derivation 1 (L552–558). Part XV, the main claim that kinds can be dropped (L540).

**Ideas, in order.**
1. The float that does two jobs, told first (example 1).
2. A response pattern: how a piece behaves at every setting of the range, taken together. Two pieces are one kind on a range when their patterns agree under some pairing of their slots. Kinds are relative to the range: a coarser range lumps more pieces together, and pieces of one kind may come apart on a finer range.
3. Patterns are built from behaviour, not from the values slots happen to take: two pieces that differ only in values are one kind, whatever the difference is called (example 2). A change that sets a slot replaces only the piece that fixes it, not the pieces that read it; a change under which both behave alike separates nothing.
4. The same comparison works across two candidates (through their links) and between a piece and its anchor (Derivation 1, idea 6).
5. What ordinary speech calls a cause, a measurement and a status rule are families of response patterns (L123–125, in words from the word sheet). A piece that reads another has a measurement's pattern: change only the reading and the other stays; change the other and the reading follows. Which of the two an account offers as producing an outcome is settled by the pattern, not by the account's wording. Status rules (L347): "which status under this rule?" is answered by the rule's pattern; whether the rule governs a practice, and whether it should, are different questions.
6. No separate anchoring requirement: "each piece must be tied to one of the same kind" is either redundant (if a change in the range separates the two kinds, piece-by-piece matching already fails) or empty (if none does, they are one kind on that range). There is no third case. Derivation 1 proves that piece-by-piece matching gives each piece its anchor's pattern. So "kind" can be dropped from the definition of an account without losing any case.
7. The complaints answered: a cause and a correlation differ in how they respond to a change, which is what the theory checks (complaint 1); this is not "a thing is whatever you can do to it", because a kind is what a level's allowed changes can tell apart, a content can hold more, and every claim about kinds is tied to its level (complaint 2); a status rule and a cause do differ, and the difference is one of pattern, which is not available before the allowed changes are fixed (complaint 11).
8. Matching over many steps (Part VIII): matching step by step for each basic change gives matching for every chain of changes; two-way matching is what treating two set-ups as equivalent needs, one-way matching is enough for a simplified version; with approximate matching, if each step's error is small and one step of the candidate can stretch a difference by at most a fixed factor, the error after many steps stays under a bound that grows with that factor; without such a factor no bound follows, and an exact question is never quietly swapped for an approximate one. (A one-line everyday illustration is allowed here, for instance measuring string that is off by a little each time; do not make it a worked example.)
9. What would defeat this: a case where a label tells two accounts apart that no allowed change tells apart, and the difference does explanatory work (section 11).

**Worked examples.**
- **The float that does two jobs** (O9). *Situation:* a float in a fuel tank turns a dial and, when low enough, opens a valve to the reserve; Milan says the falling float opens the valve, Lena says the dial reaching "low" does; bend the needle to "full" and the valve still opens, hold the float up with a wire and the dial reads "full" and the valve stays shut. *Fixed verdict:* Milan is right; the float is read by the dial and also does the work. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees. The dial has a measurement's response pattern (change only the dial and the valve is unaffected; change the float and the dial follows); the float has a cause's pattern for the valve. Which one an explanation offers as producing is settled by the pattern, not by its wording. Unchanged in draft 4.
- **Two thermostats** (O10). *Situation:* two thermostats of one make, set to eighteen and twenty-two degrees; Rosa says they are one kind in two states, Emil says two kinds. *Fixed verdict:* Rosa is right about what the devices are; Emil's difference matters only when the question is why this room is warm. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees (several readers found the theory silent; the ruling upheld the agreement). The setting of eighteen or twenty-two is a value of a slot, not a difference in behaviour; draft 4 says outright that two pieces differing only in values are one kind, whatever the difference is called. Emil's difference answers a different question (why this room is warm), with its own range.

**Changed from file 11.** Draft 4 says how pieces of two candidates are compared, and that patterns are built from behaviour, not values, with a change that sets a slot replacing only the piece that fixes it; the error build-up result now states what it assumes and what it measures (a slip found in the defects round of September, repaired).

**Not settled.** No case in any round has been aimed at the claim that kinds can be dropped; an outside reader tried to break it and could not. The text does not say whether a slot pairing may also change the values it carries (this touches recoding, section 7).

**Where to find it.** Part 0 L11, L37, L39, L57; Part II L111–127; Part V L245, L279–281; Part VII L345–347; Part VIII L353–363; Part XV L540; Derivation 1 L552–558; cases O9, O10.

### Section 4 — Which pieces do the work (1,400 words)

**Covers.** Part VI from its start through "Commitments that do no work" (L287–313): the cut-down candidate, supports, load-bearing groups, the tipping edge, the smallest-supports theorem, redundant routes and routes that ran, interference, spread-out support, idle commitments. Derivation 9 (L614–616). Part 0, "It does not say prediction is explanation" (L23). Part VI's "Rivals" and "Problems" (L315–317) go to section 5.

**Ideas, in order.**
1. Tomas and the sun god, told first (example 1), left open until idea 8.
2. The cut-down candidate keeps some commitments, with the background held fixed. A support is a group of commitments that on its own, with the background, makes an account. The theory assumes neither that adding a commitment keeps a support, nor that there is a smallest one.
3. A group is load-bearing in a support when the support stops being one without it. A group can be load-bearing while no single commitment in it is. Load-bearing is relative to the support judged, and the supports judged are groups of the commitments actually written, whether or not anyone has set them out, never supports that someone could write with other commitments.
4. The tipping edge: for a stated family of changes to the candidate itself, the pairs of changed candidates of which one is an account and the other is not.
5. The smallest-supports theorem, in words: when the commitments are finitely many, adding never breaks a support, and the whole is a support, a single commitment is load-bearing somewhere exactly when it belongs to some smallest support, and is needed in every support exactly when it belongs to all of them. It applies only where its assumptions hold.
6. Redundant routes (example 2): two routes, each enough alone, neither needed. A route is a support of the candidate whether or not anything ever runs it; it ran in a system when actual events realized it (the route that ran, defined in section 9). Credit for a repair goes by the routes that ran, not by the routes a candidate contains. A route present from the start is a route whether or not anyone described its work; a piece tied to a different target after a removal makes a different candidate, whose success is its own, not the old one's.
7. Same outputs, different accounts (Derivation 9): two systems with the same outputs for every input can differ in which routes run, so no description of outputs alone settles a claim about an account; the same holds for judging where text came from by the text alone, and for judging use from delivery logs. Hence the theory does not say that prediction is explanation.
8. Interference: the whole candidate fails although a smaller group succeeds, and the added commitment is not declared "not a commitment" to fix this. A spread-out support: endlessly many commitments, each giving a tighter bound, with no single one and no smallest group doing the work; the whole group is load-bearing together. Idle commitments: the four requirements have no rule that each commitment does work; in a candidate that has a support, a commitment is idle when adding it to or removing it from any support leaves a support; the candidate with it is then an account exactly when it is one without it, and it is load-bearing in no support. When commitments are endlessly many, a group of idle ones can still be load-bearing. Return to Tomas.
9. What left the theory here: file 11's lemma counting the variants of an account that pass, over a family of "explanatory jobs", and its definition of "reach". Nothing in the theory now counts, grades or ranks candidates (section 5).

**Worked examples.**
- **The seasons and the sun god** (N1, a proposed case). *Situation:* Tomas explains the seasons by the tilted axis that keeps pointing one way, each piece checkable, then adds, "And the sun god, who approves of this arrangement, keeps the tilt steady." *Fixed verdict:* Tomas explains the seasons; the sun-god sentence is not part of what explains them. *What the theory says (no ruling; the view recorded in the change list and accepted in the cross-examination of the third draft):* if the sentence restricts nothing, it is an idle commitment: adding or removing it leaves every support a support, it is load-bearing in none, and Tomas's candidate is an account exactly when it would be without it, which fits the fixed verdict. Say plainly that another reading exists: if the god is taken as a second piece that keeps the tilt steady, then either it matches, and is a redundant route that does count as part of what explains (against the fixed verdict), or it does not match its anchor, and piece-by-piece matching fails (and "Tomas explains" is at risk). Which reading applies turns on which pieces Tomas offers as doing the work. A telling that swaps the sun god for another god is not a rival (section 5).
- **The spring nobody mentioned** (O45). *Situation:* an account of why a door swings back holds two connected springs, each enough alone; the second is never described as doing anything until the first is removed, and then the author says it was always there. *Fixed verdict:* the second spring was in the account from the start, connected and sufficient; it was already a route; nobody saying it did work does not make it a later addition. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees (file 10 could be read two ways, so this was one of the two changes toward the fixed verdict between file 10 and file 11). The two springs are redundant routes; a route present from the start is a route whether or not anyone described its work; and draft 4 says the commitments are the pieces offered as doing the work "whether or not anyone has described their work", so the verdict now rests on firmer text. The second spring is not idle: removed from the support in which it is the only spring, it leaves no support.

**Changed from file 11.** Draft 4 drops file 11's lemma counting passing variants over "explanatory jobs" and its definition of reach; it says the four requirements have no rule that each commitment works, with the two-sided test for an idle commitment, applied only in a candidate that has a support; it says the commitments of a cut-down candidate are those it keeps, and that the supports judged are groups of the written commitments; it separates a route from a route that ran, and credits by routes that ran.

**Departure.** Deutsch counts superfluous details as a defect of an explanation; here an idle commitment is load-bearing in no support, and nothing grades a candidate for carrying one.

**Not settled.** The two readings of the sun god, above. The cut-down operation and the stated family of changes behind the tipping edge are used but not listed among the stated inputs of Part XIV. If you want idle parts counted as a defect, that would be a change of claim; the text now says they are not.

### Section 5 — Rivals, problems and tests: what "easy to vary" means here (1,700 words)

**Covers.** Part VI, "Rivals" (L315) and "Problems" (L317). Part IX, "Standing" (L387–393), "What a test refutes" (L395) and "Receipts" (L397), because "established" rests on them. Derivation 2 (L560–568). Your decision of 24–25 September on hard to vary. The sources note's departures on hard to vary and reach.

**Ideas, in order.**
1. The unused joint setting, told first (example 1).
2. Rivals: two candidates for one question, one offered in place of the other, that conflict at some allowed setting of the target that both links carry over, inside the range or outside it. They conflict at a setting when their answers there differ, or when each could match there under some behaviour of the target the physics allows and no such behaviour lets both match (as when two of their pieces with one anchor behave differently there).
3. What a candidate offers: offered for a question, it claims matching and the right answer at every setting of the range, tried or not, and the rest of the requirements; outside the range it claims nothing on that question, but what its set-up gives there can still conflict with another's.
4. What is not a rival: a rewriting of the same candidate (one carried onto the other by a one-to-one mapping that takes the link along and leaves the answers as they are) conflicts nowhere; nor do two candidates cut differently that some behaviour of the target would let both match at every shared setting. Mention the dog and the turtle that hold up the universe (N25): nothing anyone could see or do depends on which, so they are one account written two ways, and, in the fixed verdict's words, "the difference between them is idle". Nor is it a rival to add an idle piece (the sun god), to state a piece twice, or to say less without contradicting.
5. No list of all rivals: a candidate's rivals are among those someone has offered, and a candidate nobody has offered is no one's rival. This is your decision: nobody can list all rivals, even in principle. Rivals are not a selection pool either (Derivation 3, section 7).
6. Established and fits: a result is established for a judge who holds a usable receipt for it. A receipt is a chain of reasoning over evidence items (each a pointer to an event with what it is taken to show); receipts for and against are exchanged by negation; missing evidence stays missing; a record reconstructed from the claim it is meant to support is not a receipt for that claim. An argument is usable when the judge is entitled to it, it applies here, and every assumption it needs is still held; withdrawing an assumption removes the entitlement and does not make the conclusion false. Blame is shared: a failed prediction counts against the claim tested, the test's other assumptions and its instruments together, and nothing narrower. A candidate fits what is established when no established result shows it failing a requirement.
7. A problem: two rivals that both fit what a judge has established, so that what is established has not decided between them. Two sorts:
   - a problem inside the range: they conflict at a setting the range covers. Whatever the target does there, at most one is an account, whether or not anyone can find out. Finding out what the target does there (its behaviour, and its answer where their answers agree) is a test that solves the problem whatever it shows; an answer it refutes stays refuted (section 6);
   - a problem outside the range: they conflict only at settings the range does not cover. Their answers agree everywhere in the range, so no established answer holds one to account without the other; a test inside the range can refute one without the other only for a failure of its own; and where both meet the requirements, both are accounts of the question.
8. Easy to vary: a candidate is easy to vary when it and a rival pose a problem outside the range; the rival is then easy to vary too, and the words say nothing about which is right. A criticism that a candidate is easy to vary must supply such a rival: the criticism is the rival (example 2). The theory does not define "hard to vary".
9. The cure is a finer range. A claim that one of two accounts is right and the other wrong is a claim that some allowed change outside the range separates them, and must name it. A finer range that holds such a change makes a different question, on which the two, while both fit, pose a problem inside the range. Whether a candidate is an account of a question is fixed by the candidate, the question and the world, not by when anyone first asks the question or whether anyone has checked.
10. A range narrowed to leave out the settings where two rivals conflict makes a different question and solves nothing on the old one; the problem stands until what is established leaves at most one fitting.
11. Nothing here counts rivals, grades a candidate or ranks candidates. A problem the system pictures can be a noticed difficulty (section 9), for instance when choosing one of two rivals meets a demand only by dropping the other, which fits as well, where keeping every rival that fits is a safeguard.
12. Same anchors, one account (Derivation 2): two candidates that both match and answer on the range give the same answers there; if they also tie their pieces to the same anchors, on the same slots, each pair of pieces is one kind and, as far as matching and answering reach, they are one account. Without the same anchors nothing more follows: candidates cut differently are different candidates with the same answers. A claim that one way of tying pieces to anchors is "really" right must name a separating change, which makes a finer range and a different question. Say plainly: files 10 and 11 said more ("indistinguishable is identical"), the defects round found that false as written, both outside readers agreed, and draft 4 narrows it (the consequence for the defeat list is in section 11).

**Worked examples.**
- **The unused joint setting** (O24). *Situation:* two wiring arrangements agree on every tested setting and on almost every untested one, and differ at a single setting of two switches together that nobody has used and that can be reached by hand. *Fixed verdict:* they are two arrangements, and the record has not chosen between them; one reachable setting is enough. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees (on the old Derivation 2's remark that a claim that two candidates really differ must name a change that separates them). Draft 4 says it directly, on this view of the text: the two are rivals that conflict at a setting inside the range; both fit what is established; that is a problem inside the range; and setting the two switches together is a test that solves it whichever way it comes out.
- **A farmer's rule carried south** (N5, a proposed case). *Situation:* a village rule, "sow after the first autumn rains, in October, and it ripens in June", works for generations; a family moves far south of the equator, where the rains come in April and May, and means to farm by it. *Fixed verdict:* the rule tells him little or nothing he can rely on; read by months it misleads, read by seasons it points to April or May; the rule itself cannot say which reading to trust; he needs why it worked, or trial. *What the theory says (no ruling; the view recorded for draft 4):* the two readings, by months and by seasons, are rivals. At home they give the same answer at every setting of the home question, so they pose a problem outside the range, and each is easy to vary relative to the other: nothing at home decides. The cure is a finer question that includes the move south, where the two conflict inside the range; a season's trial in the south is a test that solves it whichever way it comes out.

**Changed from file 11.** Two new paragraphs, "Rivals" and "Problems", replace file 11's counting lemma, its "reach" and its sentence that the count "grades nothing"; Derivation 2 is narrowed to "same anchors, one account".

**Departures.** Deutsch judges ease of variation before any rival is offered, and would reject a bad explanation without experiment; here it is shown only by offering the rival. He holds that an explanation which could easily explain anything explains nothing; here a candidate with such a rival is an account of its question if it meets the requirements, and a finer question is where a test decides. Here "easy to vary" is relative to the question and applies to both rivals: on the question of the seasons the Greeks knew, the tilt theory is easy to vary relative to the myth once the two are offered in place of each other. Myths that conflict at no allowed change, such as the Persephone and Freyr myths, are not rivals at all. "Reach" is not defined.

**Not settled.** None of this has been seen by the outside readers. "Easy to vary" points at neither rival; if you want it to point at one, a further requirement is needed, and it risks becoming a grade. Your own example (Demeter grieves, against Persephone is underground) gives rivals only if the target allows a change that sets the in-between state on its own, such as a veil of dust dimming the sun; in a model without such a change they are not rivals. What the physics allows at a setting is not listed among the stated inputs. Whether a failure that can be derived from the candidate alone (an assumed answer, a piece tied to nothing) counts as established is not said, and a myth tied to nothing poses a problem with the tilt theory or not according to this. Two partial claims, each exact about a different link, can be rivals outside the range. A candidate nobody has challenged is never called easy to vary, however loose it is. The paragraphs were tried only on small made-up worlds, and the building order does not yet place the new words.

**Where to find it.** Part VI L315–317; Part IX L387–397; Derivation 2 L560–568; decision S20; cases O24, N5 (and N25, N1 named).

### Section 6 — Narrowed questions, and why a failed answer stays failed (1,300 words)

**Covers.** Part III, "Scope, and a question that can be wrong", first paragraph (L159), and the sentence on changing the range or what is asked during an assessment (L161). Part V, "no dodging" (L257) and the silently narrowed range (L275), recalled from section 2. Part VIII, "Historical index" (L367) and "A failed answer stays failed" (L369). Derivation 7 (L602–608). Complaints 4 (L43) and 10 (L55). Part XIV's stated inputs, as they touch limits (L522). Part XV's sentence on missing inputs (L534).

**Ideas, in order.**
1. Greta's dough, told first (example 1).
2. A range is a stated selection from the possible changes, and a stated limit is what makes it one. An account at a stated limit answers the question asked at that limit; it does not answer a broader question that failed; a narrowing after a failure is a different claim in a different frame.
3. What makes a limit right for the question is part of the claim, open to criticism; the theory records the limit and has no rule that certifies it; meeting the four requirements on the narrowed range certifies nothing about the limit. Where the claim states its reason for the limit, a verdict on the limit is given with that reason; where it states none and a verdict turns on one, the reason is a missing input, and the verdict is unsettled.
4. Why this is not "anything goes" (complaint 4): physics fixes what is possible; a range is a stated selection of it; within a range there is a fact about matching. A limit that quietly leaves out possible changes to protect an account is caught by the demand that every exclusion be stated (no dodging), and then by any criticism that supplies the change left out.
5. Bruno's floods, told next (example 2), left open until idea 7.
6. A claim keeps its frame: a claim tied to a range stays that claim when a later theory changes the range in use.
7. A failed answer stays failed: at a setting of the range where the target's answer is not a given value, any candidate that gives that value there is not an account of the question, whoever offers it, whether it is the one that failed, the same one offered again, a rival, or a changed candidate that keeps that answer; the same holds for every question with the same target and what-is-asked whose range holds that setting. Once a judge has established the target's answer there, this is established of every such candidate alike, from its own answer there, with no record of who failed before or how anyone changed their candidate. If an assumption about the test's instruments stops being held, the exclusion stops being established for every candidate alike, and none is thereby shown to be an account. A range that leaves the setting out, or a changed what-is-asked, makes a different question; an account of it does not answer the first, whose failure stands. Return to Bruno.
8. Your decision (24–25 September): a corrected mistake cannot creep back in, and a record of rescues is redundant. Draft 4 now says why: the correction sticks on its question with no record of rescues, only a receipt of what the target does. Say what it does not reach (below).
9. Fixed judging, moving questions (Derivation 7, complaint 10): an assessment holds its range fixed; an episode can change ranges, each change with its history; "an account on the old range" and "not an account on the new" can both be true, because they are claims in different frames. Moving the goalposts is changing the frame without saying so, a failure of the record, not something the theory permits.
10. A case whose verdict turns on a stated input it does not state is a case with a missing input, not a defeat of the theory.

**Worked examples.**
- **Greta's dough** (O5). *Situation:* her rule says the dough doubles in two hours; it fails in January; she restates it for rooms above twenty degrees and says why (the yeast that makes the gas works slowly in the cold); "not on Tuesdays" would have nothing to say. *Fixed verdict:* a legitimate narrowing; the limit follows from a piece of her explanation. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees. Say plainly that this ruling was contested: four of five readings found the theory silent, one outside reader called the ruling wrong, and a fresh re-reading upheld it at medium confidence. Draft 4 now says outright that a verdict on a limit is given with the reason the claim states: Greta states one, tied to a piece of her account, and "not on Tuesdays" states none. Her narrowed claim is still a different question; her January failure stands on the first.
- **The forecaster who keeps finding honest limits** (O1). *Situation:* Bruno's flood rule fails in March, and he limits it to months outside the thaw; it fails after a dam release, and he limits it to days with no release; it fails again, and he limits it to days with a west wind; each limit is honest and checkable, each restated rule has been right so far, and he records every failure. *Fixed verdict:* no explanation of the floods; each limit honest alone; the series as a whole a retreat. *What the theory says:* the round found files 10 and 11 silent. On draft 4's view of the text (not yet tried in a round), the first half now follows: each restated rule narrows the range, so it is a different question that does not answer when the stream floods, and the flood question's failures stand, so Bruno has no account of the floods. The second half does not: nothing in the theory judges a series of narrowings, and his limits state no reason, so a verdict on them lacks a stated input and stays unsettled.

**Changed from file 11.** Draft 4 says that meeting the requirements on a narrowed range does not certify the limit, and that a verdict on a limit comes with the reason the claim states; it adds the paragraph "A failed answer stays failed"; and Part XV now says a case with a missing input is not a defeat.

**Departure.** Both source books hold that a limit of scope needs its own explanation; here a stated limit is recorded, not certified.

**Not settled.** Bruno's "retreat" is not reached. The failed-answer paragraph does not reach settings other than the one that failed, runs of rescues, changes of level of detail, or a coarser what-is-asked settled by the same receipt. "The stated reason for a limit" is not defined, and the text never says it is the same thing as the "grounds" of a criticism. Say plainly: your decision says a record of rescues is redundant; the analysis of 24 September found that a record still does one job, telling a retreat from a limit honest from the start, and draft 4 does not settle that. The new paragraph has not been seen by the outside readers.

### Section 7 — Where links come from: selection, pictures and surprise (1,800 words)

**Covers.** Part 0, L13 and complaints 3 (L41), 6 (L47) and 9 (L53). Part I, "Two provenances, not one" (L77). Part IV, "Occurrences and contents" (L167–169), "The primitive layer and the simulation layer" (L171–179), "Three provenances" (L191–201), "Representation is derived" (L203–213), "Expectation, surprise, violation" (L215–225). Part VIII, "Recoding" (L365). Part XII, "Selection in the physical module" (L481). Derivations 3 (L570–576), 4 (L578–584), 8 (L610–612) and 10 (L618–632). Part XV, the main claim about the two histories (L542).

**Ideas, in order.**
1. The discarded lamp controller, told first (example 1), left open until idea 7.
2. Carriers and contents: a carrier is a physical thing or event that bears content; a content is a set-up with what it claims on its range. Carriers are not the same because they carry the same words; contents are not the same because they give the same outputs.
3. Two layers: the thing layer, where there are lasting things with edges and identity (its pieces are the threads that keep each thing the same thing; its changes are moving, hiding, telling which is which again), and the expectation layer, the dependences among things, where predictions are made. Neither is assumed to exist in any real system; the theory says what it would be for them to exist and be linked.
4. Three histories of a link. Selected: a pool, variation, trials (the settings actually met), and survival (being in the pool and matching on every trial); nothing in the trials pictures the link, the trials, or what counts as survival. Constructed: an episode whose construction evidence prepares the link, in which the link or its target is pictured. Declared: written in by the modeller; no creativity claim may rest on it. Neither of the first two reduces to the other: selection has no pictured target and no criticism in its history, construction has both. The expected arrangement is selected links at the thing layer and constructed links at the expectation layer, but it is not required.
5. A picture: a carrier pictures a content at a level of detail when the set-up it embodies has a link to the content that matches on the content's range, with a history of selection or construction. A declared link makes no picture, only a modeller's assertion. A system can picture a false theory: the link from carrier to content matches while the content's link to the world fails. A carrier keeps its history when present access to it is lost, and a later record worked out from the carrier is not a second, independent witness. The one thing taken from outside here is which set-up a physical carrier embodies at a level: that comes from the physics.
6. Expectation, miss, surprise: for a link to the expectation layer, at a setting that actually happens, the expectation is what the layer gives; a miss is the link failing to match there; surprise is a miss by a selected link at an untried setting. With no link there is no surprise; with trials covering the whole range there is no surprise. Expectation and miss apply to every link, surprise only to selected ones: a constructed link that fails is missed, not surprised; a miss the system pictures can be a noticed difficulty.
7. Survival leaves open only what the pool leaves open (Derivation 3): at an untried setting, the trials leave a selected link's value open only where the pool holds another survivor that behaves differently there; where it does not, the pool fixes the value. The pool is the links the physics and the stated design allow, and a link that would need something every member of the pool is built without is not in it. So a selected link matches where it was tried and, wherever its pool allows an alternative, is unconstrained where it was not; that is why the thing layer can be wrong and why surprise is possible. What is given up is the older blanket claim that every untried value is open. Return to the lamp controller.
8. Surprise needs untried settings (Derivation 4): a system can be surprised only if it holds a selected link whose trials are strictly fewer than the settings the world allows. Two responses: re-tuning (add the setting to the trials and let variation adjust the link within its pool) and response by construction (a fresh set-up or link, with construction evidence). Re-tuning is learning; construction is creating; only construction can originate (section 8).
9. The hidden-thing episode (Derivation 10), in words: a row of cells; two things with positions and speeds; the senses show only which cells are filled. A selected expectation layer, tried on moves and speed changes but never on hiding, predicts from recently filled cells. A thing goes behind a screen; the layer expects nothing there, and is missed when the thing reappears where its speed says it should: surprise. Re-tuning cannot help if every member of the pool predicts from filled cells only; the failure is in the design, not in a setting. Construction: a fresh layer with a tracker for each thing, carrying position and speed through hiding, tied to each thing's thread; the trackers have the response pattern of things, and nobody declared them "objects". If built by an owned stretch with a real new join, and new, that is an origination; if it repairs "have a usable account of reappearance" while keeping "predict moves correctly", it is a repair and created knowledge (sections 8 and 9). The swap: swapping the two things changes nothing the senses show; tying each tracker to the other thing gives a second link with the same matching and answers, so as far as matching and answering reach the two are one account; "tracker one is really thing one" needs a separating change, and there is none here: at this level, identity is nothing more than path. The episode is a proof by example that the theory hangs together, not a claim about any infant, animal or program.
10. The second example, Nadia and the cards, placed after idea 9 as the everyday twin of the episode.
11. Recoding and rewriting: a stated, invertible recoding of a carrier keeps its content when a reader who applies the stated key recovers every pairing; rewriting everything (carriers, links, histories, ranges) by a one-to-one mapping that keeps every connection keeps accounts, originations, repairs and created knowledge (Derivation 8), but not blurrings, changed boundaries or lost event identities. A stretch of a carrier filled in from another source keeps that other source's history, whatever it happens to agree with.
12. The complaints answered: survival is not truth (survival is how a link got there; matching on changes it never met is a separate fact); selection appears once, at the bottom, and construction is never reduced to it; "selected" is a physical history, checkable in principle, not a label.
13. What would defeat this (section 11 in full): a selected link fixed at an untried setting by its trials although its pool holds a differing survivor; a showing that every construction can be rewritten as selection without loss; a showing that the thing layer is not what explanation works on.

**Worked examples.**
- **The discarded lamp controller** (D3-T, a proposed case). *Situation:* each lamp controller has switches A and B; a tester keeps a controller whose lamp is dark with A off and lit with A on, with B off, and throws out the rest; only two designs exist, both buildable; the tester never tries A and B both on, and the two designs differ there; one design failed (dark with A on) and was thrown out; Ivo points to it as proof that passing leaves open what a controller does with both on. *Fixed verdict:* no (high confidence); the thrown-out design failed at a tried setting, so it says nothing about controllers that pass; Ivo would need two designs that both pass and still differ. *What the theory says (no ruling; the view recorded for draft 4):* this is a selection with a pool of two designs and trials at the tried settings. The thrown-out design did not survive. By Derivation 3, the untried setting is left open only where the pool holds a differing survivor, and it holds none, so passing tells you which design you have and what it does with both switches on. Draft 4 adds a second reason: any design whose answer at the tried setting is the failed one fails there (section 6). Call the tester's settings "trials", never "tests".
- **Nadia and the cards she has never seen** (O3). *Situation:* Nadia learns a card game only by watching cards laid down, predicts the next card from those just played and is often surprised, then starts keeping a tally of cards "waiting somewhere", and her predictions improve sharply. *Fixed verdict:* she built something new, a piece that stands for cards she has never seen; that is construction, and it is her own. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees: a response by construction (a fresh set-up with a piece for unseen cards), and a first picture may be constructed from a challenge at hand without ever having observed what it pictures. Point out what draft 4 changes: whether her earlier misses were surprises in the theory's sense depends on whether her earlier habit came about by selection; the case does not say, and the verdict does not turn on it. ("Her own" is taken up in section 10: no boundary is stated.)

**Changed from file 11.** Draft 4 defines surviving the trials; defines expectation and miss for every link and keeps surprise for selected ones, a constructed link that fails being missed, not surprised; describes the pool as the candidate links the physics and the stated design allow, and restates Derivation 3's requirement in those terms; routes Derivation 10's swap through an exchange of the two things (and drops its remark on Derivation 3); and Part 0 now says selection's history holds no pictured target (a clarification).

**Departures.** Deutsch calls criticism and experiment a kind of selection; here selection is blind, with no pictured target in its history. Deutsch's "problem" (conflicting ideas, which can arise with no observation) is wider than surprise; here surprise is kept for selected links, and his wider problem is a noticed difficulty (section 9).

**Not settled.** If a programmer designed the pool, success at untried settings is the programmer's knowledge, and the theory cannot yet say so. With its pool requirement Derivation 3 is close to true by its own words, and so hard to defeat. Nobody has tried to rewrite a construction as a selection without loss. The lamp-controller case has not been used in a round. Nothing tests whether the thing layer is what explanation works on.

**Where to find it.** Part 0 L13, L41, L47, L53; Part I L77; Part IV L165–225; Part VIII L365; Part XII L481; Part XV L542; Derivations 3, 4, 8, 10 (L570–584, L610–612, L618–632); cases D3-T, O3.

### Section 8 — Building something new: construction, understanding and finding questions (1,500 words)

**Covers.** Part 0, L15. Part III, "Contracts have provenance" (L153–155), recalled. Part X from "Deployment" through "Origin" (L403–425): understanding, construction, unspoken pictures, construction is not selection, newness, origination. Derivation 5 (L586–592). Part XV, the main claim about finding questions (L544). Part X's "Ownership" (L427) goes to section 10 and "Episodes" (L429) to section 9.

**Ideas, in order.**
1. The apprentice and the third lock, told first (example 1).
2. Understanding: a system understands a content when it holds a picture of it (a matching link with a history of selection or construction), uses it in its work on challenges, and keeps the ability to use it for a stated use task. Its stock is the contents it understands for some real use. A system may understand a false theory. A narrow lasting use is what it is: it shows neither the wider understanding it falls short of nor a lasting inability to reach it (one line on the phrasebook case, O14, allowed).
3. Construction: an actual stretch owned by the system, ending at a stated point, that prepares a picture for explanatory use, contains a real new join relevant to that use, and is not just a chain of passings-on that keep content as it was. Construction evidence names the processes, what came in, the joins made and the resulting picture. Reconstruction by a learner is construction; relay is not. A first picture can be constructed from a challenge at hand without having observed what it pictures. A small new join inside received content is construction of that join, and the rest keeps its inherited history (one line on the one-tooth case, O18, allowed).
4. Unspoken pictures: a pianist imagines a passage without playing it; a geometer handles a spatial relation without naming every piece; an investigator notices an inconsistency before saying what it rests on. The theory asks whether the distinctions are there, with matching and a history, and used where use is asked, not whether they are written in some format. Pictures can be partial, spread out or extended in time; keeping a criticized item in mind can be being able to bring back the distinction. Construction evidence may pick out a join by its use, as using an objection does (section 9). Use alone does not construct: received content used as received keeps its inherited history, and passing it on without such use is relay.
5. Construction is not selection: a selected link has no pictured target in its history; a constructed one does. A system can show both; the evidence differs (example 1).
6. New: two contents are equivalent at a level of detail when links run both ways between them, each matching on the content's range; this is sameness of structure, not of words or looks. A content is new to a system when nothing in its stock before was equivalent to it.
7. Origination: the system used the content on the question, it was new to the system, and the system constructed it. The content may be a set-up, a link or a range; when it is a range, originating it is finding a question. A matter mentioned in passing is not thereby a slot of the account; it becomes one when the account allows changes to it, and adding it is construction (one line on "The seal was tight", O23, allowed).
8. Finding a question (Derivation 5): a range with what is asked can itself be treated as a set-up (its slots are the changes and circumstances; its pieces the rules that close it; its changes adding or removing a change, or altering what is asked), so understanding, construction and newness apply to it. Finding the right question is then creative on the same footing as answering one. A theory that takes questions as given cannot say this; this one can, because ranges have histories. That a question was found says nothing about its worth (example 2).
9. What would defeat this: a showing that treating a range as content either cheapens creativity or misses a real case of finding the right question.

**Worked examples.**
- **The apprentice and the third lock** (O11). *Situation:* on Monday she tries keys from a ring until one opens the lock; on Tuesday, at a new lock, she feels where a blank catches and files it until it turns; on Wednesday, at a third lock, she picks a key from the ring at once, saying it looks like Tuesday's. *Fixed verdict:* Monday is blind fitting; Tuesday is making something new; Wednesday is neither, but reuse. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees. Monday is selection: a pool (the ring), trials and survival, with no picture of the lock needed. Tuesday is construction: a real new join made to a picture of what the lock needs, and new to her. Wednesday is not new: the key is equivalent to something already in her stock, so she uses it on the question without originating anything.
- **The rota** (O12). *Situation:* a team argues for weeks about who is lazy; Noor asks which tasks have no owner; the arguing stops and the work gets done; nobody had said every task should have an owner. *Fixed verdict:* Noor found a better question, and its merit is real, though the need it met was recognised only afterwards. *What the theory says:* the round found files 10 and 11 silent, on the point of merit only. The theory reaches "found a question": if Noor constructed the range, it was new to her, and she used it on the team's challenge, she originated a question. Whether it was better and its merit real is a claim of worth, which the theory takes from the standard of worth, from outside; the case supplies none, so that half is unsettled. Draft 4 now says so in Part 0.

**Changed from file 11.** Draft 4 adds the paragraphs on unspoken pictures and on a join identified by its use, with the guard that use alone does not construct; and it defines when two contents are equivalent.

**Departure.** Deutsch holds that grasping any idea takes conjecture; here not every acquisition is construction: reconstruction is, relay is not. The close parallels with Deutsch on relay and on unspoken knowledge are named in the sources note and not claimed as derived.

**Not settled.** Counting a new question as creative may cheapen creativity: any question that was constructed, new and used counts, and its worth is outside the theory. An outside reader called the proof of Derivation 5 "a sketch, not a proof" while finding nothing false; the project agreed a piece is missing and counted no defeat, since the defeat list names only Derivations 1 to 3. The text never says in words when a link into a content matches on the content's range, which the definition of equivalent needs.

**Where to find it.** Part 0 L15; Part III L153–155; Part X L403–425; Part XV L544; Derivation 5 L586–592; cases O11, O12 (and O14, O18, O23 named).

### Section 9 — Criticism, repair and created knowledge (1,400 words)

**Covers.** Part I, "Conjecture, criticism, action" (L71). Part IX, "Histories" and the route that ran (L375), "Bearing" (L377–383), "Reason use" (L385). Part X, "Episodes" (L429). Part XI, "Repair" (L435–441), "Created explanatory knowledge" (L443–453), "Worth, and the normative relation" (L455). Complaint 8 (L51). Part 0 L25, as it touches worth and credit. Part IX's standing, blame and receipts went to section 5.

**Ideas, in order.**
1. The two plumbers, told first (example 1).
2. The course of events: events in an order that never loops back, with a physical reading of what happened. A route that ran is a connected cluster of actual events joining a pictured input to an outcome, whose pieces behaved as they should, and through which the pictured difference actually passed. A route that started and did no work, or had already stopped when the outcome came, did not run for that outcome; this is read from the course of events, not from the outcome.
3. Criticism: a criticized item, an alleged fault, grounds, and a connection from grounds to fault. It bears on its item when the connection, taken as a candidate for the fault question (does the item have that fault, in respect of the original question?), is an account. A criticism can exist without bearing. A warning sign is not a criticism until something pictures it as grounds for an alleged fault in an item.
4. Using an objection: a response uses an objection when the objection shapes the response in the right way (its roles kept; a mere recoding of the objection giving the same response; a change in its content giving the change the response's own rule calls for) and lies on a route that ran. Using an invalid objection does not make it valid. Ideas may be entertained without proof; a criticism is itself a guess; a thinker may act on a view without proving it.
5. Episodes: an episode of criticism holds a noticed difficulty, an item that was there before it was criticized, an objection, and a response that depends on what the objection says. A noticed difficulty is a failed demand, or a conflict in which what the system holds meets one demand only by breaking a safeguard, when the system pictures it. A creative episode holds an origination connected to its inquiry. Closing an episode is a decision, not a proof.
6. Repair: a contribution is a stretch of events with the changes of content it makes. Demands (what is to be repaired) and safeguards (what must keep holding) are stated inputs, each stated with the occasions it covers; a safeguard is lost when it fails on any occasion it covers. A repair: some demand failed before and holds after; every safeguard that held before held on every occasion in between; and the repair was produced by the contribution. Stating demands claims nothing about their worth, and the theory does not rank alternatives. Losses outside the safeguards must be exposed.
7. Produced by: a route that ran joins the contribution to the repair. Credit goes to each contribution the course of events shows; where two sufficient contributions both ran, both are credited, and the course of events gives no sharing of credit it does not contain (example 2). Three attributions stay apart: a correct account that produced nothing; an act that repaired without an account; a repair produced through the use of an account.
8. Created knowledge: a creative episode and a repair, where a demand for knowledge (that a correct account, or the correction of a use through one, be understood) failed before and holds after, through a content the system originated, that is an account of its question, that the contribution prepared, that the system understands afterwards, and whose join lies on the route that did the repair. The account's range is the one fixed when it was originated; a later narrowing to rescue it is a different claim and does not count.
9. Worth (complaint 8): repairing a demand shows it was repaired; it shows nothing about whether the demand, or the question behind it, was worth having. Where a claim invokes worth, the theory takes the standard of worth from outside and marks the place. The artistic case: an effect, a purpose, the purpose achieved (the effects fall inside the aim), and artistic value taken as an input. None is defined as another; no view of beauty follows from achieving a stated effect.

**Worked examples.**
- **Two plumbers** (O13). *Situation:* Ana inspects a leak, says the washer has cracked and explains why that lets water past; before she can replace it, Ben tightens a nearby joint at random, and the leak stops because the tightening shifted the washer back into place. *Fixed verdict:* Ben's act stopped the leak; Ana's explanation is correct and produced nothing; nobody made a repair that came from an explanation. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees, on the three attributions: Ana holds a correct account that produced nothing; Ben made a repair without an account; so there is no created knowledge here. Unchanged in substance in draft 4.
- **Two valves in one second** (O20). *Situation:* Ana and Ben each close a different valve within the same second; the leak stops; the records are complete, and either valve alone would have stopped it; each claims the repair. *Fixed verdict:* both did something that would have stopped it; neither can claim it alone; there is no way to say whose water stopped. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees (and corrected the older baseline for file 10 from silent to agrees). Both routes ran, so the repair is produced by both contributions; both are credited; and the course of events contains no sharing of credit, so the theory gives none.

**Changed from file 11.** Draft 4 defines the fault question and the connection of a criticism; says a warning sign becomes a criticism when pictured as grounds for an alleged fault; defines a noticed difficulty; defines a contribution; says a safeguard must hold on every occasion it covers, not only at the end; defines "what the contribution prepared" and "produced through the account"; and makes the standard of worth one of the two foundations, taken from outside, not a stated input.

**Departures.** Deutsch holds that there are objective truths in aesthetics; the theory abstains, taking worth as an input and deriving no view of beauty.

**Not settled.** The theory stays silent on "Four seconds" (O35), because the safeguard's occasions are not stated (every moment of the work, or only moments when someone draws water), and on the three cases whose fixed verdicts say an achievement was "mostly" one party's (O21, O27, O50), because that needs a weighting of credit the cases do not state. The building order does not place what a route that ran depends on (its stated contrasts).

**Where to find it.** Part 0 L25, L51; Part I L71; Part IX L375–385; Part X L429; Part XI L435–455; cases O13, O20 (and O35, O21, O27, O50 named).

### Section 10 — Owning an ability: boundaries, tasks, barriers and universality (1,300 words)

**Covers.** Part 0, L27. Part I, "Recursive scrutiny" (L73) and "Substrate independence" (L75). Part X, "Ownership" (L427). Part XII, "Tasks" (L461), lasting performance (L463–469), staying able (L471), "System boundary and continuity" (L473), "Owned capability" (L475), "Achievement" (L477), "Grades" (L479). Part XIII in full (L485–509).

**Ideas, in order.**
1. The technician's whisper, told first (example 1).
2. Ownership: a stretch is the system's own when its processes run inside the boundary and resource terms stated for the system. Work supplied from outside the boundary (a diagnosis, a decisive question, an instruction about what to read) stays an outside contribution however it is carried out inside; a process run inside the boundary is the system's own whoever wrote it; where the boundary is drawn decides, not where a process sits in the casing. Credit for content and ownership of a process are different: a routine written outside and run inside is the system's process, and its content is still its writer's contribution. Ownership is not defined by the ability it is meant to ground.
3. Boundary and sameness rule: an ability is attributed under a stated boundary (which processes and resources are the system's) and a stated sameness rule (what makes it the same system through change). A replaced board that keeps the stated sameness leaves the same system. Both are stated before the attribution, not chosen after it. (One line on Nadia, section 7: the case states no boundary, and the ruling read "her own" in the ordinary sense of a person working alone; readers noted this as a live contrary.) Where the boundary is not stated outright, a statement that names the system and says whether a process runs inside or outside it states the boundary for that process; a statement that the process is the system's own, without saying where it runs, is the attribution, not its boundary.
4. Tasks: a physical thing, its properties, and a task (from a stated input property to a stated output property, with resources and side effects stated). Possible means that no law of physics stops the task being done as well as one likes, short of perfect, both in the doing and in the doer staying able; one success is not possibility. The theory adopts a task-based way of stating physics (the sources note traces its wording to Deutsch's 2013 paper on constructor theory).
5. Lasting performance: every carrying-out of the procedure, from any start where the doer is ready, finishes with a right output and leaves the doer ready again; there must be carrying-outs, and getting stuck is not success. Staying able: the widest condition of the doer from which it always comes back.
6. Ability: an owned lasting performance, or an owned, possible, finite way of making one, under the same sameness rule and resource terms. A theorist's description of a procedure is not the system's having it. "Owned because it can, and can because owned" grounds neither (section 11).
7. Able to advance: for each stated starting point, every carrying-out ends with the challenge advanced and the system still in shape. A first discovery is not a repeatable task; what must last is the set-up that makes inquiry possible.
8. Degrees of accuracy: what the physics allows at each degree, what the system can do at each degree (never more than the physics allows), and what is possible (achievable at every degree). Ability at some degree does not imply possibility at every degree.
9. Substrate independence (Part I): any carrier may bear a set-up, and every attribution must be allowed by the physics; which sorts of matter can bear which set-ups, and whether contents pass between sorts, is fixed by the physics. Where two sorts cannot exchange what they bear, the contents only one can bear form a barrier for a system built of the other (example 2).
10. Reviewable, and criticism at any depth: an aspect of a system's practice (a question, a range, a link, a method, what it attends to) is reviewable when an owned way forward makes it pictured, criticized and able to change how it is used. Criticism at any depth: for every finite chain of such items, the system has an owned way to go on.
11. Barriers: a domain, described on its own terms, that every allowed fair aid leaves out of reach. A finite list of failures is not a proof of a barrier (recall the machine case, section 1); a way round refutes a proposed barrier.
12. The universal class: systems with criticism at any depth that can, with a fair aid, understand every explanatory content and advance every well-posed challenge, both lists fixed independently of the system. Criticism at any depth does not give universality; a growing history does not certify it; a finite record does not establish it. The theory shows no one to belong, human, machine or institution (Part 0).

**Worked examples.**
- **The technician's whisper** (O41). *Situation:* an assessment is stated at the start as robot-only; midway a technician tells the robot which gauge to read; the robot reads it and completes the diagnosis; the log records the instruction. *Fixed verdict:* the boundary was robot-only and the instruction crossed it; the diagnosis is a team result; robot-only credit is not available. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees, resting on sentences file 11 added: an instruction about what to read, supplied from outside the boundary, stays an outside contribution however it is carried out inside, and the boundary is stated before the attribution, not chosen after it. (The ruling kept file 10 at "agrees" too, so this is not a change between versions; do not call it one.)
- **Two sealed sorts of matter** (N24, a proposed case). *Situation:* a universe has two sorts of matter that pass through each other without effect; each has a computer that can do any calculation its sort allows, and some calculations are possible only in one sort; Asha says the two computers together are a machine for any calculation the laws allow; Bram says no machine can be, since a question written in one sort can never be read by a computer of the other. *Fixed verdict:* Bram is right; his reason largely explains why, together with the stated fact that each sort allows calculations the other cannot do. *What the theory says (no ruling; the view recorded for draft 4):* draft 4's Part I says substrate independence holds only as far as the physics lets contents pass between sorts of matter, and that where two sorts cannot exchange what they bear, the contents only one can bear form a barrier for a system built of the other; Asha's weak sense and Bram's claim ask different things, so they are not rivals.

**Changed from file 11.** Draft 4 drops "today" from ownership; separates credit for content from ownership of a process; says how a boundary is stated when it is not stated outright; defines the degrees of accuracy and the three lists they give; defines a fair aid; and limits substrate independence to what the physics lets pass between sorts of matter.

**Departures.** Part I's limit on substrate independence matches Marletto's principle of exchange between sorts of matter (a parallel, named, not claimed as derived). Deutsch holds that people are universal explainers; the theory defines the universal class and shows no one to belong. Marletto defines knowledge as information that can keep itself embodied in physical systems; the theory leaves that out, as an attribution of the physics outside its class.

**Not settled.** Two terms in the definition of the universal class, the use task for each content and the task of advancing each challenge, are not defined. The lists of explanatory contents and well-posed challenges are to be fixed independently of the system, and the theory does not say how. The sealed-matter case has not been used in a round.

**Where to find it.** Part 0 L27; Part I L73–75; Part X L427; Part XII L461–479; Part XIII L485–509; cases O41, N24 (and O30, O42, O17, O51 named).

### Section 11 — What the theory rests on, and how it could be refuted (1,200 words)

**Covers.** Part 0, "What is primitive, what is an index, and what is derived" (L29–31), complaint 5 (L45), "Where to attack this" (L59–61), and the central guess (L17) recalled. Part XIV in full (L513–528). Part XV in full (L532–546). Derivation 6 (L594–600).

**Ideas, in order.**
1. The uncited textbook, told first (example 1).
2. Two foundations: the physics (states and properties of physical things, allowed processes, what counts as controlled action, resources, degrees of accuracy, and which set-up a physical carrier embodies at a level of detail) and the standard of worth (only where a question invokes worth; taken as input and never derived).
3. Frames and stated inputs. Frames (level of detail, boundary, sameness rule, range) are what a claim is relative to; none is true or false. Stated inputs are what a claim must state and the theory records without supplying: demands and safeguards with their occasions; the limit of a range and what makes it right; the boundary and sameness rule of an attribution; a weighting of credit beyond what the course of events contains. The boundary, the sameness rule and a range's limit are both frame values and stated inputs. A verdict that depends on one is given with it; where it is missing, the verdict is unsettled and the theory says so, rather than choosing the input to suit the verdict wanted. No probability of truth, merit score or ranking of thinkers is supplied; a claim that needs one is unsettled.
4. Everything else is defined from the foundations, the frames and the stated inputs (the list of L520: roles, kinds, sorts of question, pictures, histories, accounts, understanding, construction, newness, origination, repair, knowledge, ability, criticism at any depth, universality). Nothing rests on a basic term meaning "really explains", "is a cause" or "is knowledge" (Derivation 6). The two foundations are a theory of matter and, when a question needs it, a theory of reasons; neither is about explanation.
5. The building order: which definitions rest on which, with frames and stated inputs resting on nothing. It has no circles: a picture justified only by its own construction, or an ownership and an ability justified only by each other, has no place in it, and a separate proof that would supply the place counts only when the account uses it (example 1; one line on "Owned means capable", O49, allowed).
6. The classes, widest first: the base class (filled-in models with everything of the right type, allowed by the physics wherever a physical attribution is made); the creative-episode class; the knowledge-creation class; the criticism-at-any-depth class; the universal class. A single origination does not put its author in the universal class.
7. How it could be refuted, in the order of how much would fall. None is protected by notation, by this document existing, or by any version label.
   - Enough: a candidate meeting all four requirements on a range of possible changes, with a link that is not merely declared, that plainly explains nothing (with the known tricks and the requirement each fails, from section 2; a full response table counts only if it passes all four and still explains nothing).
   - Needed: a real explanation whose set-up no link can match on any range of possible changes (explanations of absence are the exposed case).
   - Kinds can be dropped: a label that tells apart two accounts no allowed change tells apart, where the difference does explanatory work.
   - Two histories: a selected link fixed at an untried setting by its trials although its pool holds a differing survivor; a showing that every construction can be rewritten as a selection without loss; a showing that the thing layer is not what explanation works on.
   - Finding a question: a showing that treating a range as content cheapens creativity or misses a real case of finding the right question.
   - A mathematical error: a counterexample to the smallest-supports theorem, the pinning-down rule, the blocking rule, the error build-up result, staying able, or Derivations 1 to 3 under their stated assumptions.
   - A case whose verdict turns on a stated input it does not state, or on the standard of worth, is a case with a missing input, not a refutation.
8. The record so far (example 2 and one paragraph): file 10's Derivation 3 was defeated by the forbidden-wire case and repaired in file 11; the old Derivation 2 was found false as written in files 10 and 11, so that entry of the defeat list stands triggered for both, and draft 4 narrows the claim (section 5); on file 11 the round gave the fixed verdict on 46 of 52 cases, silence on 6, and none against.
9. Complaint 5: the theory shares something with three older views (that what a sign stands for comes from a history of selection; that science captures structure; that a mind is defined by what it does, not what it is made of) and differs from each (selection and construction kept apart, with creativity only in construction; structure kept piece by piece and under change, with no demand for a global copy; physical obligations at every attribution). Whether the mix is new is a question for scholars.

**Worked examples.**
- **The uncited textbook** (O37). *Situation:* an account traces every picture to its starting basis except one, which it justifies by a later theorem whose proof in the account uses that same picture; a separate proof of the theorem exists in a textbook the account does not cite. *Fixed verdict:* as written, the order has a circle in it; the textbook proof would break the circle and has not been used; the order is not settled until it is. *What the theory says:* the round on file 11 found that it agrees: the building order has no circles, a picture justified only by its own construction has no place in it, and a separate proof that would supply the place counts only when the account uses it. Unchanged in draft 4 except that the building order now also places ownership, ability and repair.
- **The forbidden wire** (O48). *Situation:* the only arrangement that would respond differently at the untried setting needs a wire between two terminals, and every device in the stated pool is built without that wire. *Fixed verdict:* then there is no alternative in that pool; a different response is imaginable but not available; the premise is not met. *What the theory says:* file 10 said every untried setting is always left open, and the round ruled that file 10 disagrees: a counterexample to Derivation 3, which the defeat list counts as a defeat. File 11's one declared change required the differing link to be in the pool and to survive, and the round ruled that it agrees; draft 4 keeps this, with the pool defined as the links the physics and the stated design allow. Use it to show how the defeat list works, and its cost: the qualified claim is now close to true by its own words, and hard to defeat.

**Changed from file 11.** Draft 4 says everything else is derived from the foundations, the frames and the stated inputs; lists a weighting of credit among the stated inputs and applies the missing-input rule to the standard of worth; separates a frame from a stated input; says frames and stated inputs rest on nothing and places ownership, ability, produced by and the demands in the building order; says a case with a missing input is not a refutation; keeps the full response table inside the "enough" attack; and follows Derivation 6's proof through the fuller order.

**Not settled.** Draft 4 is not frozen and has not been tried on cases; its new paragraphs on rivals, problems and failed answers have not been seen by the outside readers. The building order is a summary: it leaves out several terms, including the new words of section 5. The cut-down operation and the stated family of changes behind the tipping edge are not listed among the stated inputs. The defeat list's entry for Derivation 2 stands triggered for files 10 and 11.

**Where to find it.** Part 0 L17, L29–31, L45, L59–61; Part XIV L513–528; Part XV L532–546; Derivation 6 L594–600; cases O37, O48 (and O49 named).

Search the text for every word in the sheet's "never appear" list, for "part" in lowercase, "condition", "version", "variant", "reach", "hard to vary" used as a theory term, "grade", any name of an AI model or its maker (the theory's title, once, in section 0, is the only exception), and any symbol or Greek letter. Only quotations and the "Where to find it" lines may hold codes.
2. Check that every case is named by its title in the body and that each example says which kind of verdict it reports (ruling on file 11, draft 4's view, or proposed case with no ruling).
3. Check each section has: an example before the first general statement; the "changed from file 11" lines; the "not settled" part; the "Where to find it" line.
4. Count the words: each section within 10 per cent of its target, the whole between 16,000 and 19,500.
5. Check that nothing contradicts your decision on hard to vary (rule 7).
