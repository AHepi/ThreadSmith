# Cold read 2 of "The theory in plain words"

I read "whole v2.md" once, start to finish, as a newcomer who has never seen the theory, then went back to check each place I marked. Line numbers point into "whole v2.md" and are only there to help the reviser find each place.

Each entry gives: where it is, a short quote, what confused me, and the question I wanted answered.

## Part 1. Every confusing place, in reading order

### The theory in one page

## Part 2. Words used for more than one thing, or one thing with several words

This gathers places already marked above, because they undercut the document's own promise of one word for each thing.

- **answer**: the target's answer, but also a candidate itself (Quentin's answer, Answer A and B, "a wrong answer ... whoever offers it"), and everyday "does not change the answer".
- **mark**: a reader's judgement, and also the ruling labels after each case.
- **objection**: the eleven complaints, and also the thing a response in an episode responds to.
- **record**: what has been observed; a list of past failures; "a failure of the record"; "put on the record".
- **trial**: the trial key, the farmer's "trial", and the trials of selection.
- **route**: a support (in the cases' wording), and a route that ran (events).
- **reading**: a slot, and the number shown.
- **task**: everyday tasks in the rota, and the physical task of section 11; also the "use task".

- **built / building**: construction, the building order, "building models", and designs that "can be built".
- **table**: the table of observed answers, the full response table, odd-sized tables, and the table with empty columns.

- **things / objects**.
- **modeller / author / theorist**.
- **established / known / found out**.
- **fixed verdict / agreed verdict**.

## Part 3. The three hardest parts

**1. Section 6: rivals, problems and "easy to vary".** This is where I lost my footing most. It stacks up many new words (offered, conflict, both links carry over, evidence item, receipt, usable, established, fits, problem inside and outside the range, test). It leans on sections I had not read yet (selection, pools, "what survival leaves open", safeguards). Its key examples need assumptions the cases do not make: "the case does not say so", twice. The Demeter and dust example could not be followed. And the end result unsettles me without resolving anything: "easy to vary" says nothing about which rival is right, the tilt of the Earth's axis can be "easy to vary relative to the myth", and the document never tells me plainly whether being easy to vary is a fault.

**2. Section 8: where links come from.** Carriers, contents, two layers, pictures, three histories, pools, trials, survival, expectation, miss, surprise and re-tuning all arrive in one section. "Picture", used since the first page, is only defined here. The layers have no concrete example and are then said to be claimed of no real system. The hidden thing is hard to picture, and its swap paragraph (line 674) chains three proofs in a way I could not follow. "Surprise" means something narrower than it does in everyday speech.

**3. Section 3: the third and fourth requirements, with section 5's supports.** The second half of the third requirement ("come out fixed and equal, or one of them is no longer fixed") and the fourth ("No dodging") are the core of the theory, and they are the hardest to read. The fourth requirement's name does not say what is dodged, its two conditions seem unrelated, the paragraph linking it to the third seems to apply a requirement to a range, the pendulum example does not seem to fit, and section 7 restates it differently. Salt being "background" was jarring. Section 5 then adds supports, load-bearing groups, smallest supports, redundant supports, interference, spread-out support and idle commitments. The springs help, but "idle ones can together be load-bearing" undid my grip.

A close fourth is **section 11 on tasks and "possible"**: "One success does not make a task possible" and the millimetre cut "not possible, though some system can do it" go against everyday meaning.

## Part 4. Did the one-page summary prepare me?

Partly.

**What worked.** The pole example is a good opening. The outline is in the right order. The village sowing rule and the two plumbers are exactly the right size for a summary. The last paragraph honestly says what is unsure.

**What did not work.**
- The page has about 25 bold words, too many to carry.
- It uses "link", "picture", "the physics", "embodied", "frame", "stated inputs" and "the universal class" without saying what they are.
- It does not name the four requirements, then introduces a second, different set of "four things".
- It mentions "the careful person" and "the two older texts" before either is introduced.
- It says a listed way of being shown wrong was "triggered" without saying whether that matters.
- The board example in its third paragraph was the first thing I could not picture.

**What it left me unready for.** It prepared me reasonably for sections 2, 3 and 7. It did not prepare me for sections 8, 11 and 12 (layers, pictures, tasks and "possible", foundations and classes), which the summary covers in one or two dense clauses each.

## Part 5. Did the examples help?

Mostly yes. The examples are the best thing in the document. Starting most sections with a case works well, and the "Back to ..." returns give a sense of payoff.

**Examples that helped most:** the fever and the thermometer; the float that does two jobs; the two thermostats; salt on the step (the case itself); Tomas and the sun god; the two springs; the sowing rule carried south; Greta and Bruno; the two footbridges; the apprentice's three days; the phrasebook; the parrot; the one tooth; the order of adjectives; the two plumbers; the job sheet and "Four seconds"; the kettle; the everyday illustrations of hinges, meshed gears, the lamp wire and Kasia's note.

**Examples that did not help, or confused me:**
- the domino network: it is never said how it works;
- the odd-sized tables: "undone" is never explained;
- the two balances: they need numbers;
- keeping count with string: the set-up is strange, and the theory does not reach the verdict;
- the table with empty columns: its point is unclear;
- Demeter and the veil of dust;
- the hidden thing: I could not picture it;
- two sealed sorts of matter: exotic, and Asha's claim is restated;
- the forbidden wire: given without its setting;
- the robot and expert cases, which blur together.

**Two patterns hurt the examples.**
- Many end "not yet ruled", or show that the theory does not reach the verdict (the string, the farmer, Bruno's second half, Tomas). A newcomer then cannot tell whether the example shows the theory working or failing. One plain closing line per case ("so the theory gives the careful person's verdict", or "so the theory does not yet reach it") would fix this.
- Some general statements still come before, or without, any example: status and purpose questions, selected ranges, hidden slots, renaming changes, two-way and one-way matching, spread-out support, the proof on same anchors, re-tuning, barriers, the classes, and staying able.
