## 2. One example

The sentence that says what an explanatory aim asks for, as the theory stood:

> An explanatory aim requires an account, or the correction of a use through one, to be deployable.

Both readers, separately, found the same fault. By its grammar, "to be deployable" belongs to both halves, so the sentence asks a correction to be deployable: something a person has and can bring out and use for a task. But in the theory only a piece of content, such as an account, can be had and put to use that way. A correction is a change made over time, not a thing one has.

The new wording moves one word:

> An explanatory aim requires a deployable account, or the correction of a use through one.

Nothing else moves. The word "one" now points back to "a deployable account", so the second kind of aim, mending a use through an account, still needs an account the person can use.

## 3. The other three changes

- **Cause and measurement.** The theory tells a cause from a measurement by how each responds to changes. The sentence on causes said a cause's pattern changes when its output is set, "and under replacement of the component". That second half did no work: in the theory's own terms, setting a part's output already is a replacement of that part. It was taken out. A cause and a measurement are still told apart exactly as before.
- **A name no one defined.** A sentence in the mathematics of keeping a capability over time used a name for a kind of set that the theory never defines, and that mathematicians use in two opposite senses. Read one way, the claim goes through; read the other way, it does not. The sentence now spells out which sets it means.
- **The summary of an account.** Near the end, the theory lists what each of its ideas is built from. For an account it named only the matching of the thing explained, piece by piece and in its answers. It left out the other two requirements: not assuming the answer, and not dodging the question. Those two are what keep an account apart from a mere restatement of the answer, and the theory elsewhere speaks of "the four conditions". The summary now names all of them.
