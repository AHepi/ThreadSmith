## 1. What was found, and what it means for your question

You asked, in effect: constructor theory already defines knowledge, so how do we tell when the vague, shapeless thing inside a creative agent counts as knowledge? Three findings bear on that.

**First: in constructor theory, knowledge and error correction are tied at the root.** Constructor theory is the physics in Marletto's book: it describes the world by what changes can and cannot be made to happen. The book says knowledge is information that can keep itself in existence. It also says that the laws of physics preserve nothing complicated for free: anything complex wears away unless something keeps catching and fixing its errors. Put the two together and knowledge is information that gets its own errors fixed, either directly or through what it builds. A bacterium's DNA is the book's example: the cell that repairs each new copy of the DNA is itself built from that DNA.

**Second: the book gives a definition and one test, but no way to apply them to a thought.** The test: knowledge is what you would have to wipe out, everywhere it has been copied, to stop something from being done reliably. The book applies it to a cricket's genes and a factory's plans, never to an idea in a head. And the book says outright that how knowledge is created by thinking is still an open problem, and asks, without answering, “What is it, exactly, that we have to store and error-correct?” (Marletto, ch. 7)

**Third: your earlier frameworks already met the same wall, and took a position on it.** Two of them (FW3 and FW4) said that constructor theory's knowledge and knowledge that explains something are different kinds of thing and do not imply each other. Their examples: a mistaken idea that spreads by being repeated keeps itself in existence but explains nothing; an explanation that was worked out and then lost explained something but kept nothing. The next framework (FW5) objected that the examples mix up an idea, its copies, what can be done now, and what happened once, and put a bridge in its place: an explanation also counts as constructor-theory knowledge when it is kept going *because* of what it says. Then the bridge was dropped when the present theory was first written, and the word "knowledge" itself was removed in the clean-up of words you asked for. The present theory now has neither "information" nor "knowledge".

**What this means for your question.** Constructor theory asks about the future of a piece of information (will it keep itself going?). Your frameworks, and the present theory, ask about the past of a problem (did something improve it?). Those are two different questions, and nothing written so far joins them except the dropped bridge. Checking FW3's two examples against the books shows each one rests on a reading. A mistaken idea that spreads is still knowledge on Deutsch's own account, but knowledge about the minds it lives in, not about what it says. Whether a lost explanation was knowledge depends on whether "able to keep itself in existence" means "in a suitable place" or "did in fact last". Nothing is settled here; those two readings are yours to weigh.

## 2. Error correction, as the book gives it

An example first. A mole cricket keeps its burrow's entrance round and clean. Marletto, as a child, kept stuffing it with grass; each day it was clear again. When the cricket left, her changes, and the rain's, were no longer undone, and the hole faded in a few days.

- **The book never defines error correction.** The phrase appears five times in the whole book. What it gives is the need for it: without something that catches and fixes errors, anything complex falls apart.
- **Error correction is a job something keeps being able to do.** The book calls a thing that does a job and stays able to do it again a *catalyst*: a catalyst is something that makes a change happen and comes out unchanged, ready to do it again. The cricket is one; so is the cell that repairs DNA; so is a factory's repair crew.
- **Only information can be corrected this way.** To put something back, you must tell the damaged state from the right one, and copy the right one in. In the book, information is exactly what can be set to different states and copied. Deutsch adds that you cannot tell an error from a correct value at sight in anything smooth and continuous, which is why computers work in steps.
- **The book uses "error" in three ways.** For a copy, an error is a departure from the original (a monk's slip copying a manuscript). For natural selection, there is no original: an "error" is whatever fails to survive. For a thinker, an error is a flaw in an idea measured against a problem, and the problem can itself change. Only the third is what your theory calls criticism.
- **Knowledge can arise with no designer because error correction can.** Copying slips supply variety for free; the world weeds out what fails; and what survives builds its own repair. The book adds that thinking can make leaps that natural selection cannot.
- **On creative agents** the book says little: creativity is the ability to create new knowledge; human brains have it; how they do it, and whether a computer could, are open.

The idea that "a signal, a failure or a mismatch is not a criticism: a criticism says what is wrong and why" appears in the oldest framework and in every one after it, down to the present theory. The idea that "how many things an explanation reaches is a count you can go by" appears in one framework (FW3) and is gone by the next but one.

- **Size.** 172 boxes: from the oldest framework (FW0) 20, the software specification (I2) 14, FW2 32, FW3 23, FW4 20, FW5 30, the present theory 33. 132 arrows: 54 changed, 44 kept, 17 added, 11 dropped, 6 replaced.
- **How sure.** 45 arrows are stated by your frameworks themselves (their change lists or text), 16 by this project's own records, and **71 are Claude's reading**. Every arrow that crosses a missing document is Claude's reading.
- **Where the chain breaks.** Between the oldest framework and FW2 lie four documents that were not supplied. FW4 says itself that its link to FW3 is only "related". Between FW5 and the present theory lie four more missing documents, and then this project's own files.
- **What came through every version.** Newness is judged against what the thinker could already do. Credit for an idea needs an account of how it was built. A criticism says what is wrong and why. Predicting is not explaining. An idea is not the same as the ink or brain state that holds it. Creating something is not undone by losing it. No score, ranking or probability is put on explanations. Nothing is ever beyond criticism.
- **What was tried and dropped.** A single basic "because" that everything else was built on (FW3; replaced in FW5). A way of preferring one explanation to another, and a count to do it with (FW3 and FW4; dropped in FW5). "Every learning of an idea is a creation of it" (FW3 and FW4; dropped in FW5). "Two layers of error correction", one fixing damaged copies and one fixing mistaken ideas (FW2; softened in FW5, not in the present theory).
- **Where information theory went.** Shannon's information theory, the mathematics of signals and noise, never entered any of them: none mentions it, and neither does Marletto's book. What did enter: a four-valued way of recording evidence (the oldest framework; gone by now); constructor theory's information (FW2 and FW5; the word dropped when the present theory was first written, but its machinery of possible and impossible jobs kept); constructor theory's knowledge (from the oldest framework to FW5; dropped); accounts of the mind as a prediction machine, used only as contrasts (FW2); and two ideas turned down outright, one that treats information as mere correlation and one that measures learning by expected information gain.

## 4. Ways of telling when something is knowledge

The sources offer eleven ways. Grouped, they come to four, and each starts from a different question. They are not ranked.

1. **Does it keep itself going?** *Example:* a grandmother's bread recipe, copied by every grandchild who liked the bread, still baked seventy years later. This is constructor theory's definition, its "what would you have to wipe out" test, and its account of how a knot taught at a sailing club passes from hand to hand.
2. **Did it come from correcting errors?** *Example:* two people give the same train time; one checked the timetable against yesterday's delays, the other guessed. This is Deutsch's addition: knowledge hardly ever arises except by evolution or by thought, both of which correct errors.
3. **Did it improve a problem?** *Example:* a choir director hears the altos come in late, finds the conductor's gesture there is unclear, changes it, and the problem goes away without breaking anything else. This is what all six frameworks and the present theory ask, each in its own terms.
4. **Does it track something, with a history of how it came to?** *Example:* a frog snaps at small dark moving dots and catches flies, though nobody taught it. This is the present theory's notion of one thing standing for another; the theory never calls it knowledge, and it is set here beside the first question only because both need no one who knows.

**One step comes before all of them**: saying which thing, held in what, looked at how closely. *Example:* before asking whether a cook's "feel for when a soufflé is ready" is knowledge, you must say where the feel lives (her eyes, her timing, a tap on the dish) and what it is about (this oven or any oven). Every source says this must be done; none says how to do it for a mind.
