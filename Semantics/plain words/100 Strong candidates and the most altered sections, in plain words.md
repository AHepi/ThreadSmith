# Strong candidates and the most altered sections. In plain words

*A note first. Written on 26 September 2026 by Claude, for you, after your "Yup do that" and your request for the most altered sections. One agent did it, by program, from the record of every edit and proposal. It uses none of the words you asked to be removed, except inside a sentence quoted as it stood. The full analysis is "Semantics/results/S100 Strong candidates and the most altered sections.md", with a table of every sentence beside it.*

---

## 1. What was found

**36 sentences are strong candidates.** Each has stood in all ten versions of the theory, from the first standalone text to today's, with no change in what it says, while the sentences around it changed more than those around three sentences in four. Word swaps from your word-removal pass are allowed, and shown.

- **7 were challenged and kept:** someone proposed a change to the sentence, and it was turned down or never made.
- **29 were never challenged:** no proposal ever touched the sentence itself. That may only mean no one examined it.

**Most of the 29 sit in three places:** how the theory tells kinds of parts apart by how they respond to changes; what a prediction and a violation are; and how a question can itself be in error. By the theory's own order of what depends on what, these come early: they rest on an organization, a question and the range of changes a question allows, and on little else.

**The most altered sections are, above all, the theory's own list of what would rule it out.** Five of the ten most altered sections are items of that list, and two more are about what the theory takes from outside itself and what it defines. Word swaps aside, your own passes made at most one change in six in any of the ten; the rest came from readers, rulings and fixes for faults.

**On values, the record shows one thing plainly.** From the first standalone version on, the theory has not defined value. It takes value from outside, as an input, wherever a question calls for one, and marks the place. That arrangement has never changed; what changed, many times, is its name and the sentences around it. So, on whether values should be part of the theory or separate: in the record they have always been separate, and several of the steadiest sentences, those defining a repair, make no appeal to value. The record shows how the wording moved, not which arrangement you want.

## 2. Two examples

**A strong candidate, challenged and kept.** Where the theory says what a repair aims at, one sentence reads:

> Losses outside \(P\) must be exposed.

It has read so in every version, while the five sentences around it changed almost twice each on average. One reader of your statement on choosing proposed "A repair claim exposes its losses outside \(P\)." instead. The proposal was dropped, and the sentence stands as it was.

**A much-altered sentence.** The first item of the theory's list of what would rule it out has held five wordings:

1. First version: "**(A) Sufficiency.** Produce a candidate that satisfies all four conditions of Account (Part V) on a physically admitted contract, with a non-declared transport, and that plainly explains nothing."
2. From the first revision on: "**(A) Sufficiency.** A candidate meeting all four conditions of (E) on a physically admitted contract, with a non-declared transport, that plainly provides no account."
3. After your word-removal pass: "**(A) Sufficiency.** A candidate meeting all four conditions of (E) on a physically admitted contract, with a non-declared transport, that nonetheless explains nothing."
4. After the repair you asked for on physical possibility: "**(A) Sufficiency.** A candidate meeting all four conditions of (E) on a contract of its question, with a non-declared transport, that an argument not using (E) rules out as an explanation of what its question asks."
5. Today: "**(Suff) Sufficiency.** A candidate meeting all four conditions of (E) on a contract of its question, with a transport whose provenance is not declared (Part IV), such that an argument not using (E) rules out the claim that it is an explanation of what its question asks."

Its ending went back and forth: "explains nothing", then "provides no account", then "explains nothing" again, then two versions of "an argument rules it out". It nearly doubled in length.

## 3. How the measures were made

- **The sentence is the thing counted,** as in the record: a formula or a list item counts as one sentence. Headings and the dated note on how the text was made were left out of the lists.
- **How long a sentence has stood:** a program followed each sentence back through the ten versions, finding the same sentence in any wording.
- **Three kinds of change:** word swaps (your word-removal pass's replacements of single words, and names changed across the whole text); your own passes (the rest of the word-removal pass, the repair on physical possibility, conflict and premises, and the points of your answers of 26 September); and everything else, from readers, rulings and fixes for faults. A change seen in several places counts once. A proposal is a change suggested and not made.
- **Neighbours:** the other sentences of its section and the two on either side.
- **The lines drawn:** a strong candidate has stood in all ten versions, has no change except word swaps, and has neighbours that changed at least 1.21 times each on average; only a quarter of all sentences have neighbours that changed that much. The numbers fall into no natural groups, so the line is simply stated.

## 5. What the patterns suggest

These are patterns only, not reasons.

- **Instruction, then description, then argument.** Items of the list of what would rule the theory out began as instructions to the reader ("Produce a case", "Show that"), became descriptions of a case ("A case where"), and then became "an argument that rules out the claim that", each step a separate rewording.
- **Lists that grew at every round.** The sentences on what the theory takes as given grew each time; one went from 57 words to 111.
- **Names that changed while the place stood.** "Normative relation" became "appraisal relation", "worth" became "an appraisal", "primitive" became "import", and the labels of the items of the list of what would rule the theory out were renamed, some twice. What each name stood for stayed where it was.
- **Few changes that did not stick.** Only one whole sentence went back to an earlier wording: a clause added to the worked example of two layers in the first revision was taken out in the next draft. 34 shorter phrases were added and later taken out, or taken out and later put back, such as "explains nothing" above.

## 6. What is unsure

- **Untouched may mean unexamined.** The 29 never-challenged sentences may never have been read closely.
- **The counts follow how the record placed things.** Some changes stand on a neighbouring sentence, and a change that names a whole paragraph counts on each of its sentences.
- **Your own passes add to the counts by design.** They are counted apart, but the line for "neighbours changed a lot" includes them.
- **The sorting into three kinds follows set definitions,** not a reading of each change.
