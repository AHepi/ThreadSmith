(1) The parts doing the work

- A. There is one shared store of self-control, used by every kind of restraint.
- B. Each act of restraint draws it down, so less is left for the next task.
- C. Rest refills it.
- D. The store is sugar: a sugar drink refills it, a sweetener does not.
- E. Because the store is shared, the domain of the first task should not matter.

(2) What holds each in place, or what could replace it

- A. Held up by the crossover results (food then puzzle, emotion then handgrip); but a simple shift in mood or motivation after a dull, effortful task ("I have done my share") would spread across domains just as well, with no store at all.
- B. Held up by the shorter second task; but paying people a little for the second task, or telling them willpower is not limited, makes the drop vanish, which an empty tank should not allow. Choosing to save effort fits the same facts.
- C. Held up by recovery after a break; but a break also lifts boredom and resets attention, so rest does not single out a store.
- D. Held up by sugar beating sweetener; but the brain's sugar use barely moves during hard thinking, and swishing sugar in the mouth and spitting it out works about as well as drinking it, so a taste signal for reward can take its place.
- E. This is A restated; the mood-or-motivation rival also predicts domain will not matter, so the prediction cannot tell them apart.

(3) The weakest part

D, the sugar store. It is the most specific claim and the easiest to break. Change the situation: three groups after the first task, one swallows a sugar drink, one swishes it and spits, one gets sweetener. If swishing restores the second task as well as swallowing does, no sugar reached anything, and the store is not sugar. If only swallowing works, D earns its place.

Beyond that, the basic drop itself has been hard to find when many labs ran the same cookies-then-puzzle setup with large groups; the effect came out near zero.

(4) Verdict

SET IT ASIDE. The crossover pattern is real in some hands, but every piece of the shared-store account has a rival that fits the same facts, the sugar piece has been directly contradicted, and the core effect does not reliably reappear. Nothing here needs a store to explain it.
