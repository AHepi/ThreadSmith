**(1) The parts doing the work**

- A. Mood rides on serotonin signalling.
- B. In depression that signal is too weak (too little released, or cleared too fast).
- C. Blocking the clearing raises serotonin, the signal strengthens, mood lifts.
- D. The weeks-long delay is the receptors adjusting.
- E. Taking away serotonin's raw material lowers mood in recovered people.
- F. The drug "does nothing" without the block; other transmitters matter less.
- G. Therefore depression is a serotonin shortage and the drug tops it up.

**(2) What holds each in place, or what could stand in for it**

- A. Held up only by the drugs helping; a chemical that lifts mood need not be the thing mood "rides on" (aspirin eases headaches, but headaches are not aspirin shortages).
- B. Nothing direct holds this: measurements of serotonin, its breakdown products and its receptors in depressed people have not shown a consistent shortfall.
- C. The first half is solid (serotonin in the gap rises within hours); the "signal strengthens, so mood lifts" half is assumed, since the rise comes in hours and any mood change in weeks.
- D. A patch added after the fact; new connections growing, slow changes in how people take in bad news, or simply time passing would explain the delay just as well.
- E. Real, but it shows the drug's effect needs serotonin, not that depression does; the same trick barely dents mood in healthy people who have never been depressed.
- F. Shaky: the drug's edge over a dummy pill is small, and drugs that act on noradrenaline, or on neither (ketamine, bupropion), lift mood about as well.
- G. Rests entirely on B and F.

**(3) The weakest part and a test**

B, the shortage itself. Change the situation: lower serotonin in people who have never been depressed and see whether they become depressed. If a shortage causes depression, they should; when this has been tried, they mostly don't. A second check: a drug that speeds up clearing (tianeptine) should worsen mood on this story; it doesn't.

**(4) Verdict**

RELY ON IT FOR A NARROWER QUESTION: "In people who have got better on these drugs, does the benefit depend on serotonin being available?" Yes. Set aside the wider claim that depression is a serotonin shortage which the drug corrects.
