**(1) The parts claimed to do the work**

- A. The price already contains everything that is known.
- B. Anyone with an edge trades on it, and the trading erases the edge quickly.
- C. So a manager's good year is luck, and next year is a coin flip.
- D. Prices jump on news at once, not over days.
- E. A cheap index fund beats most active funds after costs.
- F. Without informed traders, prices would drift from what businesses are worth; with them, the drift closes.

**(2) What holds each in place, or what could stand in for it**

- A. Held in place by B, and only by B; on its own it is a definition, not a finding.
- B. Held in place only if trading is cheap and easy, which fails when shorting is costly, borrowing is limited, or the edge takes years to pay off.
- C. Could just as well come from managers being skilled but fees eating the skill, or from too few years of data to tell skill from noise.
- D. Could come from fast trading rather than fully correct prices; markets also overshoot and later reverse, which is not the same as "at once."
- E. Follows from arithmetic, not from A: the average active dollar earns the market before fees, so after fees most must trail, even if prices are wrong.
- F. Held by nothing observable; it is a story about a world nobody has seen.

**(3) The weakest part**

B, because the whole chain hangs on it and it is doing far more than the everyday version can carry. "Trading erases the edge" only works if trading against the mispricing is safe and cheap. When it is not, a known mispricing can sit there for a long time.

One change that would test it: raise the cost of betting against a stock (harder to borrow shares, or ban short selling for a period) while leaving everything else alone. If B does the work, known bad news should still hit the price at once. If instead the price lags for days or weeks until sellers show up, then the edge was not being erased by trading, and "the price holds everything known" is a slogan, not a mechanism.

**(4) Verdict**

RELY ON IT FOR A NARROWER QUESTION: it is a good reason to expect that most active funds will trail a cheap index fund after costs, and that last year's winners are a poor guide. It is not a good reason to believe prices are always right, or that mispricings cannot persist when correcting them is costly.
