**(1) The parts claimed to do the work**

- A. The wave equation is the whole story; nothing gets added to it.
- B. Measuring spreads the "two at once" from the particle to the apparatus and to you, so you end up in two branches.
- C. The branches stop interfering with each other because they tangle with the surroundings very quickly.
- D. You see one result because "you" is one branch, and the other branch holds another you.
- E. It matches every interference experiment, including cases where branches are brought back together and interference returns.
- F. The rival view has to add a collapse rule with a threshold nobody has measured.

**(2) What holds each part in place, or what could replace it**

- A. Held in place by the fact that no experiment has ever needed an extra term; but a collapse-with-tiny-threshold story fits the same data, so the data alone doesn't pick A.
- B. Held by plain math: apply the equation to particle-plus-apparatus and you get a combined state, and this is confirmed in small lab systems.
- C. Held by decoherence, which is measured and matches theory; it explains why branches don't interfere, though a collapse view uses the same decoherence.
- D. This is a reading of the math, not a measured fact; "the combined state describes probabilities of one outcome" would explain your single observation just as well.
- E. Held by real experiments on small systems; but every rival that agrees with the equation at small scales predicts the same thing, so it doesn't separate the views.
- F. Held by the current absence of any observed collapse; it is a fair point against the rival but not evidence for extra branches.

**(3) The weakest part and a test**

D is weakest. Saying "the other branch holds another you" explains why you see one outcome only if you already accept that unobserved branches are real, and nothing in the argument makes the branch you didn't see do any work. Change the situation: interfere a system big enough (a large molecule, a mechanical resonator) at a scale where proposed collapse rules say interference should fail. If interference keeps returning as size grows, F bites and D gains ground. If it stops at some size, the other branch was never there.

**(4) Verdict**

RELY ON IT FOR A NARROWER QUESTION: it is a sound account of why measurements look final and why interference vanishes and returns in small systems. It does not settle whether the unseen branch exists.
