## Report: *Nix v. Hedden* (the tomato tariff case)

**1. The question, frozen.** The opinion says its title sentence is "the single question": within the tariff act of 1883, are tomatoes, *considered as provisions*, to be classed as **vegetables** or as **fruit**? Kind of question: **what counts as what under a rule**. Range: that statute, as read in 1883 usage. The opinion also answers two neighbours — whether the botanical sense governs, and how a court may know the ordinary meaning — and I treat those as separate claims, not as the frozen question.

**2. The explanation in parts** (the court's words):

- **A.** "They must receive their ordinary meaning," not the botanical one.
- **B.** Ordinary meaning is judicially noticed; dictionaries are "aids to the memory," not evidence; witnesses said the words had no special trade meaning.
- **C.** The working test: in "the common language of the people," vegetables are "grown in kitchen gardens" and "usually served at dinner in, with, or after the soup, fish, or meats… and not, like fruits generally, as dessert."
- **D.** The companion list: cucumbers, squashes, beans, peas are botanically fruit but commonly vegetables; so are potatoes, carrots, cabbage, lettuce.
- **E.** Precedent: *Robertson v. Salomon* (beans are not seeds).

**3. Part by part.**

| Part | Mark | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A (ordinary meaning governs) | **Borrowed** | Rests on the statute-reading and evidence law in *Brown v. Piper*; not tested here. |
| B (judicial notice; dictionaries as aids) | **Borrowed** | Its job is real — how the court may know the meaning when witnesses gave none — but it rests on another explanation (evidence law) that is not on the table. |
| C (kitchen garden + meal position) | **Held if** | Remove it and the answer has nothing behind it but the phrase "common speech." But it is held by a job the court never tested: that ordinary speakers really sort food this way. Ask sellers and consumers what the witnesses were not asked. |
| D (companion list) | **Held if** | Does the job of keeping cucumbers and beans in vegetables. It rests on *Robertson*, not on a test run here. |
| E (*Robertson* precedent) | **Borrowed** | Remove it and the tomato holding stays; it holds the reading to an earlier case whose own reasoning isn't tested here. |

**4. Whole-explanation checks.**

- **Remove C:** the conclusion collapses into assertion. **Remove A:** nothing left at all. **Remove B:** the outcome survives, but the court loses its licence to decide without evidence — a different job, honestly done. So A and C are load-bearing; B and E are not.
- **Swap, clue by clue.** Replace "served at dinner, not as dessert" with "eaten raw or cooked": lettuce and cucumber stay, but apples walk in. Breaks. Replace "grown in kitchen gardens" with "grown on vines": tomato, cucumber, squash, bean, pea are all vine-grown — the class swallows the botanical reading it just rejected. Breaks. Each clue fails alone and both are needed: a group, not a heap. **Swap the whole test** for "the botanically seed-bearing part": that is the rival the court names and rejects — good, it names what stops it.
- **Poke.** Should matter: ordinary speakers calling tomatoes fruit, or tomatoes served as dessert. Should not matter: the dictionary's botanical wording, or the same dispute under a later act. Both halves exist, so C is a working part, not a label. Note the court is careful that dictionaries are *readings* of common usage, not the thing read — the reading moves when usage changes; the usage is what decides.
- **Flip.** The rule A is outcome-neutral: if the witnesses had said sellers and consumers call tomatoes fruit, the same rule yields "fruit" with no part changed. So the outcome rides entirely on the one fact the court supplied itself — common usage — entered by judicial notice, on a record where the only witnesses testified about trade, not common speech. If the *people* turn out to call it a fruit, does A flip? The opinion doesn't say. That is the loose joint.
- **Answer in the starting points?** "Ordinary meaning" doesn't contain "vegetable." But the fact that does the work — what the people call it — arrives as the court's own knowledge, i.e. as a conclusion. It is *held if* the judges' sense of a dinner matches the people's.
- **Pairs that pull.** The wider the power of judicial notice, the weaker the claim that this is *the people's* language — the people were never asked. Also: which clue wins when "kitchen garden" and "not dessert" disagree (rhubarb, melon, pumpkin)? The line is never drawn.
- **Patches.** None visible; it is a first-instance judgment affirmed. The concession "botanically speaking, tomatoes are the fruit of a vine" is a limit that only says what is being asked about, so it is free and harmless — not a rescue.

Left out without saying so: foods where the two clues pull apart; whether usage may drift after 1883; how the test fares outside "provisions." "Provisions" is the statute's own bin, so it is **fixed** by the act, not a patch.
- **Rival.** "Not common usage — the court's own table manners." No change on this record separates them, because the only readings of common usage are the judges'. **They are the same explanation at this level.** The finer question is a new one: ask the people.
- **Where the parts came from.** C is **fitted**: it was framed with the very items it classifies in view (tomatoes, cucumbers, beans, lettuce), so fitting them is no evidence. A is **built** — it commits to unmet cases and could be caught out. The claim about what people call a tomato is **asserted**. Dictionaries and *Robertson* are **borrowed**.

**5. What would make it harder to vary.**

1. One poke, written down before looking: ask a sample of sellers *and* consumers, as the trial did not, whether "tomato" is a fruit or a vegetable. Predicting "vegetable" first is what turns judicial notice into a test.
2. Run the two clues separately on foods where they pull apart — rhubarb, melon, pumpkin, an iced tomato dessert — and say which clue wins. If neither does, C is a group of clues with no stated line, and saying so tightens the claim.

**6. What this does not show.** Held parts are not true parts. The classification is a reading of a rule, not a discovery about tomatoes; and "vegetable" is partly convention, which the statute could have fixed by definition. Passing here shows which bolts are tight, not that the verdict was right.

**7. One next step.** Write down, before looking, what the court's own test says about rhubarb and melon; then check it against how they are actually served and sold. Agreement or disagreement both tell you something; the written prediction is what makes it a test.

## Report 033 - source D2

The RFC’s explanation holds up well as a canonical, historically accurate description of TCP Reno’s four algorithms. It is not a complete modern specification, but its core causal story is sound. The parts that do the real work are the ACK-clocking rationale, the cwnd/rwnd distinction, the AIMD split between slow start and congestion avoidance, and the duplicate-ACK trigger for fast retransmit/recovery. The loose parts are mostly units, initial values, loss-detection heuristics, and the assumption of a single loss per window.

**Parts that do the work**

- **Slow start.** The explanation correctly identifies the problem: a sender that blasts a full receiver window into a routed network can overflow intermediate queues. The fix—ACK clocking—is the key insight: inject new packets at the rate acknowledgments return. Adding `cwnd` and sending `min(cwnd, advertised window)` cleanly separates sender-side congestion control from receiver-side flow control. The exponential growth description is essentially right for one ACK per segment.
- **Congestion avoidance.** The document correctly treats packet loss as the congestion signal, keeps `ssthresh`, and switches from exponential to linear growth after congestion. The formula `cwnd += segsize*segsize/cwnd` is the standard per-ACK increase that yields roughly +1 segment per RTT when ACKs are per segment. The distinction between slow start and congestion avoidance is the conceptual backbone.
- **Fast retransmit.** Duplicate ACKs indicate out-of-order delivery; waiting for three is a reasonable heuristic because reordering is usually limited. Retransmitting before the timeout avoids a long stall. This part is practical and still recognizable in modern TCP.
- **Fast recovery.** The reason for not returning to slow start—duplicate ACKs show that packets are still flowing and sitting in the receiver’s buffer—is correct. Halving `cwnd`, inflating by three segments, and then deflating to `ssthresh` on the first new ACK is the classic Reno behavior.

**Loose or incomplete parts**

- **Initial values.** `cwnd = 1` segment and `ssthresh = 65535` bytes are implementation choices, not laws. Modern TCP uses an initial window of 10 MSS (RFC 6928), and the 65535-byte `ssthresh` is obsolete.
- **Units and ACK counting.** Slow start says “incremented by one segment every time an ACK is received.” That ignores delayed ACKs and cumulative ACKs. Modern TCP does `cwnd += min(N, SMSS)` for `N` newly acknowledged bytes. With delayed ACKs, growth is less than pure exponential—the RFC admits this but does not formalize it.
- **Congestion-avoidance formula.** The formula only gives exactly +1 MSS per RTT when ACKs arrive per segment. With delayed ACKs, it can be about +0.5 MSS per RTT. “At most one segment each RTT” is safe but imprecise. It also does not say what to do if an ACK covers multiple segments.
- **Duplicate ACKs as loss proof.** Three duplicate ACKs are a heuristic, not proof. Reordering can trigger false fast retransmits; a single duplicate ACK may also be caused by a lost ACK or network duplication. The RFC does not define precisely what counts as a duplicate ACK (same ACK number, no window update, etc.).
- **Fast recovery assumptions.** Step 1 sets `ssthresh` from `cwnd`, but `cwnd` may exceed the actual flight size, so the halving can be too generous. Modern RFCs use `FlightSize/2`. Step 3 assumes the next new ACK acknowledges the retransmission and all intermediate data, and that only one packet was lost. Multiple losses in the same window produce partial ACKs; Reno can stall or time out. NewReno and SACK address this.
- **Scope and age.** The document calls itself the Internet standard, but it was obsoleted by RFC 2581 and then RFC 5681. Today’s deployed algorithms include CUBIC, BBR, and others. The RFC is a snapshot of Reno, not a complete description of modern congestion control.

**What would test it**

- **Trace-driven simulation.** Run a TCP connection over a bottleneck with variable RTT, bandwidth, and buffer size. Measure `cwnd` over time. The RFC predicts exponential slow start, a linear congestion-avoidance phase, fast retransmit after three duplicate ACKs, and a fast-recovery sawtooth. Deviations would show where the model is incomplete.
- **Delayed ACK experiments.** Force the receiver to ACK every other segment. Measure slow-start growth. The RFC’s “one segment per ACK” predicts slower-than-doubling growth; the exact factor would test whether the ACK-counting rules are stated tightly enough.
- **Multiple-loss tests.** Drop two or more segments in one window. The RFC’s fast recovery assumes a single loss; a test would show partial ACKs, timeouts, and the need for NewReno/SACK.
- **Reordering tests.** Reorder packets so three duplicate ACKs occur without actual loss. If fast retransmit fires, the three-dup-ACK heuristic is shown to be loose.
- **High-BDP tests.** Compare initial-window 1 versus 10. The RFC’s one-segment start would perform poorly on long-fat networks, testing whether that initial value is fundamental or merely historical.
- **Implementation comparison.** Implement RFC 2001 versus RFC 5681 and compare `ssthresh` calculation, initial window, and duplicate-ACK handling. Differences would isolate which parts were essential and which were loose.

In short, the explanation is a good conceptual and operational account of Reno. It does the important work: ACK clocking, cwnd, AIMD, duplicate ACKs, and fast recovery. It is loose on units, initialization, loss detection, and multi-loss behavior, and it is not current. The right way to test it is to compare its predictions against packet-level experiments and modern RFC behavior.

They establish that the problem is common and not merely anecdotal.
- **Meta-epidemiological links to bias.** The claim that inadequate allocation concealment yields larger treatment effects is the explanatory backbone. It converts “poor reporting” from an aesthetic complaint into a threat to validity. The same applies to unblinded outcome assessment, early stopping for benefit, and selective outcome reporting.
- **Protocol–publication discrepancies.** The examples of intention-to-treat mislabelling, unexplained primary outcome changes, and underreported stopping decisions are among the most persuasive parts. They show that reports can be not just incomplete but misleading.
- **Item-by-item rationale and examples.** For each checklist item, the document explains *why* the item matters, gives a concrete example of good reporting, and often cites evidence. This is exactly what an explanation-and-elaboration document should do.
- **Conceptual distinctions.** The separation of allocation concealment from blinding, sequence generation from implementation, and intention-to-treat from per-protocol analysis is clear and useful. These distinctions do much of the pedagogical work.

**Where it is loose**

- **Causal language about CONSORT itself.** The document says CONSORT is “associated with improved quality” and “should be helpful.” That is appropriately modest, but the surrounding narrative sometimes implies that adopting CONSORT will fix reporting. The evidence for that is observational: journals that adopt CONSORT may differ from those that do not, and secular trends, editorial pressure, and author training could confound the association.
- **Reporting vs. conduct.** CONSORT is a reporting guideline, not a methodological quality instrument. The document says this explicitly, but the explanation often slides between “poorly reported” and “poorly done.” Better reporting can reveal poor methods; it does not make them good. A trial can be perfectly CONSORT-compliant and still be biased.
- **Bias direction and magnitude.** The claim that inadequate reporting/design is “associated with biased estimates” is supported, but the direction is not uniform: some biases exaggerate, some attenuate, some are unpredictable. The document tends to emphasise exaggerated treatment effects.
- **Selective evidence.** Many cited reviews come from specific fields, English-language journals, or high-impact journals. The document acknowledges some specialty and language variation, but the evidence base is not fully representative.
- **No direct evidence that the E&E document improves anything.** The explanation and elaboration may help authors and peer reviewers, but there is little or no evidence that reading it changes reporting behaviour, reviewer stringency, or reader comprehension.
- **Contested concepts remain contested.** “Blinding,” “random,” “intention-to-treat,” and “adequate” allocation concealment are clarified, but the document itself notes that “double blind” has 18 different combinations in practice. Definitions are improved, not settled.
- **Normative claims dressed as empirical ones.** Statements like “authors should report” or “it is unethical to expose humans unnecessarily” are reasonable, but they are normative, not empirical. They do not need testing, but they should not be confused with evidence.

**What would test it**

1. **Randomised or controlled evaluations of CONSORT adoption.** Compare journals or author groups randomly assigned to receive CONSORT plus E&E versus usual guidance, then measure item-level reporting completeness. This would test whether the explanation adds value beyond the checklist.
2. **Protocol-linked audits.** For a cohort of trials, compare protocols, publications, and CONSORT adherence. Test whether better CONSORT compliance predicts fewer unexplained outcome changes, fewer selective reports, and better alignment with pre-specified methods.
3. **Meta-epidemiological studies with sharper exposure measures.** Link reported allocation concealment, blinding, and outcome pre-specification to effect estimates, while adjusting for trial size, setting, and intervention type. The existing evidence is suggestive; stronger causal designs would help.
4. **Reader comprehension and decision studies.** Give clinicians, reviewers, and patients trial reports with varying CONSORT compliance. Test whether they can more accurately judge validity, extract outcomes, and make guideline-consistent decisions.
5. **Intervention studies on the E&E document.** Test whether authors who use the E&E produce more complete reports than those who use only the checklist, and whether peer reviewers trained on E&E catch more reporting deficiencies.
6. **Implementation and equity checks.** Test whether CONSORT improves reporting in low-resource settings, non-English journals, and non-pharmacological trials, or whether it mainly benefits well-resourced authors.
7. **Selective outcome reporting experiments.** Use prospective registration and protocol access to measure whether CONSORT 2010’s new items reduce outcome switching and omitted harms.

**Bottom line:** The document is a credible, well-argued rationale for CONSORT 2010. Its empirical audits and meta-epidemiological citations do the heavy lifting. Its causal claims about CONSORT’s effects and its assumption that better reporting straightforwardly improves validity and care are looser. The right test is not whether CONSORT is logically appealing—it is—but whether adopting it changes reporting behaviour, reduces protocol–publication discrepancies, and helps readers judge trials more accurately.
