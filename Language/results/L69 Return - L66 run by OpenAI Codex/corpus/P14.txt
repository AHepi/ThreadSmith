1. Natural selection acts wherever there are things that vary, that pass on what they are, and that leave different numbers of descendants because of it.
2. Groups can be such things.
3. A group whose members help one another at a cost to themselves leaves more descendant groups than a group of members who do not, because the helping group outlasts, outgrows or outfights its rivals.
4. Within any one group, a member who helps does worse than a member who does not, because the helper pays the cost and every member shares the benefit.
5. So selection within groups works against helping, and selection between groups works for it.
6. Which wins depends on how much the groups differ from one another and how much the members differ within them.
7. When groups differ greatly, because helpers cluster together and stay together, selection between groups is the stronger, and helping spreads.
8. This is not a way of speaking; it is a cause: the group structure produces the spread of helping, and remove the structure and the spread stops.
9. Accounting that credits the spread to the individual's relatives is arithmetic done afterwards, and it does not change what produced the outcome.
10. Where cooperation has spread, as in the social insects, the cell, and the human tribe, groups were the things selection was acting on.
