What kind of argument it is

The tomato paragraph argued about what produced something: the cold killed the plants. Yours gives reasons to expect something: he usually does this, and he is that sort of person. After the one patch your paragraph demanded

A second kind of link, SINCE: a reason to expect, tested on whatever follows by any route, and always reported with what it quietly assumes. And a way to name the conclusion: the claimed line that others are reasons for, and that is itself a reason for nothing. All earlier paragraphs were rerun and did not change.

In plain words

  • The conclusion is the prediction: he will smash the cake. The checker worked that out from the structure. It needed to, because three of your four sentences are main-clause claims and the standing slot alone could not say which one is the point.
  • The habit carries it. “That’s usually what he does” plus “a wedding is coming” is enough. It rests on one thing you did not say: that this wedding is not an exception. Bride’s mother watching, a promise made, a cake on a high table: anything like that and the argument lets go, which is exactly how “usually” should behave.
  • “Always crude and impolite” does no work. Take it out and the prediction stands just as firmly. Keep it and drop the habit, and nothing gets you from crude to cake. It would take a line nobody wrote: crude people smash cakes. In hard-to-vary terms it is a label. It may be true and it clearly matters to you, but it holds nothing in place here.
  • One thing the checker cannot see but you might ask. Is “crude” known from anything other than the cake-smashing? If not, the character and the habit are one observation said twice. The ledger cannot tell, because the paragraph does not say where “crude” comes from.

What this means for the language

New claims, dated 20 September 2026. Earlier pages stay as they were.

  • Two kinds of because. BECAUSE for what produced it, SINCE for a reason to expect or believe it. The rulebook had one. When the text does not say which, the translator’s choice is a filled-in line.
  • Habits now do work. The rulebook listed the habit line as legal but unused. It behaves as a “usually” line over occasions, of the SHOWS kind.
  • Standing is not role. CLAIMED against GIVEN separates what a sentence asserts from what it leans on. It does not say which claim is the conclusion and which are its reasons. The links say that.
  • A result can be a change of state. Broken, dead, open, smashed. The rulebook has only moves, stays and touches.
  • Rule 11 (joining the rigs) was barely needed. Your paragraph has a cause in it, but argues nothing about that cause. The join is still unbuilt and untested.

Traps

  • Reading “does no work” as “is false”. The checker says nothing about whether Markus is crude.
  • Forgetting the links are filled in. You never wrote “because”.
  • Choosing BECAUSE where SINCE is meant, or the reverse. It changes which test runs.
  • Treating “to be expected” as a forecast. It means only that the prediction follows from what you said.
