# L81 Calibration - what Arm B measures, in file 11's terms, and when a vague translation is acceptable, second version

Second version, 22 September, after the first was tested by a fresh Opus agent with the hard-to-vary skill ("L81 Review", results/). The first is kept beside this one. What the review found and this version changes: the ledger is a representation claim, file 11's (R), not an Account claim (E); the query is defined on the prose, not on the ledger; the first version misquoted L64's six admitted changes and dropped one silently; the report is a measurement of the ledger, not a transport; the marking rule compared two ledgers and never the prose, so a shared error, such as a hedge sent to the bin, was invisible and could yield a false finding against the writer; and no role in Arm B saw a ledger beside its prose, so the note's own refutation condition could not fire. Each is answered below, in file 11's words where it has them. A draft for the owner to strike.

## 1. The goal, in file 11's terms
The project wants a reader's prose argument checked and its consequences drawn by a computable route (decision L4). In file 11: the prose is the target organisation D; the ledger is an occurrence o that instantiates an organisation; the translator makes a transport t from D to the ledger's organisation. The claim "this ledger is the prose's ledger" is a **representation** claim, (R): there is a transport, faithful on the contract C, whose provenance is selected or constructed (Part IV). Faithful on C means F1 and F2 of Part V hold on C (Part IV, "Transports"). So **F1 and F2 are the translator's conditions**, and the provenance obligation is the translator's too: a language model's transport is *selected*, tuned on the cases it met, and Derivation 3 says it is underdetermined on unseen changes wherever its population admits a differing survivor. Arm B's texts are unseen; Derivation 3 therefore predicts that two translators may differ there, and says such a difference is a fact about the translators' histories, not about the prose.

The question p carries the query Q, which file 11 defines **on the prose**: Ans_p(a,b) = Q(D,a,b) (Part III). The checker computes Ans on the ledger; question fidelity (A) is the equality Ans_E(τ(a),σ(b)) = Ans_p(a,b). Whether that equality holds is not something two ledgers can settle between them; only a reading of the prose beside the ledger can. That is the sentence the first version lacked, and it is now the marking rule's last line.

The contract C is L64's six admitted changes, quoted: take out one line or a group; WITHDRAW a line; MAKE a fact NOT SO; change what fills a slot (response class, direction, strength level); add supposed lines in a named case; open a told world alone. The fourth is rig 2's and Arm B runs rig 1 only: **it is excluded from Arm B's contract by this stated scope**, as Part V's non-vacuity requires, and the cost is named in section 3. The variation test of L64 line 143 (drop a sentence, deny a clause, swap a name) is a set of edits on the prose, carried to the ledger by τ; it is how F1 and F2 are tested, not a seventh change.

The checker's report is not a transport. In Part II's terms it has a **measurement's signature**: change only the reading and the ledger stays as it was; change the ledger and the reading follows. The read-back test asks an identification question about the reader (Part III, "the respect is the query"): given the report, can the reader identify the finding and its sentence. It is not a production question, and passing it does not show the report caused anything.

## 3. When a vaguely incorrect translation is acceptable
File 11 Part II: a kind is an edit-signature; two components no admitted change separates are one kind at that grain. Derivation 2 runs one way only: F1, F2 and (A) on C give one account; equal outputs do not give F1 and F2 (Derivation 9). So the rule cannot be "same outputs, therefore acceptable". It is this:

**Acceptable, and expected.** A translation that differs from another in wording, in the form of a line, in standing where the text does not settle it, or in extra Thing lines, and that a reader who sees the prose beside it finds to commit the writer to what the prose commits them to, is variation, not error. The checker's grain absorbs the wording.

**Acceptable, and desirable.** Where the prose is vague or hedged, the translation must be as vague. "The lamp drew the visitor back" stays a drawing, not a pull; under Arm B's contract, which excludes the slot change, drawing and pulling are one kind at this grain, and that is a stated cost of the exclusion, not a licence to sharpen. A hedge ("may have been left open") is the harder case: L64 sends "may" to the bin, and 39 and L80 say nothing about what survives. A translation that writes the hedged clause as a fact is wrong about the prose even though the hedge is marked in the bin: the writer committed to a possibility and the ledger commits them to a fact, and the checker will then report a bare cause against a writer who never claimed one. This is a false finding produced by the language's silence on modality, and it is the case the owner's question is about. Arm B carries a hedged pair to see it happen or not.

**Not acceptable.** Four cases, each with its home in file 11 or in 39:
1. **F2, and F1 where it can be seen.** An admitted change to the prose changes the prose's commitments and not the ledger's, or the reverse: drop "because the window was opened" and the ledger keeps a cause; deny "the letter stayed dry" and the ledger's dryness survives; reword a sentence that carries no argument and the ledger moves. Tested by the variation test and the near-neighbour rewordings.
2. **A claim the question asks about is put where no query reaches it.** T10-D's cause claim inside a told world under the old driver. This charges the language and the driver (L72 finding 1), not the translator who followed 39; Arm A moved the driver, and Arm B asks whether anything is still out of reach.
3. **Non-circular dependence, both halves.** The answer must follow from independent boundary conditions: an added line whose content is the conclusion (patch 10, "added lines alone give the conclusion"; 38 line 113's note); and there must exist an admitted change under which the answer changes, so a ledger whose findings survive every admitted change has no dependence to report.
4. **A sentence reaches neither a line nor the bin.** This is 39's final check 4, not a condition of (E), and the new gauge counts it.

**The marking rule for Arm B.** A difference between two translators, or between a translation and the prose, counts as an error only under one of the four, or on a prose reader's verdict that the ledger commits the writer to what the prose does not (a hedge made a fact; a claim the reported person never made). Everything else is variation, recorded in the sameness diagnostic and not scored. A shared error of the two translators shows only in the prose reader's verdict, never in the comparison between them; that is why the role exists.

## 4. What changes in Arm B's design because of this
- A **prose reader** (Mimo, a fresh call per report, sees the passage and the new report, never a ledger) answers, per finding: does the passage's reported person claim this as a fact, hedge it, or not make it? And: is anything the passage's reported person argued missing from the report?
- The corpus gains a **hedged pair** (may have / had), a **narrator's-own-argument** passage (so the variation test and the world placement are not entangled on every text), and **near-neighbour rewordings** of three F-sides (the change that should not matter beside the one that should).
- Known exposure: the encoding guide's first example is Astra Ultra's L72 ledger of T10-D, a told-world cause claim, which is the structure Arm B's first expectation tests. The translators are taught the encoding; Arm B tests whether they apply it to new reports, not whether they discover it. Said here so the marks are read with it.

## 5. What would show this calibration wrong
A text on which two ledgers give the same findings under all five changes in force and the prose reader says one is wrong about the prose: then the grain is too coarse and C is short a change, and the fix is a new claim at a new index (Part III), not an edit to this note. The hedged pair is built to reach this. Or a text where the variation test passes on both ledgers and the reports still disagree in kind: then the report, not the translation, is the variable, and the driver is charged.

## Traps
- Reading "vagueness is acceptable" as "anything goes". The four cases and the prose reader's verdict are the whole rule.
- Marking Arm B by sameness_2's counts. Diagnostic only.
- Reading equal outputs from two translators as fidelity. Derivation 9.
