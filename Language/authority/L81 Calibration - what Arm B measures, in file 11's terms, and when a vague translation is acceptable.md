# L81 Calibration - what Arm B measures, in file 11's terms, and when a vague translation is acceptable

Written 22 September under decision L11, before Arm B's plan, to answer the owner's standing question: are the goals and targets calibrated to Semantics revision 1 (file 11), and to the goal, which is reasoning with prose? And: when is a vaguely incorrect translation acceptable? A draft for the owner to strike; tested by an Opus agent with the hard-to-vary skill before Arm B's plan leans on it.

## 1. The goal, in file 11's terms
The project wants a reader's prose argument checked and its consequences drawn by a computable route (decision L4). In file 11: the prose is the target organisation D; the ledger is a second organisation E; the translator is a transport t from D to E; the checker's questions are queries Q on E; the contract C is L64's six admitted changes (take out a line; deny a line; withdraw; make not so; open a told world alone; a named case). The claim "this ledger is the prose's ledger" is an Account claim (file 11 Part V, (E)): component fidelity F1 and assembled fidelity F2 on C, question fidelity (A), non-circular dependence, non-vacuity. A finding is an answer of Q; a consequence is an answer profile under an admitted change.

Two things follow that the record has half-said. First, **the transport is the translator's, and F1/F2 are the translator's conditions**: a ledger is faithful when, for every admitted change to the prose, the ledger changes as the prose's commitments do and nowhere else (L64 line 143, the variation test, never yet run). Second, **the checker's report is a second transport**, from E to the reader's understanding, and the read-back test is its fidelity condition (L64 section 6, fourth row). Arm A tested the second (the report says what the rig computed). Arm B tests the first.

## 3. When a vaguely incorrect translation is acceptable
File 11 Part II: a kind is an edit-signature; two components no admitted change separates are one kind at that grain. Derivation 2: indistinguishable is identical. So the rule is not about wording at all:

**Acceptable, and expected.** A translation that differs from another, or from the "right" one, in wording, in the form of a line ("Ada folded the note" against "folding: ada folded note"), in standing where the text does not settle it, or in extra Thing lines, but that yields the same answer under every admitted change, is the same ledger at this grain. The checker's grain absorbs the vagueness. Marking such differences as errors would be marking the wrong thing (L64 section 8's fifth condition, as review A1 read it).

**Acceptable, and desirable.** Where the prose is vague, the translation must be as vague: "the lamp drew the visitor back" stays a drawing, not a pull (Astra Ultra on T11-D, L72). A translation that sharpens adds a commitment the writer did not make; that is an unmarked addition, and it is the error, not the vagueness. The translator's one licence to add is the marked line (filled in, usual case), and a finding that leans on it is reported as conditional (38 line 113).

**Not acceptable.** Four cases, each a named condition of (E):
1. An admitted change to the prose would change the prose's commitments and not the ledger's, or the reverse (F1/F2): drop the clause "because the window was opened" and the ledger keeps a cause; or deny "the letter stayed dry" and the ledger's dryness survives. This is what the variation test catches and nothing else does (review A3).
2. A claim the question asks about is put where no query reaches it (question fidelity): T10-D's cause claim inside a told world under the old driver. Arm A fixed the driver's half; Arm B asks whether 39's rule still hides anything.
3. An added line supplies the answer (non-circularity): patch 10's "added lines alone give the conclusion", and any filled-in line whose content is the conclusion.
4. A sentence reaches neither a line nor the bin (non-vacuity of the record): my T10-B. The new gauge counts it.

**So the marking rule for Arm B**: a difference between two translators, or between a translation and the text, counts as an error only under one of the four; everything else is variation, recorded in the sameness diagnostic and not scored. A translator that refuses to repair a broken sentence (L66's four refusals) is right under this rule and the plants that rely on repair are not tests of the checker.

## 4. Where the goals were not calibrated, and what changes
- L66's plants asked the translator to carry an error into the ledger so the checker could catch it; under the rule above, a faithful translator will not, and did not. Arm B's texts are therefore not planted with slips: each pair differs in a real commitment (a connective present or absent; a clause denied), and the test is whether both ledgers move with it.
- L64's bin threshold counted fragments; the condition that matters is 4 above (a sentence lost) and 1 (a load-bearing clause binned). Arm B counts those, not fragments.
- The "same said-content" condition of L64 section 8 (fifth) is restated: two ledgers of one text that differ in **answer profile** on C, both translators citing 38 and 39, show the language wrong; differing in wording shows nothing.
- Reasoning with prose is the goal, so Arm B's texts are prose arguments a reader would actually want checked (a report of an argument with a cause claim; a report with an inference), not fixture sentences.

## 5. What would show this calibration wrong
A text on which two ledgers give the same answer profile under all six changes and a competent reader says one is wrong about the prose: then the grain is too coarse and C needs a seventh change. Or a text where the variation test passes on both ledgers and the checker's reports still disagree in kind: then the report, not the translation, is the variable, and Arm B's second question charges the driver.

## Traps
- Reading "vagueness is acceptable" as "anything goes". The four cases are exact and each is a condition of (E).
- Marking Arm B by sameness_2's counts. They are diagnostic.
