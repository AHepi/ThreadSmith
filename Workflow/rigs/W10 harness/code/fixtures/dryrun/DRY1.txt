Mallow and Renton disagreed about the crossing, and the disagreement is worth setting out
because each of them had a reason and the reasons do not fit together.

Mallow held that the crossing closed because the silt built up faster than the winter current
could carry it away. On her account the north bank is the shallow one, so the silt settles there
first; the winter flow is the lowest of the year, so the current is weakest exactly when the
silt is heaviest; and the dredger, which had kept the channel open every spring since 1880, was
withdrawn in 1913 and never replaced. Take away any one of the three, Mallow said, and the
crossing stays open: raise the winter flow and the silt goes downstream; deepen the north bank
and it settles elsewhere; keep the dredger and it is lifted out each spring.

Renton held that the crossing closed because the traffic had already gone. On his account the
railway bridge opened in 1911, the carters stopped paying the toll within two years, and the
trust that owned the crossing stopped paying for the dredger because there was nothing left to
dredge for. The silt, on Renton's account, is not the cause but the mark: it is what a channel
looks like when nobody has a reason to keep it open.

The two accounts agree on everything that was written down at the time. Both explain the closure.
Both explain why it happened in winter. Both explain why the north side went first. Renton points
out that Mallow's account cannot say why the dredger was withdrawn, and treats 1913 as a date
that fell out of the sky. Mallow points out that Renton's account cannot say why the crossing
was still passable in 1912, a full year after the bridge opened, and that a channel with no
traffic does not silt any faster than a channel with traffic.

What would tell them apart is a crossing somewhere else where the traffic went and the dredging
continued. The register does not record one, and neither of them went looking.
